# Body copy for an animated explainer

The hook earns the first 30 seconds; the body has to earn the next twelve minutes. Bencivenga's
rules for the body of an ad translate directly: every paragraph gives a reason why, joins claim to
proof, stays interesting by staying unpredictable, and uses one metaphor that does the explaining.
Labels as in `principles.md`. Wording rules (banned words, rhythm patterns, TTS numbers) stay in
`docs/library/narration-style.md`; this file is about *what each line has to do*.

---

## 1. Explaining a mechanism, step by step

Bencivenga called himself a "reason-why" writer and put the reason why at the centre of
persuasion (`sources.md` S16 B9) [primary]. In an explainer, the mechanism *is* the reason why,
and the replay data says it's what viewers rewind (`video-autopsies/README.md` §B4: a mechanism
made simple is the commonest replayed peak) [observed].

The recipe [house]:
1. **State the outcome first, plainly.** "A fridge doesn't make cold."
2. **Then the steps, one per line, one picture per line.** Each step is a cause the viewer can
   see, never more than one new idea per line. 3–5 steps; if it needs more, split it into two
   mechanisms with a turn between them.
3. **Every step carries its "because"** (S16 B10) [primary]: "The gas expands, *so* it gets cold."
4. **Show the step before you name it.** Picture first, then the word ("this is called…"). Jargon
   comes after the viewer already understands it, if at all.
5. **Close with the consequence the viewer can check**: a number, a sum, a demonstration ("put
   your hand behind the fridge").
6. **Then the twist**: the step that the simple version left out, which opens the next loop.

## 2. Plain statement, then twist

The rhythm of Bencivenga's own bullets in the 1994 job-interview ad, as analysed in S10: a flat,
believable statement, then the turn that makes it interesting [secondary]. It's his I = B + C in
two sentences: the plain line carries belief, the twist carries curiosity (S16 B23) [primary].

| Plain statement | Twist |
|---|---|
| "Gravity slows time." | "Not as a metaphor. Measurably." |
| "Every ship that crosses Panama climbs a staircase of water." | "Then it pours the staircase into the sea." |
| "Your body replaces most of its cells." | "Not the ones in the middle of your eye." |
| "The war had one casualty." | "It was a pig." |

Use it at the start of each section and at most turns [house]. Don't stack two twists: the plain
line between them is what keeps the viewer believing.

## 3. One governing metaphor, without characters

Bencivenga calls metaphor the "golden key" (S16 B25) [primary]; the house rule is one governing
metaphor per video, drawn, reused and paid off (`principles.md` §14). MAINSPRING has no characters
or mascots (our ban list rules out bean people, blobs and caricatures; `animation-study/README.md`
§6.1), so **the metaphor's objects are the cast**.

How a metaphor becomes the animation [house]:
- **Pick it from our kit**: counter wheels, date flaps, a wound spring, ledgers, stamps, tally
  blocks, paper tape, balance scales (`animation-study/README.md` §5). The metaphor should be a
  *machine*, because a machine can show cause and effect.
- **Map every term of the explanation to a part of the machine**, once, and keep the mapping for
  the whole video: in the GPS example, each clock is a mainspring clock, time running faster is
  the hands pulling ahead, the correction is a key turned before launch.
- **Agency belongs to objects**: a counter wheel can hesitate, a stamp can come down, a spring can
  run out. Objects can act; they don't get faces, names or voices.
- **Real people appear as their records**: a signature, a logbook, a photo redrawn on card, a quote
  card. Never as a cartoon of the person (that's also the safe pattern in
  `fact-check-playbook.md` §5).
- **Pay it off in the climax**: the machine from the cold open does the final reveal (the two
  clocks, the staircase, the ledger with one line not stamped).
- **Narration names the metaphor once, then stops naming it.** "Think of each satellite as a
  clock on a tower" once; after that, the picture carries it.

## 4. Levity beats

Bencivenga's first copy chief banned jokes in headlines (S16 B27) [primary], and the
comedic-history lane still needs jokes in the body. The rules [house]:
- **The joke is a fact, a cut or a prop.** Deadpan narration over an absurd true detail ("The
  bucket is still in Modena."), a cut from a huge army to a small bucket, a ruler snapping.
- **One levity beat every ~60–90 s at most**, and never in the 3 s before a proof beat: the
  viewer has to take the proof seriously.
- **Never mock victims**, never invent dialogue or thoughts for real people, never use a funny
  voice (no character voices; our narrator is one deadpan voice).
- **Understatement beats exaggeration.** Exaggeration is hype in a different costume
  (S16 B3: hype triggers "yeah, sure").
- **Don't borrow running gags or catchphrases** from other channels (`animation-study/README.md`
  §6.2 lists the lines we can't use).

## 5. Keeping the middle unpredictable

"Predictability kills curiosity" (S16 B23) [primary] is the body's main risk: once viewers can
guess the next five minutes, they leave.
- **Every section opens a question the last section made unavoidable** (but/therefore, never "and
  then"; `retention-script` §10).
- **Put the step the viewer would have guessed second, and the one they wouldn't, first** when the
  logic allows.
- **The back half must add information, not recap** (`retention-script` self-check 12).
- **Run the Borden heckler on each paragraph** (S16 B24): *Ho-hum?* *Why bring that up?* *For
  instance?* *So what?* If a line provokes "for instance?", the next shot is the instance.
