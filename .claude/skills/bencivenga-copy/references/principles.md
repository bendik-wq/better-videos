# Bencivenga's principles, translated for YouTube documentaries

Every principle below is paraphrased. Short quotes are marked and sourced (`sources.md` S-numbers).
Evidence labels: **[primary]** = Bencivenga's own site or newsletter; **[student]** = reported by
someone who attended his seminars or worked with him; **[secondary]** = a summary by a third
party; **[house]** = our adaptation, not his claim (none of the [house] rules has been tested on
our channel yet; `checklist.md` §10 lists what each rests on). S16 is his newsletter, read in full;
"B7" means Bullet 7.

---

## 1. The job: overcome four objections, in order

**[primary, S1]** The Persuasion Equation is about **content, not style**: it tells you what an
ad must contain, not how to word or design it. The four parts can come in any order and any
creative form, as long as all four are there. He scores each part out of 25.

| Part | Objection it defeats | Documentary translation [house] |
|---|---|---|
| **Urgent Problem** | No interest | The viewer must feel a question they want answered *now*. Not "a topic": an itch. "How does a lake kill people?" is an itch; "Lakes of Cameroon" is a topic |
| **Unique Promise** | No difference | Why this video, not the five already on the topic. A primary record, a mechanism nobody animated, a new date, an overlooked person |
| **Unquestionable Proof** | No belief | The viewer must believe the title within a minute. Evidence on screen, not narration asserting |
| **User-Friendly Proposition** | No decision | Make starting and staying easy: a clear contract (what you'll understand), a sense of pace, chapters that promise payoffs |

His shorthand **[primary, S1]**: no problem, no sale. Problems are markets. And as desire goes up,
price resistance goes down. For us **[house]**: the stronger the itch, the more minutes a viewer
will "pay".

**How to use it:** before writing a word, fill the four boxes for the idea in one sentence each. If
a box is empty, the copy will fail no matter how well it is written. An empty Unique Promise box
is the most common failure on history topics: everyone has made "The Titanic". A filled one reads:
"The man who had the key to the binoculars locker was taken off the crew at Southampton, and kept
the key."

## 2. Proof ≥ claim (the credibility lesson)

**[primary, S16 B3, B7]** In his own newsletter: "Never make your claim bigger than your proof",
and join claim and proof "at the hip" in the headline so you never trumpet one without the other.
His reason: over-marketed readers meet every big claim with two silent words, *yeah, sure*, so
raising the promise just makes "a bigger pile of hype"; raising the proof is what sets you apart.
Readers screen with two questions: is this of interest to me, and is it believable? Most writers
answer only the first. To predict which of two headlines will win without testing, ask which
offers the more compelling proof (B7: Caples' *When Doctors Have Headaches, What Do They Do?* beat
*Tension Headache?* by 71%, because the doctors are the proof).

**[primary, S16 B3]** The IF…THEN guarantee: "If you've got 20 minutes a month, I guarantee…" beat
the control, because an easy condition makes a big promise believable; a *moderately* easy one is
better still, because "candor is gloriously effective".

**[student, S6; S9 excerpt]** Students of the retirement seminar and the "100" notes report the
same rule (proof ≥ claim; believable proof is the rarest element; coming on too strong costs
credibility). These now corroborate S16 rather than carry the rule.

Translation **[house]**:
- **Calibrate the title to the proof.** If the best evidence is a single eyewitness, the title can't
  be "The Truth About…". If it's an official report with numbers, it can carry a hard number.
- **Big claim, then immediate proof.** The gap between claim and proof should be seconds, not
  minutes. A title claim must be backed on screen before 1:00.
- **Underclaim, then overdeliver.** "The 72-second signal no one has explained" is stronger than
  "Proof of Aliens?" because the video can fully deliver it.
- **Specifics are proof.** An exact number, a name, a time of day is believed more than a round
  figure, because a round figure sounds made up.

## 3. Specificity

**[primary, S16 B14; secondary, S10]** Gather "seven times more information than you can use",
because each factual nugget "will outweigh 100 airy adjectives" (B14). His job-interview ad didn't
say "lots of resumes": it pictured seven full-size sacks stuffed with resumes (as analysed in
S10). (The "fuzzy dice" Bullet, B16, is about something else: meet buyers exactly where they're
searching. See §16.)

Translation **[house]**:
- Replace each adjective with a fact. "A massive explosion" → "the explosion that blew a ship's
  anchor two miles inland".
- Prefer odd numbers to round ones *when that is what the source says* ("1,746", "72 seconds").
  Never round a sourced odd number up for drama, and never invent a precise-looking one.
- Prefer a picture to a statistic: a number the animator can draw beats a number the viewer must
  imagine (seven sacks > 2,000 resumes).
- Name the person, the street, the hour. Each named detail is a tiny proof.

## 4. Reason-why copy and the word "because"

**[primary, S16 B9–B10]** Give reasons in three places: why this beats the alternatives
(including doing nothing), why the claims are true, and why to act now. He drew on Ellen Langer's
copier study (via Cialdini): a request with a reason, even a thin one, was granted far more often.

Translation **[house]**:
- Every surprising fact gets its *because* in the same sentence or the next one. "The lake didn't
  overflow. It exhaled, because 200 metres down, the water was holding carbon dioxide like a shaken
  bottle."
- "Why watch this" in the hook is a reason-why: name the reason the viewer has never seen it
  explained this way (the record, the reconstruction, the new finding).
- "Why now": tie evergreen history to a live peg when one exists (an anniversary, a new find, a
  similar event this year). If none exists, the reason is the stake: "it could happen again, and
  here's where".

## 5. Pre-empt the objection (acknowledge disbelief)

**[primary, S16 B3]** Candor raises response. **[secondary, S4, unverified provenance]** The
"toolbox" notes add *acknowledge disbelief* to the proof list. The two together: name the
reader's doubt yourself, then answer it or bound it honestly; it disarms the doubt and makes the
next claim more believable.

Translation **[house]**:
- In the hook, say the cynic's line out loud, then hit it with the proof object: "This sounds like
  folklore. But the telegraph operators' logs survive, and they say…"
- Name the obvious counter-explanation before the viewer does, then show why it fails ("It wasn't
  a volcano. There was no ash.").
- **Candor buys belief.** Say what is uncertain: "Estimates run from 2,000 to 2,500. We use the
  official count." Admitting a limit makes the confident claims more credible.
- Never pre-empt an objection you can't answer. Then the honest move is the open question
  (LEMMiNO-style ending, `video-autopsies/README.md` §B8).

## 6. Fascinations (bullets) are the core skill

**[student, S3]** Bencivenga worked in the Boardroom tradition alongside Mel Martin, "the
master of fascinations", whose envelope teasers ("What never to eat on an airplane", "Bills it's
okay to pay late") broke records. A fascination tells you *something* is worth knowing and hides
*what* it is, so the only way to scratch the itch is to read on.

A strong fascination has **[house, from S3 plus general bullet craft in S5, which is not Bencivenga-specific]**:
1. **A real itch** (fear, desire, or a gap in something the reader thought they knew).
2. **A specific hidden payload**: the viewer can tell there's one exact answer.
3. **Built-in believability**: a number, a source, a page number (Boardroom added page numbers so
   readers could check the claim). For us: a date, a named record, an on-screen source.
4. **No give-away of the answer.**

Full craft and 40 worked examples: `fascinations.md`.

## 7. Interest = benefit + curiosity; predictability kills it

**[primary, S16 B23]** In his tribute to Gary Halbert he gives the headline formula: interest is
benefit plus curiosity (I = B + C), either one alone is "a devastating weakening", and
"predictability kills curiosity". He learned it from Caples, who told young writers to study the
*Reader's Digest* contents page: a column of titles competing side by side, where a few pull hard
and most don't. That page is the YouTube home feed.

Translation **[house]**:
- Benefit for a documentary viewer = *understanding*, *a story to retell*, *a stake that touches
  them*. Curiosity = the specific gap.
- A title that reads like the Wikipedia article name ("The Halifax Explosion") has no gap. A title
  that only teases ("You Won't Believe What Happened") has no benefit or specificity.
- If the viewer can predict the video from the title, the title fails. Test: ask what the video
  will say; if a friend can guess the twist, find a new angle.

## 8. The Borden questions

**[primary, S16 B24]** Run every headline and every paragraph past four heckler questions:
*Ho-hum?* (it's boring), *Why bring that up?* (relevance), *For instance?* (give an example),
*So what?* (what does it mean for me).

Translation **[house]**: these are the four checks for every chapter opener. If a line provokes
"for instance?", the next shot must be the instance.

---

## 9. What are we really selling?

**[primary, S16 B4]** The cosmetics line he liked: in the factory we make cosmetics; in the store
we sell hope. Ask what the product *really* is to the buyer.

Translation **[house]**: we don't sell "a video about Tunguska". We sell *the feeling of knowing the
answer to a mystery*, *a scale you can't forget*, *a story you'll tell at dinner*, or *a warning
that touches you*. Name the real product for each episode in the brief; it decides the title shape.

A desire list attributed to him in the unattributed "toolbox" notes (S4; provenance weak, so
treat it as a useful list, not his rule): recognition, rejuvenation, relaxation, relief, existing beliefs,
money, results, revenge, role performance, romance (stimulation). Documentary viewers mostly buy
**stimulation** (wonder, dread), **role performance** (sounding smart, understanding the news),
**relief** (the mystery solved) and **existing beliefs** (reopening something they already
believe).

## 10. Research wins the game

**[primary, S16 B27; student, S8]** His three lessons, in his own Q&A: copy is salesmanship in
print, not clever wordsmithing; writer's block is "Lazy Research Syndrome" (Caples told him to
gather seven times more than he could use); writing is thinking on paper, so turn the problem over,
sleep on it and write in the morning. Students conclude the game is won or lost in research (S8).

Translation **[house]**:
- Packaging comes from the research pack, not from imagination. The best titles are a fact found on
  page 40 of a report.
- Before drafting, list the 10 strongest proof elements found. The title, hook and climax are built
  from the top three.
- **The 25-headline overnight trick** (S16 B28): write 25 titles before bed, choose in the morning.

---

## 11. A gifted product beats a gifted pen

**[primary, S16 B19]** The product is the horse and the copywriter the jockey; copy can't
create demand that isn't there, and a me-too product gets a me-too response.

Translation **[house]**: if the *story* is weak, no title saves it. Kill weak ideas at the
`idea-engine` stage. A gifted topic has: a known name, an absurd concrete object or number, a
mechanism that can be animated, and at least one primary record.

---

## 12. Fear of loss beats promise of gain (use carefully)

**[primary, S16 B13]** Losses motivate more than equivalent gains: "it costs you an extra 75
cents a day" beat "you can save an extra 75 cents a day" by a wide margin (a study he cites).

Translation **[house]**: "What we lost when…" and "the warning nobody heeded" frames outperform
"the amazing discovery". Allowed only for real, sourced stakes. Never invent a threat to the viewer.

---

## 13. Make the first step easy (the monkey's fist)

**[primary, S16 B15]** The light line thrown first, which then pulls the heavy rope: make the
first commitment tiny and irresistible.

Translation **[house]**: the first 5 seconds are the monkey's fist. Ask nothing; show the image the
thumbnail promised. The contract (the question stack) comes at 0:25, once they are already holding
the line.

---

## 14. Metaphor as a persuasion tool

**[primary, S16 B25]** Metaphor is his "golden key": nothing persuades as quickly or as
memorably as a well-made comparison.

Translation **[house]**: in animation the metaphor *is the shot* (`docs/library/visual-metaphors.md`).
One governing metaphor per video ("the lake was a shaken bottle"), drawn, reused, paid off in the
climax.

---

## 15. Setbacks and testing

**[primary, S16 B7, B8; student, S11]** He chose winners by testing, and when a test lost, he studied
the winner to learn why. A student who worked with him advises writing several test panels to raise
the odds of beating the control (S11).

Translation **[house]**: always ship two titles and three thumbnails into Test & Compare
(`packaging` Step 5). Log the loser's shape and the reason in `docs/library/idea-log.md`. One
variable per test.

---

## 16. Intercept the viewer where they're already looking (the "Fuzzy Dice" Bullet)

**[primary, S16 B16]** A master salesman's rule: intercept customers wherever they're already
looking for someone like you. Online, that meant matching the page to the exact words people
search ("leather steering wheel covers", not "car accessories").

Translation **[house]**: YouTube has two doors, and a title must know which one it's for.
- **Search** (evergreen explainers): the noun people type ("GPS", "Panama Canal", "tardigrade")
  goes in the title, early, and in the description's first line. Jared Owen's catalogue is 100%
  questions that name the object (`animation-study/README.md` §1.1) [observed]: a search-shaped
  catalogue.
- **Browse and suggested** (home feed, the column next to another video): the itch and the proof
  cue go first; the searchable noun can come later or live in the description and chapters.
- When one title must do both, put the search noun inside the itch ("GPS Would Drift 10 km a Day
  Without Einstein") and test it against the browse version (`worked-example.md` §3).

## 17. No cleverness in the headline (where the comedy goes)

**[primary, S16 B27]** His first copy chief made sure he never again showed a pun or joke in a
headline: "I am not the star of the ad. The prospect and the product are." Writers who show off
are like "fishermen who reveal the hook".

Translation **[house]**: in the comedic-history lane, the **title is plain and literally true**
("Two Cities Went to War Over a Bucket"); the absurdity of the fact is the pull. The jokes live in
the body: a cut, a prop, a deadpan beat (`SKILL.md` Workflow F; `fascinations.md` §3b).

## 18. Build on the strongest proof you own

**[primary, S16 B29]** He advised clients to build their products on their strongest proof
elements (case histories, endorsements, testimonials, reasons why, outcomes, expertise, and "a
spirit of candor and integrity"), and to make credibility a visible part of the marketing, because
skepticism is the enemy most often met.

Translation **[house]**: the channel's proof is its method. Show it: the source card, the
computed sum, the redrawn document, "here's how we know". Over a season, a reputation for
showing the record is itself a proof element that every new title borrows.
