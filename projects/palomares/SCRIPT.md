## COLD OPEN
1. Thirty-one thousand feet over Spain, an American B-52 slams into its own fuel tanker.
2. Seven men die, and four hydrogen bombs fall out of the fireball.
3. Three come down near a farming village called Palomares. Two of them crack open and dust the fields with plutonium.
4. The fourth is gone.
5. A fisherman saw a parachute go into the sea, but all he can point to is open water.
6. So the U.S. Navy bets dozens of ships and one tiny submarine on his memory.
7. They find it. | They get a line on it.
8. And they lose it again.
9. Sixty years later, the bomb is long gone. | The fence in Palomares is still up.
10. So how do you lose the same hydrogen bomb twice? | And why is there still plutonium behind that fence?

## 1. FOUR FELL. THREE LANDED.
11. Twenty past ten, January the seventeenth, 1966. Clear blue sky over the Spanish coast.
12. Every day, American bombers carrying live H-bombs refuel up here.
13. In this bomber's belly: four hydrogen bombs. | Each one more than seventy times the Hiroshima bomb.
14. At the controls, Larry Messinger eases up under the tanker's boom, a little fast. | "All of a sudden," he said, "all hell seemed to break loose."
15. The tanker's belly rips open. Jet fuel pours over the bomber, and a fireball swallows both planes.
16. The tanker has no ejection seats. All four men aboard die.
17. The radar navigator ejects, but can't get free of his seat. He drags his parachute out by hand. | It opens just before he hits the ground.
18. Villagers cut him free, and a local electrician drives him to a clinic.
19. Three men on the bomber don't make it. | Seven dead. Four alive.
20a. A fishing boat picks up the co-pilot, Mike Rooney. | In port, it rams the dock.
20b. Then he's put in a bread truck, and the driver keeps looking back at him. | Rooney does the math: plane crash, boat wreck. One more, and "that's going to be three strikes and I'm out."
21. A wing burns in a tomato field. Radar foil hangs in the trees like tinsel. | Nobody on the ground is hurt.
22. Bomb one lies in a dry riverbed, nearly intact. "Parece un torpedo," says the policeman who reports it. It looks like a torpedo.
23. Next morning, bomb two turns up behind the cemetery, in a crater twenty feet across. Bomb three lies by the houses.
24. In bombs two and three, the conventional explosive went off, at least in part. | So why didn't Palomares vanish?
25. A nuclear blast needs every charge around the core to fire at once. | A crash fires them from one side, and blows the core apart instead.
26. No nuclear blast. | Just plutonium dust, settling over the tomato fields.
27. Three bombs found. | The rack held four.
28. A villager had picked up a round metal plate: bomb four's tail cover. It blows off when the parachute system fires.
29. Bomb number four came down under a sixty-four-foot parachute. | Just not on land.

## 2. THE DEAD MAN IN THE PARACHUTE
30. At the moment of the crash, five miles out, a shrimp boat is about to haul in its nets. The skipper: Francisco Simó Orts.
31. He watches the fire fall, then the parachutes. Under a small one, half a man, "with his guts trailing."
32. Under a big one, a dead man, "hanging still and silent." It hits the sea hard, | and it's gone.
33. His nets are still down, so he can't get there in time. He memorizes where it went instead: a mountain lined up behind an old chimney.
34. Days later, he apologizes to an Air Force lawyer. He's sorry he couldn't save the other flyer. | "What other flyer?"
35a. Officially, nobody was missing a bomb either. A reporter asked an Air Force spokesman about it. | "What bomb?" Just debris, he said.
35b. So the reporter tried again: any sign of the thing you say is not the bomb? | The reply: "There is no sign of the thing that is not the bomb."
36. Day five. Simó guides a Navy minesweeper out, steering by the shore. Sonar finds two echoes, two thousand feet down.
37. Fish, maybe. Or a parachute, partly filled with air bubbles.
38. Did the Navy ignore him? | No. Admiral William Guest's very first search area took in Simó's sighting. Guest later compared the job to combing the hills "at midnight on a moonless night."
39. In February, a bomb engineer sketches what Simó saw. Simó redraws it.
40. Now it makes sense. | The half man was an empty parachute bag under a small chute. The dead man, hanging still, was bomb four, under its big canopy.
41. Around then, Simó drags his nets where the Navy has banned fishing, and snags something huge.
42. In Barbara Moran's telling, he radios the Navy: | I have your bomb. If you want it, come get it.
43. It's a two-ton concrete block, anchoring a Navy buoy.
45. Day thirty-one. A Soviet spy ship, Lotsman, parks at the edge of the zone. | Once, it comes within twenty-five yards of an American warship.
46. In Washington, a Navy scientist, John Craven, maps the odds. | Out with the fleet, Tony Richardson runs a mechanical calculator every night, grading the sea square by square.
47. Guest takes one look at Richardson: "Where the hell did you get this high school kid?" | Palomares was one of the first big searches run on the odds. | Remember that calculator.
48. Day forty-three. The tiny submarine, Alvin, catches a furrow on the seabed. | Marvin McCamis, an Alvin pilot watching from the surface, said it looked "like a barrel had been dragged over the bottom."
49. Then Alvin loses the trail. | And the clock on the whole search starts running out.

## 3. ONE MORE DAY
50. Day forty-four. Washington finally admits one weapon "has not yet been recovered." The Boston Globe: "One of our H-bombs is missing."
51. Spain had a tourist season to save. So the American ambassador, Angier Biddle Duke, went for a swim.
52. Day fifty. Duke wades in at a beach down the coast. Trunks, loafers, a blue bathrobe, a bathing cap. Asked about radioactivity: | "If this is radioactivity, I love it!"
53. That afternoon, he swims again at the beach in Palomares itself.
54. The water was fine. | The plutonium was in the fields behind the beach.
55. On land, cleanup crews ate box lunches with their legs dangling into a bomb crater. | They were fed tomatoes from the contaminated fields. One military policeman: | "Breakfast, lunch and dinner."
56. Among them, an Air Force health technician, Victor Skaar: "Did we follow protocol? Hell, no." | Keep an eye on that name.
57. At first, soil had to go if it read over seven thousand counts a minute on a radiation meter. | By the end, the cutoff was sixty thousand, more than eight times looser.
58. Hot soil was shipped to America. Most of the rest was "plowed under and watered down." | Some soil and crops that read between those two numbers went into trenches near the bomb two crater. | Nobody kept a precise record of where.
59. Back at sea, day fifty-six: the ambassador braces for the search to be called off. | The diving chief, Red Moody, flies home.
60. Day fifty-seven. Guest calls the area "searched to ninety-eight percent" and wants Alvin moved. A lieutenant, Brad Mooney, pushes back.
61. Guest: "All right, goddamn it. One more day, and that's all."
62. That same day, Simó is out on a Navy minesweeper, offering his own nets.
63. Today, McCamis takes the controls. It's his son's birthday, and he's asked to be put right back on that furrow.
64. Half a mile down, he backs Alvin down a slope that's nearly a cliff, to keep the furrow in his front window.
65. A gray, featureless bottom. A parachute. | And under it, a fin. | Both pilots shout: "That's it! That's it!"
66. Up top, Admiral Guest is handed a note at lunch: "Alvin reports instrument panel." Code for: we've found it. | He leaves the table without a word.
67. On the minesweeper, Simó hears the news. | It's close to where he saw the dead man fall, fifty-seven days ago.
68. Alvin wedges into a crevice below the chute and switches off its lights. Three men sit in the dark beside the bomb for eight hours. | They share one illicit cigarette.
69. The calculator hadn't found it. | A fisherman's landmarks, a furrow and a stubborn crew had.
70. The bomb was perched on a slope, deep in an undersea canyon. | Finding it took fifty-seven days. | Losing it would take three minutes.

## 4. LOST AGAIN
71. Red Moody gets the call at home. | "Tell them I'm coming back."
72. The bomb weighed more than a ton. Alvin's arm could lift fifty pounds. Within four days, it had slid twenty feet downhill.
73. On CBS, Charles Kuralt told America: "We live in a world in which it is possible to mislay a hydrogen bomb."
74. And if America didn't get it back? Kuralt spelled it out: | "the nagging, distant possibility that someone else will."
75. The Navy lowered Poodle, a steel frame shaped like a giant shuttlecock, with sonar beacons and a strobe light. | It fell over and tangled, and the bomb shifted again.
76. Then the weather clears, for one day only. | March the twenty-fourth.
77. Alvin twists a grappling hook into the parachute's lines. | From the hook, a rope runs to an anchor, then the steel frame, then one nylon line, half a mile up to the ship Mizar.
78. Guest doesn't want to lift on one line. But bad weather is coming back.
79. McCamis asks to stay down and watch. Too dangerous. Alvin is ordered up.
80. So nobody is on the bottom. | Just that blinking strobe, in the dark.
81. On deck, Moody asks for small boats on either side to hold the ship steady. | Mizar's captain says he can handle his own ship.
82. Around sunset, Mizar's winch begins to turn. Guest prays. Moody and Jon Lindbergh, Charles Lindbergh's son, watch the line.
83. Remember Guest's moonless night? | Tonight, it isn't a figure of speech.
84. An hour in, the steel frame rises. The anchor follows. | And the bomb lifts off the seabed.
85. For three minutes, the bomb hangs half a mile below Mizar. | Then the instruments jump. | The line goes slack.
86. Lindbergh felt "a terrible sinking feeling." Moody's first thought, Barbara Moran writes, can't be repeated here.
87. It's about nine at night. | The bomb is gone again.
88. An hour later, they reel the anchor in. The line below it ends in a frayed stump.
89. Three-quarters of its strands were cut clean. The rest had just split.
90. Moody, who had helped design the rig, felt personally responsible.
91. Mizar's captain had cut power mid-lift, and the ship had drifted. Was that it? A sharp rock? A snag on the anchor? | Nobody ever figured it out for sure.
92. Kuralt had said it was possible to mislay a hydrogen bomb. | Two days later, America did.
93. That same day, a ship sailed for South Carolina loaded with barrels of Palomares soil and crops.
95. At dinner, McCamis: "Oh, boy. Now we got to go find it again."

## 5. THE THIRD LINE
96. Next morning, McCamis said the seabed where the bomb had lain looked "torn up by bulldozers."
97. A second submarine, Aluminaut, passed within a hundred feet of the old spot several times, | and saw nothing.
98. Guest guessed uphill. | A false lead.
99. Days of nothing. The official summary would later call it, with great restraint, "much anxiety."
100. Back home, a board was already weighing whether the Navy could give up. | Its chairman knew what failure meant: "a congressional investigation, and mud all over the face of everyone."
101. Day seventy-five. The Alvin crew finally gets to search downhill. McCamis spots "a clod of dirt that seemed out of place."
102. And past it, the parachute, "still tightly wrapped around an object." | A hundred and twenty yards away, and two hundred and fifty feet deeper.
103. This time, they brought a robot, Curve. Its pilot, Larry Brady, said its patched-up cable "looked like a python had swallowed a dog."
104. Guest wanted three lines before he'd lift. Curve's engineer warned the ballooning parachute could tangle. Guest: "I can't help that."
105. Curve set line one, then line two. | Each one dragged the bomb deeper still.
106. One in the morning. Curve goes down to attach the third line. The admiral asks for a closer look.
107. Brady: | "We're fouled."
108. Curve is caught in the parachute, tangled up with the bomb it came to save.
109. Guest wants to cut its cable and come back later. The head of the robot team faints.
110. Brad Mooney, the lieutenant who'd won that one more day, argues with the admiral alone, for hours. | "Admiral, this is dumb as hell to cut this thing loose."
111. His point: wrapped in that parachute, the trapped robot was holding the bomb. | It had become the third line.
112. Before dawn, under a nearly full moon, the lift begins.
113. This time, two small boats hold the lifting ship, Petrel, steady. | Just what Moody had asked for, the night they lost it.
114. Guest would later say: "I'd prefer combat any day to this."
115. It rises so smoothly, they hardly notice it leave the bottom.
116. An hour and forty-five minutes later, the parachute breaks the surface.
117. Eight forty-six in the morning. An American deck. Nobody else's. | The ship's log: | "Weapon on deck with parachute."
118. Eighty days after the crash.
119. "Thank God we finally did it," Guest said. | Nobody cheered.
120. Simó got a medallion, and a scroll. It praised his "profound knowledge of the sea," which "led to the finding of the nuclear bomb."
121. In 1968, the Navy lost a submarine, the Scorpion. Richardson's math put the wreck within two hundred yards of its likeliest square.
122. The math did find something. | Just not this bomb.
123. But two bombs had cracked open on land, and not all of that dust got on a ship.

## 6. THE REST OF IT
124. Palomares, today. | A head-high wire fence rings about a hundred acres.
125. Around the year 2000, Spain measured again: "higher than first estimated." So the state took the worst land. | And put up the fence.
126. Those trenches nobody mapped? In 2007, ground radar swept the fields by the bomb two crater. | It found the probable sites of two of them.
127. Underneath the fence: enough soil to fill twenty Olympic pools, holding about a pound of plutonium.
128. In 2015, John Kerry signed a statement of intent with Spain, and called for action to resolve it "once and for all." | As reported, it wasn't binding. No deal has followed.
129. A golf-resort company fought the state over the price of its contaminated hillside. It wanted almost seventy-five euros a square meter. It got | sixty-eight cents.
130. And the men who shoveled the dirt? | The New York Times reported they gave more than fifteen hundred urine samples. The Air Force's chief of radiation testing threw out about two in three.
131. Decades later, he told the Times: | "All you can do is bow your head and say you are sorry."
132. The men who buried the barrels in South Carolina became eligible for radiation compensation in 2012. The airmen who dug up that soil? | 2022. | Victor Skaar took Veterans Affairs to court.
133. Skaar says: "First they told me there were no records, which I knew was a lie, because I helped make them." | In January 2026, the court turned him down. One judge noted he was eighty-nine.
134. Lettuces grow a few steps from the fence. | Nothing there says why it's fenced.
135. The village mayor says it's "just a few plots." Other residents put it simply: | "We only ask that they clean it up."
136. But in court, Spain's own state lawyers argue the other way. A cleanup, they said in 2025, "could be counterproductive."
137. In April 2026, according to a local paper, they said it outright: | "Palomares was never a nuclear accident."
138. Doses, they say, are "low and stable." | No judge has ruled.
139. And beside the beach where the ambassador went swimming, environmentalists say, sixteen hundred new homes have been approved.
140. Bomb number four came home. | The rest of it never left.
