## COLD OPEN
1. Thirty-one thousand feet over Spain, an American B-52 slams into its own fuel tanker.
2. Seven men die, and four hydrogen bombs fall out of the fireball.
3. Three come down near a farming village called Palomares. Two of them crack open and dust the fields with plutonium.
4. The fourth is gone.
5. A fisherman saw a parachute go into the sea, but all he can point to is open water.
6. So the U.S. Navy bets dozens of ships and one tiny submarine on his memory.
7. They find it. | They get a line on it.
8. And they lose it again.
9. Sixty years later, the bomb is long gone. | The fence in Palomares is still up.
10. So how do you lose the same hydrogen bomb twice? | And why is there still plutonium under that fence?

## 1. FOUR FELL. THREE LANDED.
11. It's twenty past ten on Monday, January the seventeenth, 1966. The sky over the Spanish coast is bright, clear blue.
12. American bombers on airborne alert refuel up here every day, with live weapons aboard.
13. In this bomber's belly: four Mark twenty-eight hydrogen bombs. | Each one more than seventy times the Hiroshima bomb.
14. The relief pilot eases up under the tanker's boom, a little fast. "There was no call for breakaway," he said. | "But all of a sudden, all hell seemed to break loose."
15. The tanker's belly rips open. Jet fuel pours over the bomber, and a fireball swallows both planes.
16. That's the official finding. | Two of the survivors always insisted something exploded in the bomber's tail first.
17. The tanker has no ejection seats. All four men aboard die.
18. The bomber's commander ejects into the fire. "I knew I was going to lose my arm. But I thought it was better to lose that than lose everything."
19. The radar navigator's seat won't separate. He drags his parachute out by hand. | It opens just before he hits the ground.
20. Three men on the bomber don't make it. | Seven dead. Four alive.
21. Fishermen pull three survivors from the sea. One rescue boat rams the dock. Then it's a bread truck to the infirmary, and the co-pilot, Mike Rooney, does the maths.
22. A plane crash, a boat wreck. One more, and it's "three strikes and I'm out."
23. On land, it's raining metal. A wing burns in a tomato field. Chaff hangs in the trees like silver tinsel. | Nobody on the ground is hurt.
24. Bomb one lies in a dry riverbed, nearly intact. "Parece un torpedo," says the policeman who spots it. It looks like a torpedo.
25. Next morning, bomb two turns up behind the cemetery, in a crater twenty feet across. Bomb three lies by the houses. A shopkeeper stamps out the fire beside it.
26. In bombs two and three, the explosive went off, at least in part. | So why is Palomares still there?
27. To set off the bomb, every charge around the plutonium core has to fire at once, squeezing it inward. A crash sets the explosive off from one side, and blows the core apart instead.
28. No nuclear blast. | Just plutonium dust in the wind.
29. Three bombs in a day. | The rack held four.
30. Then a villager hands over a round metal plate: bomb four's tail cover. It only blows off when the parachutes deploy.
31. Bomb number four came down under a sixty-four-foot parachute. | Just not on land.

## 2. THE DEAD MAN IN THE PARACHUTE
32. Five miles out, a shrimp boat is raising its nets. The skipper is Francisco Simó Orts.
33. He watches the fire fall, then the parachutes. Under a small one, half a man, "with his guts trailing."
34. Under a big one, a dead man, "hanging still and silent." It hits the sea hard, | and it's gone.
35. His nets are still down, so he can't get there. He fixes the spot instead: a mountain lined up behind an old chimney.
36. Days later, he apologises to an Air Force lawyer. He's sorry he couldn't save the other flyer. | "What other flyer?" Every airman was already counted.
37. Officially, nobody was missing a bomb either. Spain wanted it quiet, and the Americans went along. Asked about it, an Air Force spokesman replied: | "What bomb?"
38. Day five. Simó guides a Navy minesweeper out with no compass, steering by the shore. The sonar picks up two echoes, two thousand feet down.
39. They pull away and ask him to find the spot again. | He does. Same two echoes.
40. The report calls them "either a school of fish or a parachute partially filled with air bubbles." Some officers believed him. Some didn't. | All he could point at was water.
41. So did the Navy ignore him? | No. The admiral in charge, William Guest, drew his first search area to take in the fishermen's sighting.
42. Guest later compared the job to combing the hills "at midnight on a moonless night."
43. In early February, a bomb engineer from Sandia sat down with Simó and sketched what he'd seen. Simó took the pencil and redrew it.
44. Then it made sense. The half man was an empty parachute bag under a small chute. The dead man, hanging still, | was bomb four, swinging under its big canopy.
45. The engineer: "I was convinced absolutely that he had seen number four go into the sea."
46. Then came the submarine. Alvin: twenty-two feet long, room for three, and white. To one man, it looked like "a big white toilet."
47. Its pilots: Marvin McCamis, a high-school dropout and ex-submarine electrician, and Val Wilson.
48. In Washington, a Navy scientist, John Craven, sketched a map of the odds.
49. Out here, a twenty-seven-year-old mathematician, Tony Richardson, cranked a mechanical calculator every night, grading the sea square by square.
50. The admiral's verdict: "Where the hell did you get this high school kid?" | It was one of the first big real-world uses of probability-based search. | Remember that calculator.
51. Just outside the search zone, a Soviet spy ship sat watching. Once it came within twenty-five yards of an American destroyer.
52. Day forty-three. Val Wilson is at the controls when Alvin's camera catches something on the seabed: a furrow, "like a barrel had been dragged over the bottom."
53. Alvin goes back for it. | Nothing.

## 3. ONE MORE DAY
54. Next day, day forty-four, Washington finally admits one weapon "has not yet been recovered." The Boston Globe: "One of our H-bombs is missing."
55. Spain had a tourist season to save. So the American ambassador, Angier Biddle Duke, went for a swim.
56. Day fifty. In trunks, loafers, a blue bathrobe and a bathing cap, Duke wades into the sea down the coast. Asked if he'd detected any radioactivity: | "If this is radioactivity, I love it!"
57. That afternoon he swims again, at Palomares's own beach, with Spain's information minister. Duke, his aide said, invited himself along in borrowed trunks.
58. A State Department history concluded the swim "probably saved the tourist season."
59. The water was fine. | The plutonium was in the fields behind the beach.
60. The cleanup crews were cooks, clerks, "and even musicians from the Air Force band," the New York Times later reported.
61. Among them, an Air Force health technician, Victor Skaar: "Did we follow protocol? Hell, no." | Keep an eye on that name.
62. Some ate box lunches with their legs dangling into a bomb crater. They were fed tomatoes from the contaminated fields: "Breakfast, lunch and dinner."
63. So how clean is clean? The line was set in Geiger clicks per minute. An early deal said seven thousand. America's atomic agency called a hundred thousand "perfectly satisfactory." | They settled on sixty thousand: more than eight times the first number.
64. Above that, soil was scraped up and shipped to America. Below it, it was "plowed under and watered down."
65. Some of the dirt in between went into trenches near the bomb two crater. | Nobody kept a precise record of where.
66. Out at sea, the hunt was dying. It took twelve days and eight dives just to find that furrow again.
67. Day fifty-seven. Guest calls the area "searched to ninety-eight percent" and wants Alvin moved. A young officer, Brad Mooney, pushes back.
68. Guest: "All right, goddamn it. One more day, and that's all."
69. That morning, Simó is out on a Navy minesweeper, offering to snag the chute with his own nets if the Navy gives up.
70. Half a mile down, it's McCamis's son's birthday. He backs Alvin down a seventy-degree slope, to keep the furrow in his front window.
71. A grey, featureless bottom. Silt hanging in the lights. Then a parachute. | And under it, a fin.
72. "That's it! That's it!" | On the minesweeper, Simó's lunch is interrupted by the news.
73. The official record: "a parachute enshrouded object," contact two hundred and sixty-one, "in the vicinity of the place where Simó Orts reported seeing a weapon descend."
74. The calculator hadn't found it. | A fisherman's landmarks, a furrow and a stubborn crew had.
75. Topside, Guest asked: "How do you know it's not a parachute full of mud?" McCamis: "In all my life I'd never had my intelligence so insulted."
76. The bomb was perched on that slope, deep inside an undersea canyon. | Finding it was the easy part.

## 4. LOST AGAIN
77. The bomb weighed more than a ton. Alvin's arm could lift fifty pounds, at most. Within four days, the bomb had slid twenty feet downhill.
78. Then came Poodle, a steel frame "like a giant shuttlecock," with sonar beacons and a strobe light. It fell over and tangled, | and the bomb shifted again.
79. The ambassador had already drafted the press release: "The fourth and final weapon… has been recovered today."
80. On CBS, Charles Kuralt told America: "We live in a world in which it is possible to mislay a hydrogen bomb."
81. Two days later, the weather breaks for one day. | March the twenty-fourth.
82. Alvin twists a grappling hook into the parachute's lines. Above the hook: a rope, a heavy anchor, the steel frame, | and one nylon line, half a mile up to the ship.
83. Guest doesn't want to lift on one line. His staff say it can hold ten times the weight, and bad weather is coming back.
84. McCamis asks to stay down and watch. | The answer is no: too dangerous. Alvin is ordered up.
85. So nobody is on the bottom. | Just that blinking strobe, half a mile down.
86. Around sunset, the winch on the ship Mizar starts to turn. Guest starts to pray.
87. Remember Guest's moonless night? | Tonight, there's almost no moon at all.
88. An hour in, the steel frame rises. Then the anchor. | Then the bomb lifts off the seabed.
89. Three minutes later, the instruments jump. The diving chief, Red Moody, and Jon Lindbergh, son of the famous flyer, are watching the line. | It goes slack.
90. Lindbergh felt "a terrible sinking feeling." Moody's first thought, Barbara Moran writes, can't be repeated here.
91. It's about nine at night. | The bomb is gone again.
92. It takes an hour to reel in the anchor. The line below it, the one tied to the bomb, ends in a frayed stump.
93. Lindbergh looked closely. Three-quarters of the strands were cut clean, as if on something sharp. The rest had just split.
94. The ship drifting? A rock? The anchor? A bad rope? | Nobody ever figured it out for sure.
95. At dinner, McCamis: "Oh, boy. Now we got to go find it again."
96. Two days after Kuralt's broadcast, | they had mislaid it.
97. That same day, a ship sailed for South Carolina with barrels of Palomares soil and vegetation. Fourteen hundred tons in all.
98. They could ship fourteen hundred tons of Spain across the Atlantic. | They couldn't hold on to one bomb.

## 5. WE'RE FOULED
99. Next morning, the seabed where the bomb had lain looked "torn up by bulldozers."
100. The submarine Aluminaut searched for five hours. It passed within a hundred feet of the old spot, several times, | and saw nothing.
101. Guest guessed the bomb had gone uphill. Alvin followed a track up the slope. | A false lead.
102. Red Moody, who had helped design the rig, felt personally responsible.
103. Day sixty-nine. Day seventy-two. Day seventy-four. | Nothing.
104. A submarine pilot put it in baseball terms: "Here we were in the ninth inning, and the score is zero to zero."
105. The Alvin crew kept lobbying to search downhill. | Day seventy-five, they get their way. McCamis spots "a clod of dirt that seemed out of place."
106. Then the parachute, "still tightly wrapped around an object." | A hundred and twenty yards away, and two hundred and fifty feet deeper.
107. This time, they brought a robot, Curve. Its patched cable "looked like a python had swallowed a dog."
108. Guest wanted three lines before he'd lift. The robot's engineer warned the ballooning parachute could tangle. Guest: "I can't help that."
109. The robot hooked the chute. The line dragged the bomb deeper still, and the chute billowed "like a Barnum and Bailey circus tent."
110. Day eighty, one in the morning. The robot dives for the third line. The admiral asks for a closer look.
111. Then the robot's pilot: | "We're fouled."
112. The robot is caught in the parachute, | tangled up with the bomb it came to save.
113. Guest wants to cut its cable and come back later. The head of the robot team faints.
114. The room is cleared, and Brad Mooney, the officer who'd begged for one more day, argues with the admiral alone, for hours. "Admiral, this is dumb as hell to cut this thing loose."
115. His point: the trapped robot wasn't in the way. Wrapped in that parachute, it was holding the bomb. | It was the third line.
116. At two minutes past five, Guest messaged the Air Force in code: the admiral "had a broken leg." | The lift was about to begin.
117. Before dawn, under a nearly full moon this time, two small boats hold the ship over the bomb on a flat calm sea.
118. It rises so smoothly, they hardly notice it leave the bottom.
119. An hour and forty-five minutes later, the parachute breaks the surface. Divers cut the robot free.
120. Eight forty-six in the morning. The ship's log: | "Weapon on deck with parachute."
121. Eighty days after the crash.
122. "Thank God we finally did it," Guest said. | Nobody cheered.
123. Simó got a scroll and a medallion. In time, he became known as Paco el de la Bomba. | Paco of the Bomb. He reportedly sued for five million dollars.
124. In 1968, the Navy lost a submarine, the Scorpion. Richardson's probability map put the wreck within two hundred yards of its most likely square.
125. The calculator found something after all.
126. Bomb four sailed home. | But two bombs had cracked open on land, and not all of that dust got on a ship.

## 6. THE REST OF IT
127. Those trenches nobody mapped in 1966? | In 2007, ground radar swept the fields by the bomb two crater and found the probable sites of two of them.
128. It took over forty years. By 1969, Time reported, half the village had left. In the 1980s, diggers cut irrigation reservoirs into the bomb two valley, | where nobody had recorded what was buried.
129. When Spain measured again, the contamination was "higher than first estimated." It took over the worst land, about a hundred acres, and fenced it off.
130. Underneath, by the official estimate: enough soil to fill twenty Olympic pools, | holding about a pound of plutonium.
131. In 2015, John Kerry signed a statement of intent with Spain, to resolve it "once and for all."
132. Its text, as reported, bound nobody. In 2024, American auditors reported "no final agreement has been reached." | None has been reported since.
133. Then Spain's own lawyers argued the other way. A cleanup, they told a court, "could be counterproductive."
134. In April 2026, a local paper reported, their brief went further. | "Palomares was never a nuclear accident."
135. Their case: people here get under a tenth of the public dose limit, and digging could stir up radioactive material. In 2013, Spain's nuclear regulator found no disease caused by the accident.
136. That case still awaits a ruling. In another, a golf-resort company asked almost seventy-five euros a square metre for its contaminated hillside. Andalusia's high court backed the state's price: | sixty-eight cents.
137. And the men who shovelled the dirt? The New York Times reported they gave more than fifteen hundred urine samples. The Air Force's chief of radiation testing threw out about a thousand.
138. The workers who buried the barrels in South Carolina became eligible for federal radiation compensation in 2012. The airmen who dug up that soil waited ten more years.
139. Victor Skaar is still fighting the VA in court. "First they told me there were no records, which I knew was a lie, because I helped make them." | In January 2026, a judge noted he is eighty-nine.
140. Palomares has just over two thousand people today, as in 1966. Lettuces grow fifteen feet from the fence. | As one Spanish newspaper noted, nothing there explains why it's fenced.
141. The village mayor says it's "just a few plots." The neighbours ask for one thing: | "We only ask that they clean it up."
142. And beside the beach where the ambassador went swimming, sixteen hundred new homes have been approved.
143. Bomb number four came home. | The rest of it never left.
