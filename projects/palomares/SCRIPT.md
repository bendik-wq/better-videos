## COLD OPEN
1. Thirty-one thousand feet over Spain, an American B-52 slams into its own fuel tanker.
2. Seven men die, and four hydrogen bombs fall out of the fireball.
3. Three come down near a farming village called Palomares. Two of them crack open and dust the fields with plutonium.
4. The fourth is gone.
5. A fisherman saw a parachute go into the sea, but all he can point to is open water.
6. So the U.S. Navy bets dozens of ships and one tiny submarine on his memory.
7. They find it. | They get a line on it.
8. And they lose it again.
9. Sixty years later, the bomb is long gone. | The fence in Palomares is still up.
10. So how do you lose the same hydrogen bomb twice? | And why is there still plutonium under that fence?

## 1. FOUR FELL. THREE LANDED.
11. It's twenty past ten on a Monday morning, January the seventeenth, 1966. The sky over the Spanish coast is bright, clear blue.
12. Pilots call this patch of sky Saddle Rock. American bombers on airborne alert refuel here every day, with live weapons aboard.
13. In this bomber's belly: four Mark twenty-eight hydrogen bombs. | Each one more than seventy times the Hiroshima bomb.
14. The relief pilot eases up under the tanker's boom, a little fast. If it looks dangerous, the boom operator calls "breakaway." | "There was no call for breakaway," he said. | "But all of a sudden, all hell seemed to break loose."
15. The tanker's belly rips open. Jet fuel pours over the bomber, and a fireball swallows both planes.
16. That's the official finding: the bomber came in too fast and rammed the tanker. | Two of the survivors always insisted something exploded in the tail first.
17. The tanker has no ejection seats. All four men aboard die.
18. On the bomber, the commander ejects into the fire. "I knew I was going to lose my arm. But I thought it was better to lose that than lose everything."
19. The co-pilot falls out through a hole in the floor. The radar navigator's seat won't separate. He drags his parachute out by hand. | It opens just before he hits the ground.
20. Three men on the bomber don't make it. | Seven dead. Four alive.
21. Fishermen pull three survivors from the water. One rescue boat rams the dock, and the airmen ride up to the infirmary in bread trucks.
22. The driver keeps turning round to check on the co-pilot, Mike Rooney. Rooney yells at him to watch the road. | A plane crash, a boat wreck. One more, he figured, and it's "three strikes and I'm out."
23. On land, it's raining metal. A wing burns in a tomato field. Chaff hangs in the trees like silver tinsel. | Nobody on the ground is hurt.
24. Then they counted the bombs.
25. Bomb one lies in a dry riverbed, nearly intact. "Parece un torpedo," says the Spanish policeman who spots it. It looks like a torpedo.
26. Next morning, bomb two turns up behind the cemetery, in a crater twenty feet across. Bomb three is lying by the houses. A shopkeeper stamps out the fire beside it.
27. In both bombs, the explosive went off, at least in part. | So why is Palomares still there?
28. To set off the bomb, every charge around the plutonium core has to fire at the same instant, squeezing it inward. Hitting the ground sets the explosive off from one side. It blows the core apart instead.
29. No nuclear blast. | Just plutonium dust in the wind.
30. Three bombs in a day. | The rack held four.
31. Then a villager hands over a round metal plate: bomb four's tail cover. It only blows off when the parachutes deploy.
32. Bomb number four came down under a sixty-four-foot parachute. | Just not on land.

## 2. THE DEAD MAN IN THE PARACHUTE
33. Five miles out, a shrimp boat is raising its nets. The skipper is Francisco Simó Orts. He's thirty-eight.
34. He watches the fire fall, then the parachutes. Under a small one, half a man, "with his guts trailing."
35. Under a big one, a dead man, "hanging still and silent." It hits the sea hard, | and it's gone.
36. His nets are still down, so he can't get there. He fixes the spot instead: a mountain lined up behind an old chimney.
37. Days later, he meets an Air Force lawyer, and apologises. He's sorry he couldn't save the other flyer. | The lawyer stops him. | "What other flyer?" Every airman was already counted.
38. Officially, nobody was missing a bomb either. Spain wanted it kept quiet, and the Americans went along. Asked about the bomb, an Air Force spokesman replied: | "What bomb?"
39. Pressed, he went further: | "I can tell you that there is no sign of the thing that is not the bomb."
40. Day five. Simó guides a Navy minesweeper out with no compass, steering by the shore. The sonar picks up two echoes, two thousand feet down.
41. They pull away and ask him to find the spot again. | He does. Same two echoes.
42. The report calls them "either a school of fish or a parachute partially filled with air bubbles." Some officers believed him. Some didn't. | And all he could point at was water.
43. So did the Navy ignore him? | No. The admiral running the hunt, William Guest, drew his very first search area to take in the fishermen's sighting.
44. Guest later compared the job to combing the hills "at midnight on a moonless night."
45. His divers measured their distance from shore with rolls of toilet paper.
46. In early February, a bomb engineer from Sandia sat down with Simó and sketched what he'd seen. Simó took the pencil and redrew it. | Then it made sense. The half man was an empty parachute bag, trailing lines from a small chute. The dead man, hanging still, | was bomb four, swinging under its big canopy.
47. The engineer: "I was convinced absolutely that he had seen number four go into the sea."
48. A week later, Simó snagged something heavy in his nets and radioed the Navy. As Barbara Moran tells it, the message amounted to: I have your bomb. If you want it, come get it.
49. It was a four-thousand-pound concrete block, anchoring a Navy buoy.
50. Then came the submarine. Alvin: twenty-two feet long, room for three, and white. To one man, it looked like "a big white toilet."
51. Its pilots: Marvin McCamis, a high-school dropout and ex-submarine electrician, and his partner, Val Wilson.
52. In Washington, a Navy scientist, John Craven, sketched a map of where the bomb was most likely to be.
53. Out here, a twenty-seven-year-old mathematician, Tony Richardson, cranked a mechanical calculator from eleven at night till three, in a warship's accounting office. Square by square, one question: if the bomb is here, would we have seen it yet?
54. The admiral's verdict: "Where the hell did you get this high school kid?" | It was one of the first big real-world uses of probability-based search. | Remember that calculator.
55. And just outside the search zone, a Soviet spy ship sat watching. Once it came within twenty-five yards of an American destroyer. An officer aboard: "I was thinking, oh crap, there's gonna be a collision."
56. Day forty-three, the first of March. Val Wilson is at the controls when Alvin's camera catches something on the seabed: a furrow, "like a barrel had been dragged over the bottom."
57. Alvin goes back for it. | Nothing.

## 3. ONE MORE DAY
58. Next day, day forty-four, Washington finally admits that one weapon "has not yet been recovered." The Boston Globe: "One of our H-bombs is missing."
59. Spain had a tourist season to save. So the American ambassador, Angier Biddle Duke, went for a swim.
60. Day fifty. In trunks, loafers, a blue bathrobe and a bathing cap, Duke wades into the sea down the coast. Asked if he'd detected any radioactivity: | "If this is radioactivity, I love it!"
61. That afternoon he swims again, at Palomares's own beach, with Spain's information minister. According to the ambassador's aide, Duke invited himself along, in borrowed trunks.
62. Moscow's Izvestia said Duke deserved Britain's Order of the Bath. A State Department history later concluded the swim "probably saved the tourist season."
63. And the water was fine. The bomb out at sea was intact. | The plutonium was in the fields behind the beach.
64. The cleanup crews had been grabbed from American bases in a hurry. As the New York Times later reported, there were cooks, clerks, "and even musicians from the Air Force band."
65. One of them was an Air Force health technician named Victor Skaar. "Did we follow protocol? Hell, no. We had neither the time nor the equipment." | Keep an eye on that name.
66. Some ate box lunches with their legs dangling into a bomb crater. The Air Force fed them tomatoes from the contaminated fields: "Breakfast, lunch and dinner."
67. So how clean is clean? Geiger counters click, and the clicks per minute set the line. An early deal said seven thousand. The Pentagon wanted fifty thousand. America's atomic agency called a hundred thousand "perfectly satisfactory." | They settled on sixty thousand. More than eight times the first number.
68. Above the line, soil was scraped up and shipped to America. Below it, it was "plowed under and watered down."
69. Some of the dirt in between, not hot enough to ship, went into trenches near the bomb two crater. | Nobody kept a precise record of where.
70. Out at sea, the hunt was dying. It took twelve days and eight dives just to find that furrow again. A towed sonar snagged on the bottom | and never came up.
71. Day fifty-six. The ambassador braces for the search to be called off.
72. Day fifty-seven. Guest rules the area "searched to ninety-eight percent" and wants Alvin moved. A young officer, Brad Mooney, pushes back.
73. Guest: "All right, goddamn it. One more day, and that's all."
74. That morning, Simó is out on a Navy minesweeper with the mathematician. If the Navy gives up, he says, he'll rig hooks on his own nets and snag the chute himself.
75. Half a mile below, it's McCamis's son's birthday. He backs Alvin down a seventy-degree slope, to keep the furrow in his front window.
76. A grey, featureless bottom. Silt hanging in the lights. Then a parachute. | And under it, a fin.
77. "That's it! That's it!" | On the minesweeper, Simó's lunch is interrupted by the news.
78. The official record: "a parachute enshrouded object," contact two hundred and sixty-one, "in the vicinity of the place where Simó Orts reported seeing a weapon descend."
79. The calculator hadn't found it. | A fisherman's landmarks, a furrow and a stubborn pilot had.
80. To hold the spot, Alvin wedged into a crevice beside the bomb and switched off its lights. Eight hours in the dark. One illicit cigarette.
81. Topside, Guest asked: "How do you know it's not a parachute full of mud?" McCamis: "In all my life I'd never had my intelligence so insulted."
82. The bomb was perched on that slope, deep inside an undersea canyon. | Finding it was the easy part.

## 4. LOST AGAIN
83. The bomb weighed more than a ton. Alvin's arm could lift fifty pounds, at most. Within four days, the bomb had slid twenty feet downhill.
84. Then came Poodle, a steel frame "like a giant shuttlecock," with sonar beacons and a strobe light. It fell over and tangled, | and the bomb shifted again.
85. The ambassador had already drafted the press release: "The fourth and final weapon… has been recovered today."
86. On CBS, Charles Kuralt told America: "We live in a world in which it is possible to mislay a hydrogen bomb."
87. Two days later, the weather breaks for one day. | March the twenty-fourth.
88. Alvin twists a grappling hook into the parachute's lines. From the hook, a rope runs up to a heavy anchor. Above it, the steel frame. And from there, one nylon line climbs half a mile to the ship.
89. Guest doesn't want to lift on one line. His staff say it can hold ten times the weight of the bomb and its rig, and bad weather is coming back.
90. McCamis asks to stay down and watch. | The answer is no: too dangerous. Alvin is ordered to the surface.
91. So nobody is on the bottom. | Just that blinking strobe, half a mile down.
92. Around sunset, the winch on the ship Mizar starts to turn. Guest starts to pray.
93. Remember Guest's moonless night? | Tonight, there's almost no moon at all.
94. An hour in, the steel frame rises. Then the anchor. | Then the bomb lifts off the seabed.
95. Three minutes later, the instruments jump. The diving chief, Red Moody, and Jon Lindbergh, son of the famous flyer Charles Lindbergh, are watching the line. | It goes slack.
96. Lindbergh felt "a terrible sinking feeling." Moody's first thought, Barbara Moran writes, can't be repeated here.
97. It's about nine at night. | The bomb is gone again.
98. The ambassador's "recovered today" press release stays a draft.
99. It takes an hour to reel in the anchor. The line below it, the one tied to the bomb, ends in a frayed stump.
100. Lindbergh looked closely. Three-quarters of the strands were cut clean, as if on something sharp. The rest had just split.
101. The ship drifting? A rock? The anchor? The steel frame? A bad rope? | Nobody ever figured it out for sure.
102. At dinner, McCamis: "Oh, boy. Now we got to go find it again."
103. Two days after Kuralt's broadcast, | they had mislaid it.
104. That same day, a Navy ship sailed for America with barrels of Palomares soil. In all, fourteen hundred tons of soil and vegetation went to a nuclear site in South Carolina.
105. They could ship fourteen hundred tons of Spain across the Atlantic. | They couldn't hold on to one bomb.

## 5. WE'RE FOULED
106. Next morning, the seabed where the bomb had lain looked "torn up by bulldozers."
107. The submarine Aluminaut searched for five hours. It passed within a hundred feet of the old spot, several times, | and saw nothing.
108. Guest guessed the bomb had gone uphill. Alvin found a track up the slope and followed it. | A false lead.
109. Red Moody, who had helped design the rig, felt personally responsible.
110. Day sixty-nine. Day seventy-two. Day seventy-four. | Nothing.
111. A Pentagon board was weighing whether to give up. Its chairman said failure would mean "a congressional investigation, and mud all over the face of everyone."
112. A submarine pilot put it in baseball terms: "Here we were in the ninth inning, and the score is zero to zero."
113. The Alvin crew kept lobbying to search downhill. | Day seventy-five, they get their way. McCamis spots "a clod of dirt that seemed out of place."
114. Then the parachute, "still tightly wrapped around an object." | A hundred and twenty yards away, and two hundred and fifty feet deeper.
115. Red Moody: "How many bombs do we have down there? Let's just go get her, but do a better job this time."
116. This time, they brought a robot: Curve, a cable-controlled machine that had already fished up fifty-two torpedoes. Its patched-on extra cable "looked like a python had swallowed a dog."
117. The bomb even got a code name, after Guest's stepson. | Robert.
118. Guest wanted three lines before he'd lift. The robot's engineer warned the ballooning parachute could tangle. Guest: "I can't help that."
119. The robot hooked the chute. The line dragged the bomb deeper still, and the chute billowed, McCamis said, "like you was under a Barnum and Bailey circus tent."
120. Day eighty, one in the morning. The robot dives for the third line. The admiral asks for a closer look.
121. Then the robot's pilot: | "We're fouled."
122. The robot is caught in the parachute, | tangled up with the bomb it came to save.
123. Guest wants to cut its cable and come back later. The head of the robot team faints.
124. The room is cleared, and Brad Mooney, the officer who'd begged for one more day, argues with the admiral alone, for hours. "Admiral, this is dumb as hell to cut this thing loose. It's totally enmeshed in there."
125. His point: the trapped robot wasn't in the way. Wrapped in that parachute, it was holding the bomb. | It was the third line.
126. At two minutes past five, Guest sent the Air Force a coded message: the admiral "had a broken leg." | The lift was about to begin.
127. Before dawn, under a nearly full moon this time, two small boats hold the ship over the bomb on a flat calm sea.
128. It rises so smoothly, they hardly notice it leave the bottom.
129. An hour and forty-five minutes later, the parachute breaks the surface. Divers cut the robot free.
130. Eight forty-six in the morning. The ship's log: | "Weapon on deck with parachute."
131. Eighty days after the crash.
132. "Thank God we finally did it," Guest said. | Nobody cheered.
133. Simó got a scroll, and a medallion with the President's face on it. Back home, people started calling him Paco el de la Bomba. | Paco of the Bomb.
134. He reportedly sued for five million dollars.
135. In 1968, the Navy lost a submarine, the Scorpion. Richardson's probability map put the wreck within two hundred yards of its most likely square.
136. The calculator found something after all.
137. Bomb four sailed home. | But two bombs had cracked open on land, and not all of that dust got on a ship.

## 6. THE REST OF IT
138. Those trenches nobody mapped in 1966? | In 2007, ground radar swept the fields by the bomb two crater, and found the probable sites of two of them.
139. Forty years on. By 1969, the tomatoes kept failing, and Time magazine reported half the village had left. By the 1980s, diggers were cutting irrigation reservoirs into the bomb two valley, | the valley where nobody had recorded what was buried.
140. When Spain measured the soil again, the contamination was "higher than first estimated."
141. Spain took over the worst land, about a hundred acres, and fenced it off.
142. Underneath, by the official estimate: enough soil to fill twenty Olympic pools, | holding about a pound of plutonium.
143. In 2015, John Kerry signed a statement of intent with Spain, to resolve it "once and for all."
144. Its text, as reported, bound nobody. Talks followed. In 2024, American auditors reported "no final agreement has been reached." | None has been reported since.
145. Then Spain's own government argued the other way. In 2025, the state's lawyers told a court a cleanup "could be counterproductive."
146. In April 2026, according to a local press report of their brief, they went further. | "Palomares was never a nuclear accident."
147. Their case: people here get less than a tenth of a millisievert a year, under a tenth of the public limit, and digging could stir up radioactive material. In its 2013 report, Spain's nuclear regulator found no disease caused by the accident.
148. That case is still waiting for a ruling. In another one, a golf-resort company asked almost seventy-five euros a square metre for its contaminated hillside. Andalusia's high court backed the state's price: | sixty-eight cents.
149. And the men who shovelled the dirt? The New York Times reported they gave more than fifteen hundred urine samples, and the Air Force's chief of radiation testing threw out about a thousand.
150. The workers who buried the barrels in South Carolina were made eligible for federal radiation compensation in 2012. The airmen who dug up that soil waited ten more years for a law that covered them.
151. Victor Skaar, the health technician, is still fighting the VA in court. "First they told me there were no records," he said, "which I knew was a lie, because I helped make them." | In January 2026, a judge noted that he is eighty-nine.
152. Palomares has just over two thousand people today, about as many as in 1966. Lettuces grow fifteen feet from the fence. | And as one Spanish newspaper noted, nothing there explains why the land is fenced.
153. The village mayor says it's "just a few plots." The neighbours ask for one thing: | "We only ask that they clean it up."
154. And beside the beach where the ambassador went swimming, sixteen hundred new homes have been approved.
155. Bomb number four came home. | The rest of it never left.
