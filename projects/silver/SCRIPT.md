## COLD OPEN
1. In the winter of 1980, Americans started melting down their grandmothers' silver.
2. Wedding forks. | Baby spoons. | Coins from the jar on the dresser.
3. Burglars started stealing it.
4. Because a year earlier, an ounce of silver cost about six dollars. | Now it cost fifty.
5. And the reason, more than anything, was two brothers from Dallas.
6. Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt, sons of one of the richest oilmen who ever lived. With their partners, they controlled about a hundred and ninety-five million ounces of silver.
7. Six thousand tonnes. | On paper, it was worth almost ten billion dollars.
8. Ten weeks later, they couldn't pay their broker.
9. Silver lost almost a third of its value in a single day. A Wall Street firm's stock was halted. And past midnight in Florida, the country's biggest bankers met, with the chairman of the Federal Reserve in the building.
10. Forty-six years later, in January 2026, silver hit a hundred and twenty-one dollars. | The next day it fell twenty-seven percent. The worst day on record.
11. Both times, the same hidden lever was in play.
12. This is the story of the brothers who tried to buy a mountain of silver | and the machine that decided they couldn't keep it.

## 1. THE MAN WHO BOUGHT METAL
13. To understand how it happened, you have to meet the man in the middle of it.
14. In 1980, Bunker Hunt was fifty-four. Two hundred and twenty-five pounds. Cheap brown suits.
15. He drove himself to work in a 1973 Cadillac, and his phone number was listed in the Dallas directory.
16. One associate said he was the kind of guy who would order chicken-fried steak and Jell-O, spill some on his tie, | and then go out and buy all the silver in the world.
17. His father, H. L. Hunt, built one of America's great oil fortunes. A gambler who sometimes had fifty thousand dollars riding on a single afternoon of sports, | and who brought his lunch to work in a paper bag.
18. Bunker remembered one lesson from him.
19. In business, you should risk a little, with the potential to make a lot. | Not the other way around.
20. Remember that rule.
21. In 1961, Bunker found the Sarir field in Libya, one of the biggest single oil finds ever made.
22. In 1973, Libya nationalised it. Billions in future profits, gone by decree.
23. And Bunker feared something even more than foreign governments. | His own government's money.
24. In 1971, President Nixon had cut the last link between the dollar and gold. Prices were climbing. And for Americans, owning gold bullion was still illegal.
25. So the Hunts went for the next best thing.
26. "Silver looked safer than overseas oil concessions," Bunker said. "And precious metals were a good hedge against paper money."
27. In late 1973, he bought about twenty million ounces, at around three dollars. Herbert started buying too.
28. By 1974 they had about fifty million ounces. | And they did something almost nobody in that market does. | They took the metal.

## 2. THE MACHINE
29. Here's how the silver market really worked.
30. Most of the silver traded in New York and Chicago wasn't metal. It was paper. Futures contracts: promises to deliver five thousand ounces on a set date.
31. Most traders never see a bar. They close the contract, take the profit or loss in cash, and move on.
32. But every contract has a back door. Hold it to the end, and the seller has to hand over real silver.
33. And the exchange vaults only hold so much.
34. Now add leverage. You don't pay for a contract in full. You put down a deposit, called margin.
35. At the end of 1979, thirty thousand dollars of margin controlled more than a hundred and seventy thousand dollars of silver.
36. And every single day, the market settles up in cash. If the price rises, the losers pay the winners | that same day.
37. By New Year 1980, the Hunts held about nineteen thousand contracts. Every one-dollar rise in silver sent them roughly ninety-seven million dollars.
38. They used the cash to buy more. | The rally was paying for itself.
39. It's a beautiful machine, | as long as the price goes up.
40. Because when the price falls, the money flows the other way. You pay. In cash. Every day. That demand is called a margin call.
41. And the people who run the exchange decide how much margin you need.
42. Keep an eye on that switch.

## 3. FIFTY DOLLARS
43. In July 1979, the Hunts went bigger.
44. With two Saudi investors, they set up a company in Bermuda: the International Metals Investment Company.
45. At the end of July, the group held about a hundred and twenty-three million ounces. Silver was around nine dollars.
46. Then they kept buying. | And they kept asking for the metal.
47. By December 31st, silver was thirty-four dollars and forty-five cents.
48. The group now held about a hundred and ninety-five million ounces.
49. Melt it into one block, and you'd get a cube of solid silver more than eight metres on each side. Taller than a three-storey house.
50. Government investigators later put it at about fifteen percent of all the silver in the world. | And their futures alone added up to about three-quarters of the silver in the exchange warehouses.
51. On January 17th and 18th, 1980, silver hit about fifty dollars an ounce.
52. The group's position was worth more than nine point eight billion dollars.
53. By then, they'd already pulled one point four billion dollars of profit out as cash.
54. Everyone else paid for it.
55. Everything made with silver got more expensive, from baby spoons to photographic film.
56. And people opened their drawers. The head of the futures regulator told Congress, "We began to see, for example, people turning in their home silverware."
57. Tiffany and Company took out a newspaper ad: "We think it is unconscionable for anyone to hoard several billion, yes billion, dollars worth of silver."
58. A family from Dallas had pushed a metal so high that the country was melting its heirlooms.
59. It looked like they had won.
60. But they had broken their father's rule. | They were risking a lot.
61. And much of it was bought with borrowed money.

## 4. THE RULES CHANGE
62. The first warning came in September 1979. The exchanges raised margins: from two thousand dollars a contract to twenty thousand, within weeks.
63. In October, the Federal Reserve, under Paul Volcker, asked banks to stop making speculative loans.
64. Then, on Monday, January 7th, 1980, New York's Commodity Exchange passed an emergency rule. | Silver Rule 7.
65. No trader could hold more than two thousand contracts. Ten million ounces. And it applied to positions they already had.
66. The next afternoon, Bunker walked into the federal regulator's office and asked a blunt question. | Did they want silver prices to go up, | or down? | And were they planning to shut the market?
67. Two weeks later, the exchange effectively did.
68. On January 21st, the exchange went to "liquidation only." No new positions. You could close out. | You couldn't buy.
69. In a market where the biggest buyer had just been told to stop buying, there was only one way to go.
70. The next day, silver fell about ten dollars.
71. The Hunts said the game was rigged. Some exchange governors traded silver themselves, and the Hunts claimed some were on the other side. A later federal review found no evidence that proved a conflict of interest.
72. Either way, the price kept falling. And the machine that had paid them every day | started billing them every day.
73. In February, margin on a position the size of theirs hit sixty thousand dollars a contract. Thirty times last summer's level.
74. In March, President Carter and the Fed told lenders to avoid financing "purely speculative holdings" of precious metals.
75. Barclays said no. Deutsche Bank said no. The big Canadian banks said no.
76. And there was a deadline.
77. In January, the Hunts had agreed to buy twenty-eight and a half million more ounces from the metals giant Engelhard, at about thirty-six dollars. Four hundred and thirty-four million dollars was due on Monday, March 31st.
78. By late March, silver was down to twenty.

## 5. SILVER THURSDAY
79. Tuesday, March 25th. Silver closes at twenty dollars and twenty cents.
80. That evening, Herbert Hunt tells the chairman of Bache, their main broker: we have no more cash. | And no more collateral.
81. Bache has been paying the exchange about twenty-two million dollars a day to cover the Hunts. By the next morning, it's advanced more than a hundred and fifty million.
82. Wednesday, March 26th. 12:15 p.m. Bache's chairman, Harry Jacobs, picks up the phone | and calls Paul Volcker.
83. That afternoon, the exchange's board meets in an emergency session | and cuts the margin requirement, from sixty thousand dollars to forty. It frees about eighty million dollars for Bache.
84. The rules had changed again. | This time, to save the broker.
85. Silver closes at fifteen eighty.
86. The Hunts announce a plan to sell bonds in Europe, backed by their silver. And they tell their brokers they're stopping margin payments.
87. Thursday, March 27th, 1980.
88. There is no buyer big enough to stop the fall.
89. At 2:13 p.m., the New York Stock Exchange halts trading in Bache's stock. Two minutes later, silver futures close.
90. Bache's unsecured loss on the Hunt accounts: a hundred and twenty-two million dollars. Enough to wipe out eighty-four percent of its parent company's net worth.
91. Silver settles at ten dollars and eighty cents. | Down almost a third in a single day. | Down almost eighty percent from January.
92. On paper, the Hunts lose about a billion dollars that day.
93. The futures regulator declines to step in. "We are not here," its chairman says, "to afford protection to large speculators."
94. Now the deadline. | Four hundred and thirty-four million, to Engelhard, due Monday.
95. Sunday, March 30th. Boca Raton, Florida. A convention of the country's biggest bankers, | and the chairman of the Federal Reserve is in the building.
96. Well after midnight, the banks say no.
97. So the Hunts pay Engelhard in kind: eight and a half million ounces of silver, plus a fifth of their oil rights in the Arctic.
98. Weeks later, thirteen banks lend the family's oil company, Placid Oil, one point one billion dollars to refinance the silver debts.
99. In today's money, that's more than four and a half billion.
100. In Congress, critics compared it to lending money to casinos. Volcker agreed with half of that. | "This was gambling," he said. | But "there is no more money involved here. This is a restructuring."
101. For the loan, the banks valued the Hunts' silver at fourteen dollars and five cents an ounce.
102. In January, the same pile had been worth almost ten billion dollars.

## 6. UNDER OATH
103. On May 2nd, 1980, after being threatened with contempt of Congress, the brothers testified under oath.
104. Herbert: "At no time did I attempt to corner, squeeze, or manipulate the silver market."
105. Bunker said they were investors, not speculators. | "You don't have a loss," he said, "until you sell something."
106. Asked how closely the brothers worked together, Bunker said: "I think the problem is that we talk too much to each other | and not enough to others."
107. There was one more story. A former senior aide swore he'd heard the Hunts had flown silver to Switzerland, with ranch hands from Texas riding shotgun.
108. He admitted he never saw the planes. | The Hunts swore it never happened.
109. Eight years later, a jury in New York weighed it all. Peru's state minerals company had bet against silver in 1980, lost, and sued.
110. In August 1988, the jury found the Hunt brothers, a Saudi partner and the Bermuda company liable for conspiring to manipulate the silver market. A hundred and thirty-four million dollars, after earlier settlements.
111. It was a civil case. Nobody went to prison.
112. A month later, Bunker and Herbert filed for bankruptcy.
113. The filing listed Hawaiian real estate, two Cadillacs, a Mercedes, | and a collection of thirteen thousand, six hundred and forty-nine Byzantine coins.
114. As one obituary later put it, they ended up "hocking everything: horses, coins, land and lawn mowers."
115. In 1989 they settled with the regulator: a ten-million-dollar penalty, and a lifetime ban from commodities trading.
116. Their brother Lamar, the one who named the Super Bowl after his kids' toy, the Super Ball, played a smaller part. | He never filed for bankruptcy.
117. Bunker Hunt died in 2014. He was eighty-eight.
118. His father's rule was to risk a little to make a lot. | His sons risked everything, | to make a little more.

## 7. THE SWITCH
119. Silver didn't get back near fifty dollars for thirty-one years.
120. In April 2011, it touched forty-nine dollars and eighty-four cents. The exchange raised margins five times in a few weeks. | Silver fell by more than a quarter.
121. In 2021, an army of online traders tried to squeeze silver. The exchange raised margin almost eighteen percent, and the squeeze fizzled.
122. Then, in October 2025, silver finally broke fifty dollars. | And kept going.
123. On Thursday, January 29th, 2026, it hit a record of about a hundred and twenty-one dollars.
124. The day before, the exchange had raised margin.
125. On Friday, President Trump named Kevin Warsh as his pick to run the Fed, the dollar jumped, and silver fell twenty-seven percent. The worst day on record.
126. Then the exchange raised margins again.
127. This time there was no Hunt. No single family holding the metal. Just a crowded trade, | borrowed money, | and the same lever.
128. Adjust for inflation, and the 1980 peak was more than two hundred dollars in today's money. | Even at its record, silver in 2026 never came close.
129. That's the hidden machinery of every market built on borrowed money. | Buyers and sellers set the price. | But whoever sets the margin decides who gets to stay at the table.
130. In 1980, that lever was pulled against the biggest buyer in the world. | Then it was pushed the other way, to save a broker.
131. And somewhere in a vault today, there's a bar of silver | that used to be someone's grandmother's fork.
