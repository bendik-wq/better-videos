## COLD OPEN (0:00–1:15)
1. Before this year, around a hundred ships a day sailed through one gap of water between Iran and Oman.
2. Together they carried oil equal to a fifth of everything the world burns.
3. At its narrowest, the gap is about twenty-one nautical miles. The lanes the tankers actually use are narrower still. Two nautical miles in. Two nautical miles out. [↺]
4. On the twenty-eighth of February, twenty twenty-six, the ships stopped.
5. Nobody built a wall across the water. Nobody sank a fleet in the channel. The strait stayed open.
6. And yet, within weeks, the oil flowing through it fell by more than three quarters. [↺]
7. So what actually closed it? **(opens L1)**
8. To answer that, you need to know why this one strait matters more than any other place on the map. And why the country that guards it most barely uses it. **(opens L2)**

**TITLE: THE 21 MILES THAT CONTROL THE WORLD**

## 1. THE GAP (1:15–4:00)
9. Start with the shape of the water.
10. The Persian Gulf is a long, shallow sea, closed on almost every side. Kuwait, Iraq, Bahrain, Qatar. Most of Saudi Arabia's oil. The Emirates. Iran. All of it sits around one body of water.
11. And that water has exactly one way out to the open ocean. [↺]
12. The Strait of Hormuz.
13. At its narrowest, between Iran's Larak Island and Oman's Great Quoin, it is about twenty-one nautical miles across. Under forty kilometres.
14. Iran and Oman each claim twelve nautical miles of sea from their coasts. Twelve plus twelve is more than twenty-one. So at its tightest point, there is no open sea here at all. Every metre of it belongs to somebody. [↺]
15. In nineteen sixty-eight, the International Maritime Organization drew the lanes ships still follow. Two nautical miles inbound. A two-mile buffer. Two nautical miles outbound.
16. And here is the detail almost nobody knows. At the narrowest point, those lanes don't run through Iran's waters. They run through Oman's. [↺]
17. Remember that. It matters later.
18. In twenty twenty-four, about twenty million barrels of oil a day moved through this water. That's equal to roughly a fifth of all the oil the world consumes, and more than a quarter of all the oil shipped by sea.
19. It's not only oil. About a fifth of the world's liquefied natural gas came through here too, most of it from one country. Qatar. [↺]
20. Now look at where it goes. Eighty-four percent of the crude went to Asia. China, India, Japan and South Korea alone took more than two thirds.
21. And the United States? About seven percent of its crude imports.
22. Yet for forty years, the United States Navy has treated this strait as if its own economy depended on it. **(deepens L2)** [↺]
23. Is there another way out? A little. Saudi Arabia built a pipeline across the desert to the Red Sea. The Emirates built one to a port beyond the strait.
24. The U.S. Energy Information Administration estimated that together they could carry about two point six million extra barrels a day in a crisis. Against twenty.
25. In the agency's words, most of the oil that moves through the strait "has no alternative means of exiting the region." [↺]

## 2. THE TANKER WAR (4:00–7:00)
26. The last time someone tried to choke this water, it lasted four years.
27. In the nineteen eighties, Iran and Iraq were at war. In nineteen eighty-four, the war moved to the sea. Each side began attacking the other's oil trade, and anyone carrying it.
28. Historians call it the Tanker War. By the end of nineteen eighty-seven, more than four hundred and fifty ships had been attacked. [↺]
29. Here's what surprises people. Most of those attacks weren't by Iran. By the published tallies, Iraq hit about three ships in every five.
30. In May nineteen eighty-seven, an Iraqi jet fired two missiles into an American frigate, the U.S.S. Stark. Thirty-seven sailors died. Iraq said the pilot had mistaken it for a tanker. [↺]
31. That same year, Kuwait asked for protection. America answered by putting Kuwaiti tankers under the American flag and escorting them with warships.
32. On the very first convoy, the lead tanker, the Bridgeton, struck a mine.
33. The next spring, the U.S.S. Samuel B. Roberts hit another. The Navy's own history says the ship "should have sunk." Its crew kept it afloat. [↺]
34. Four days later, the U.S. Navy struck back. Operation Praying Mantis. The Navy calls it the largest of its major surface battles since the Second World War.
35. And then, in July nineteen eighty-eight, came the moment nobody involved has ever forgotten.
36. The cruiser U.S.S. Vincennes, fighting Iranian boats in the strait, fired two missiles at an aircraft it believed was an attacking fighter jet. [↺]
37. It was Iran Air Flight six fifty-five. A passenger plane. All two hundred and ninety people on board were killed, sixty-six of them children.
38. The United States called it a tragic mistake. Iran has never accepted that. Eight years later, America paid compensation to the families, without admitting legal liability.
39. Weeks after the shootdown, the war ended. But the lesson stayed. [↺]
40. You don't close a strait by blocking it. You close it by making it frightening. **(foreshadows L1)**

## 3. THE LEVER (7:00–9:15)
41. To understand the next forty years, you need one document.
42. In nineteen eighty-two, Iran signed the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. That's the treaty that gives every ship the right to pass through straits like this one. [↺]
43. But Iran never ratified it. And when it signed, it attached a declaration saying that right belongs only to countries that join the treaty.
44. The United States never joined either.
45. So both sides of today's standoff stand outside the treaty meant to govern this water. [↺]
46. For decades, that gap was used as a lever. Pulled, then released.
47. In June twenty nineteen, Iran shot down an American surveillance drone. The U.S. said it was over international waters. Iran said it was over Iran.
48. A month later, Iran's Revolutionary Guard seized a British tanker, the Stena Impero, with twenty-three crew on board. [↺]
49. In twenty twenty-three, a dozen fast attack boats swarmed a tanker inside the strait and took it. The next year, commandos rappelled from a helicopter onto a container ship.
50. And in June twenty twenty-five, after American strikes on its nuclear sites, Iran's parliament voted to back closing the strait. [↺]
51. The final decision lay with Iran's security council. The ships kept moving. Oil barely flinched.
52. So, until this year, the lever was always pulled halfway.
53. One more number to remember. Before this year, insuring a tanker against war for a single trip through the strait cost about a tenth to a quarter of one percent of the ship's value. **(plants the L1 payoff)** [↺]

## 4. THE CLOSING (9:15–12:15)
54. On the twenty-eighth of February, twenty twenty-six, the United States and Israel struck Iran. Iran's Supreme Leader was killed in the strikes.
55. Iran's Revolutionary Guard warned ships away from the strait. Then it began attacking them. [↺]
56. On the first of March, the first seafarers died, on tankers hit off the coast of Oman.
57. Days later, Qatar stopped producing liquefied natural gas. An attack later knocked out seventeen percent of its export capacity, for what Qatar says could be up to five years.
58. And here is the answer to our first question. [↺]
59. The U.S. Energy Information Administration put it plainly. The strait "is not physically blocked." The threat of attack, and the cancellation of insurance, made most tankers stay away. **(closes L1)**
60. Remember that insurance number? A tenth to a quarter of one percent. By the summer, war cover for one transit was quoted at between three and a half and seven and a half percent of the ship's value. [↺]
61. For a tanker worth a hundred million dollars, that's up to seven and a half million dollars. For one trip.
62. So the oil stopped. Flows through the strait fell from about twenty-one and a half million barrels a day before the war to under five million. A fall of more than three quarters.
63. Thirty-two countries released four hundred million barrels from emergency stocks. The largest release in the history of the International Energy Agency. [↺]
64. It sounds enormous. It's about twenty days of what normally sails through Hormuz.
65. The official forecasters expected a short disruption. In March, the E.I.A. assumed it would last about a month.
66. It didn't. Around twenty thousand seafarers were trapped on ships inside the Gulf. The head of the International Maritime Organization pleaded: "Seafarers must not be targets." [↺]
67. Then Iran did something revealing. It set up its own authority to approve ships and collect fees. And it redrew the shipping lanes. Out of Oman's waters. Into its own. **(callback to line 16)**
68. In April, America answered with a naval blockade of Iran's ports. In June, a deal. Oil fell back toward seventy dollars. [↺]
69. In July, the attacks resumed, and the blockade returned.
70. In September, drones hit the escape route itself. Saudi Arabia's pipeline across the desert. Who sent them is disputed.
71. Brent oil touched a hundred and thirty-one dollars a barrel. The worst price of the war didn't come in the first week. It came six months in, when the way around was hit. [↺]
72. Through all of it, one trade never stopped. Every morning, smugglers' speedboats still raced across to Oman's port of Khasab. Washing machines one way. Pistachios and sheep the other.

## 5. WHO PAYS (12:15–13:35)
73. So who pays? **(opens and closes L3)**
74. First, Asia, where the oil was going. Then everyone else, because oil has one world price.
75. In September, Americans paid an average of four dollars and thirty-five cents for a gallon of gasoline, and over six dollars for diesel. [↺]
76. It reaches further than fuel. Qatar produces around a third of the world's helium, alongside its gas. Helium cools M.R.I. scanners. It's used to make computer chips.
77. And the answer to our second question? America guards a strait it barely uses because oil has one price. Close this water, and every pump on earth feels it. **(closes L2)** [↺]
78. As of today, the strait is largely shut, with traffic on Iran's terms. Both sides claim control. The ship counts say neither fully has it.
79. For fifty years, the world built its economy on one assumption. That this gap would always be open.
80. It was never the water that kept it open. It was everyone's belief that it would stay that way. [↺]
