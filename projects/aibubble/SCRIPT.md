# SCRIPT: How the AI Bubble Pops (episode 8)

One numbered line per VO beat (project.mjs reads them; per-line ElevenLabs cache). `|` is a spoken beat.
Facts: RESEARCH.md. "Do not claim" list (§14) respected: no "terminated", no "crash", Oracle "one notch above junk",

## COLD OPEN
1. On August 17th, the most valuable company on Earth made a promise, in a filing almost nobody read.
2. Nvidia promised that if OpenAI stops paying the rent on a giant data center in Ohio, Nvidia will cover the shortfall. | Up to a hundred and five billion dollars.
3. And what's going inside that data center? | Nvidia chips.
4. Nvidia sells OpenAI the chips. Nvidia owns a piece of OpenAI. And now, Nvidia is guaranteeing OpenAI's rent.
5. Its customer. | Its investment. | Its risk.
6. Twenty-six years ago, another company ran a play like this. It lent its own customers billions of dollars, so they could buy its equipment.
7. It was worth around two hundred and fifty billion dollars. | Two years later, its stock was worth fifty-eight cents.
8. Last week, the machine behind the AI boom started to shake. Oracle fell almost nine percent in a day. CoreWeave, fifteen. South Korea's stock market tripped a circuit breaker.
9. And if you own an S&P 500 index fund, about thirty-five cents of every dollar sits in just seven companies.
10. This is the money machine inside the AI boom. How it works, who's holding the risk, | and exactly how it could pop.

## 1. THE BIGGEST BUILD IN HISTORY
11. Start with the money going out.
12. In 2024, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta spent two hundred and twenty-eight billion dollars on capital spending. Mostly data centers and chips.
13. In 2025, three hundred and seventy-six billion.
14. In just the first half of 2026, two hundred and ninety-five billion.
15. In today's money, the entire Apollo program cost around three hundred and thirty-five billion dollars. These four companies now spend close to that every six months.
16. A single gigawatt of data centers can draw as much power as about eight hundred thousand American homes. OpenAI has talked about building thirty.
17. To pay for it, even the richest companies are borrowing. Alphabet sold a bond that won't be paid back until the year twenty-one twenty-six.
18. Meta is building a twenty-seven-billion-dollar data center in Louisiana through a separate company, funded with bonds, so the debt doesn't sit on Meta's own balance sheet.
19. All of it rests on one assumption: | that someone, eventually, pays for AI.

## 2. THE CIRCLE
20. So who's paying? | Follow the money.
21. In September 2025, Nvidia announced it would invest up to a hundred billion dollars in OpenAI. Money that would be spent on Nvidia systems.
22. That deal never became binding. Nvidia's annual report warned there was "no assurance" it would happen, and it was replaced with a thirty-billion-dollar stake.
23. Then came the guarantee.
24. OpenAI has reportedly signed more than three hundred billion dollars of cloud contracts with Oracle. Oracle borrows and spends tens of billions a quarter on servers to deliver them.
25. AMD agreed to sell OpenAI six gigawatts of chips, and handed OpenAI the right to buy a hundred and sixty million AMD shares | for one cent each.
26. Amazon is investing fifty billion dollars in OpenAI. OpenAI expanded what it will spend on Amazon's cloud by a hundred billion.
27. Microsoft has put about twelve billion dollars into OpenAI. Last year, it booked twenty-four billion dollars of revenue from OpenAI.
28. And when Nvidia invested in CoreWeave, a cloud company that runs on Nvidia chips, it also promised to buy any capacity CoreWeave can't sell. | The supplier became the customer of last resort.
29. Money goes out one door as an investment, | and comes back through another as revenue.
30. None of this is illegal. Some of it makes real business sense. But it means the same dollars can show up as investment, as sales, and as profit, at several companies at once.
31. Last quarter, Alphabet reported a hundred and twelve billion dollars of profit, on a hundred and twenty billion of revenue. Ninety-nine billion of it came from paper gains on its stakes in private companies.
32. Look at it from above, and it isn't a pipeline. | It's a circle.

## 3. THE GAP
33. A circle can spin for a long time. | As long as real money keeps coming in from outside.
34. So how much is coming in?
35. OpenAI sits at the center. At the peak, Sam Altman talked about one point four trillion dollars of computing commitments.
36. In 2025, OpenAI's revenue was reportedly about thirteen billion dollars.
37. That's commitments more than a hundred times its yearly sales.
38. OpenAI has since scaled the plan back, to around six hundred billion dollars by 2030. Its revenue is now running at about fifty billion a year.
39. That's still twelve dollars of planned spending for every dollar coming in.
40. Last week, the research firm Gartner put a number on the whole gap. To justify the spending, AI would need around eight and a half trillion dollars of revenue by 2030. Gartner's forecast is at most three point six.
41. One of its analysts put it simply: "At the moment, we have more compute capacity than we have demand."
42. The pressure lands on the companies that borrowed to build.
43. Oracle's own filing values its hundred and twenty-five billion dollars of debt at about eighty-five cents on the dollar. Last quarter, its free cash flow was negative five point four billion. S&P rates it one notch above junk.
44. In September 2025, the day Oracle's stock jumped thirty-six percent, its founder Larry Ellison briefly became the richest person on Earth. | The stock has since lost about sixty percent.

## 4. THE LAST TIME
45. We have seen this machine before.
46. In the late 1990s, the internet needed cables, switches and routers, and telecom companies borrowed to build them.
47. Lucent, the old equipment arm of AT&T, sold them the gear. And when its customers ran short of money, Lucent lent it to them.
48. By September 2000, Lucent had committed eight point one billion dollars of financing to its own customers. Nortel, more than five billion. Across the industry, suppliers lent around twenty-five billion.
49. It looked like booming demand. | Some of it was the suppliers' own money, coming back as sales.
50. Then the customers couldn't borrow any more. Small carriers went bankrupt, and Lucent admitted the downturn had hit the creditworthiness of customers in its own financing program.
51. Lucent's stock had peaked around eighty-four dollars. By October 2002, it was fifty-eight cents.
52. Nortel, once worth about a third of Canada's entire stock market, eventually went bankrupt.
53. Cisco, briefly the most valuable company in the world, fell eighty-nine percent. It took more than twenty-five years to get back.
54. The Nasdaq lost seventy-eight percent, and took fifteen years to recover.
55. And here's the twist. | The internet was real. The cables they laid ended up carrying everything we do online today.
56. The technology survived. | The investors didn't.

## 5. THE CRACKS
57. Now, look at last week.
58. On October 6th, the Nasdaq and Nvidia closed at record highs. Nvidia was worth about five point eight trillion dollars, more than Germany produces in a year.
59. On October 14th, inflation came in hot, at three point seven percent, and the interest rate on ten-year government bonds pushed above five percent.
60. Higher rates hit the most borrowed companies first. That day, Oracle fell almost nine percent. CoreWeave fell fifteen.
61. The next day, South Korea's stock market, home to the world's memory chip giants, fell seven point six percent and tripped a circuit breaker.
62. The cost of insuring CoreWeave's debt against default hit records. Its bonds now yield around fifteen percent.
63. By Friday, Nvidia had lost around six hundred billion dollars of value in under two weeks. The Nasdaq was about five percent off its high.
64. That's not a crash. | It's a crack.
65. And cracks show you where the weight is.

## 6. HOW IT POPS
66. Here's how a bubble like this could pop. Not a prediction. | A chain.
67. It starts with the price of money. Higher rates make borrowing to build data centers more expensive.
68. The weakest borrowers go first: the AI cloud companies. Nvidia's own filing says they "currently lack the ability to secure long-term infrastructure contracts and investment-grade financing."
69. If they can't borrow, they can't buy chips. If their customers slow down, they can't pay their debts.
70. Next in line are the builders, like Oracle, carrying hundreds of billions of dollars in lease commitments.
71. Then the center of the circle: OpenAI. Its contracts are the revenue everyone else is counting on.
72. And behind OpenAI, the guarantees. Nvidia's promise in Ohio ends early only if OpenAI earns a "satisfactory credit rating." | It doesn't have one.
73. And finally, you. Nvidia alone is about eight percent of the S&P 500. If you have a retirement fund, you are almost certainly inside the circle.
74. Now the other side, because it matters.
75. This is not 2000 in one big way. The biggest players are hugely profitable. Nvidia made almost sixty billion dollars of profit in a single quarter. Its shares trade at about twenty-seven times its earnings. Cisco's traded at well over a hundred.
76. And the demand is real. Microsoft's cloud business grew twenty-seven percent last year. Anthropic reportedly went from about four and a half billion dollars of revenue in all of 2025, to nearly seventeen billion in a single quarter.
77. Nvidia's Jensen Huang: "There's been a lot of talk about an AI bubble. From our vantage point, we see something very different."
78. And yet even Sam Altman, asked whether investors were overexcited about AI, said: "My opinion is yes."
79. Both can be true. | AI can change the world, | and still be a bubble.

## 7. THE TEST
80. So watch three things.
81. First, the earnings over the next few weeks. If Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon or Meta cut their spending even slightly, the money flowing into the circle slows.
82. Second, the credit markets. What it costs Oracle and CoreWeave to borrow is the pressure on the weakest link.
83. Third, OpenAI and Anthropic. When they go public, their books open to everyone, and we'll see what the center of the circle really earns.
84. In 2000, the internet was real. | The money machine around it wasn't.
85. Twenty-six years later, the question isn't whether AI is real. | It's whether the money is.
