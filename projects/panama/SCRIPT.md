**TITLE: THE CANAL THAT RUNS ON RAIN**

## 1. THE MACHINE (1:45–6:15)
14. First, let's clear something up. Because almost everyone pictures the Panama Canal wrong.
15. They imagine a ditch. A trench cut from one ocean to the other, with seawater sloshing through. [↺]
16. That's not what's here. Panama is a mountain range with a coast on each side. The canal doesn't go *through* the mountains. | It goes *over* them.
17. Here's how. Picture a ship arriving from the Atlantic. In front of it is a concrete box a thousand feet long and a hundred and ten feet wide. The lock.
18. The ship sails in. The gates swing shut behind it. | And then, with no pumps at all, water starts pouring in from below. [↺]
19. It comes through tunnels eighteen feet wide, running through the walls, pushed by nothing but gravity, flowing down from the lake above. A hundred holes in the floor of each chamber. A ship the size of a skyscraper on its side, | rising like a toy in a bathtub.
20. Three steps like this, and the ship is eighty-five feet above the sea. [↺]
21. The gates holding all that water back are monsters. In the original locks, each gate leaf is sixty-four feet wide, seven feet thick, and up to eighty-two feet tall. They're hollow, so they partly float.
22. And guiding the ships along the walls are little electric locomotives. The canal calls them mules. A hundred of them, on rails beside the chambers, holding the ships steady so they don't scrape the concrete. [↺]
23. The cheapest toll in history? Thirty-six cents. In nineteen twenty-eight, an American adventurer named Richard Halliburton swam the entire canal, and the canal charged him by his body weight. [↺]
24. And then the gates in front of it open | onto a lake.
25. Gatun Lake. A hundred and sixty-four square miles of fresh water, sitting on top of the isthmus, with jungle islands poking out of it. The ship sails across for hours.
26. Then, on the Pacific side, it does the whole thing in reverse. | Step down. Step down. Step down. Back to the ocean. [↺]
27. Now here's the part nobody thinks about.
28. Every time a chamber drains to lower a ship, that water doesn't go back up. There's no pump. There never was. | It flows out into the sea.
29. For a single crossing through the original locks, that's about fifty million gallons. [↺]
30. Let's put that in a pool. An Olympic swimming pool holds about six hundred and sixty thousand gallons. | One ship crossing Panama drains *seventy-six* of them.
31. Into the ocean. | Fresh water. | Gone.
32. On a normal day, around thirty-six ships cross. Do the math, and the canal can empty more than *two thousand* Olympic pools a day. [↺]
33. And the newer, bigger locks, the ones built in twenty sixteen, are smarter. They have side basins that catch and reuse sixty percent of the water. But they're so much bigger that each crossing still uses about the same.
34. So the whole system is a giant hourglass. Water goes in at the top, ships ride it down, and it runs out the bottom.
35. And the only thing that turns the hourglass over | is rain. [↺]
36. Luckily, it rains a lot here. On average, the canal's watershed gets over a hundred inches a year. Most of it falls between May and December. Then the dry season comes, January to April, and the lake slowly drains.
37. That's the rhythm. Fill in the wet season. Spend in the dry season. Repeat. For more than a century.
38. And the canal has a second bank account. Upriver, behind another dam built in the nineteen-thirties, sits a smaller, higher reservoir called Lake Alhajuela. In the dry season, the canal lets its water run down the river into Gatun to top it up.
39. Two lakes. One river system. One rainfall. [↺]
40. But there's one more thing drinking from this lake. [↺]
41. The same water that lifts the ships comes out of the taps in Panama City. The canal's lakes supply water to more than half of the country's population.
42. Two million people and fourteen thousand ships a year, drinking from the same bathtub.
43. Which raises an obvious question. | Why on earth would anyone build a canal like this? [↺]

## 2. WHY A LAKE? (6:15–11:15)
44. The first people to try didn't.
45. In eighteen eighty-one, a French company led by Ferdinand de Lesseps, the man who had built the Suez Canal, started digging in Panama. [↺]
46. Suez had been easy, in one way. Flat desert, sea to sea, no locks. So de Lesseps planned the same thing here: a sea-level canal. A giant trench straight through the mountains.
47. It was a catastrophe.
48. The mountains kept sliding back into the cut. The rivers flooded it. And yellow fever and malaria tore through the workers. [↺]
49. By early eighteen eighty-nine, the company was bankrupt. More than two hundred and sixty million dollars was gone. | And by most estimates, around twenty thousand people had died.
50. When the Americans took over in nineteen-oh-four, they had to answer the same question all over again. Sea level, or locks? [↺]
51. The international board of engineers voted for sea level. Eight to five.
52. But the five American engineers in the minority argued for something else. Don't cut through the mountains. | Drown the valley instead.
53. Here's the idea. A river called the Chagres runs through the middle of Panama. It was the problem. Every rainy season, it flooded everything. [↺]
54. So, they said, build a dam across it. Turn the river into a lake. Then lift the ships up to the lake with locks, and let them sail across.
55. The river stops being the enemy. | It becomes the fuel.
56. In June nineteen-oh-six, the US Senate voted for the lock canal. Thirty-six to thirty-one. [↺]
57. And so they built the Gatun Dam. When it was finished, it was the largest earthen dam in the world, and the lake behind it was the largest man-made lake in the world.
58. And as the water rose | the valley disappeared. [↺]
59. Whole villages along the river, Gorgona, Matachín, Gamboa, were drowned or abandoned. Their people were moved to new towns on higher ground. The old Panama Railroad was rebuilt, forty miles of it, because its route was going under.
60. Hilltops became islands. One of them, Barro Colorado, is still there today, a scientific research station in the middle of the lake. [↺]
61. But one obstacle was left. Between the lake and the Pacific side stood the Continental Divide, and through it, the hardest piece of the whole canal. | The Culebra Cut.
62. Nine miles of man-made canyon, dug through a mountain that kept trying to fill it back in. Landslides moved millions of cubic yards of earth back into the cut. One of them crept forward fourteen feet a day. [↺]
63. At the peak, more than forty-four thousand people were working on the canal. | More than five thousand of them died during the American years.
64. And on the tenth of October, nineteen thirteen, there was one thing left between the lake and the cut. | An earth dike at Gamboa.
65. In Washington, just after two in the afternoon, President Woodrow Wilson sent a signal. [↺]
66. It ran by telegraph down to Texas, then under the sea by cable, then overland to Panama.
67. > And four thousand miles from the White House | the dike blew.
68. The lake poured into the cut. For the first time, there was water all the way across Panama.
69. Ten months later, on the fifteenth of August, nineteen fourteen, a ship called the Ancon made the first official crossing. About nine hours, ocean to ocean. [↺]
70. And that's the answer to our first question. **(closes L1)**
71. The canal runs on rain because rain was the only thing powerful enough to beat the mountains.
72. The engineers made a trade. They didn't have to cut through the highest ground in Panama. | But from that day on, the canal would depend on the sky. [↺]

## 3. BIGGER SHIPS, THIRSTIER CANAL (11:15–15:30)
73. For most of the twentieth century, the trade worked.
74. The United States ran the canal until nineteen ninety-nine. Then, at noon on the last day of the millennium, it handed it to Panama. [↺]
75. Panama turned it into a money machine. Today, around thirteen to fourteen thousand ships cross every year. In its last fiscal year, the canal took in about five point seven billion dollars. | And nearly three billion of that went straight to Panama's treasury.
76. More than half of every dollar the canal earns ends up funding the country. [↺]
77. But ships kept getting bigger. By the two thousands, a huge share of the world's fleet simply didn't fit in the old locks.
78. So Panama built new ones. In twenty sixteen, a ship called the Cosco Shipping Panama sailed through a brand-new set of locks, seventy feet wider and eighteen feet deeper than the originals. [↺]
79. The expansion cost more than five billion dollars. And it changed what goes through the canal.
80. Giant container ships. Grain. And energy. American gas, cooled into liquid, heading for Japan, China and Korea. [↺]
81. For a tanker going from the US Gulf Coast to Japan, Panama is the shortcut. Through the canal, about twenty days. The long way around, through Suez or around Africa, takes eleven to fourteen days more.
82. So every year, more ships. Bigger ships. | All drinking from the same lake. [↺]
83. And who's on those ships? Mostly, trade going to or from the United States. In the canal's own figures, around sixty-three percent of all cargo through Panama starts or ends in the US. [↺]
84. Then comes China, Japan, South Korea, Chile, Mexico. And one cargo stands out. Liquefied petroleum gas, the propane and butane that heats homes and feeds factories across Asia, much of it loaded on the US Gulf Coast.
85. When the canal slows down, that gas either waits, or it takes the long way around. | And somebody pays for the extra two weeks at sea. [↺]
86. And on the shore, Panama itself was growing. More people, more homes, more taps. | Same lake.
87. Here's how sensitive this whole machine is. The deeper a ship sits in the water, its draft, the more cargo it can carry. And the draft the canal allows depends on one number. | How high the lake is. [↺]
88. Every foot the lake drops, the canal has to tell ships to load lighter. Less cargo per ship. Less money per crossing. And if it drops far enough, fewer ships at all.
89. We worked it out. Each foot of water in Gatun Lake is worth roughly six hundred and fifty ship crossings. [↺]
90. And in a normal dry season, the lake drops by several feet.
91. Which is fine. | As long as the rainy season fills it back up.
92. In twenty twenty-three, it didn't. [↺]

## 4. THE YEAR THE RAIN STOPPED (15:30–20:45)
93. It started with El Niño.
94. Every few years, the Pacific Ocean warms up along the equator. Weather shifts around the planet. And over Panama, El Niño usually means one thing. | Less rain. [↺]
95. In twenty twenty-three, a strong El Niño arrived. And the rain over the canal began to fail.
96. By March, the canal was already cutting the draft. Ships were told to load lighter, and lighter, and lighter again.
97. By the end of July, it started cutting the number of crossings. Thirty-six a day became thirty-two. [↺]
98. And the line began to form.
99. Ships that had booked ahead got through. Ships that hadn't anchored and waited. Off both coasts, the queue grew and grew.
100. On the ninth of August, twenty twenty-three, more than a hundred and sixty ships were waiting to cross. Some waited more than two weeks. [↺]
101. From space, you could see them. Dozens of white dots, anchored in the bright blue water at both ends of Panama.
102. And then came October. Normally one of the wettest months of the year.
103. That October, the canal said, rainfall was forty-one percent below normal. | The driest October in seventy-three years of records. [↺]
104. From August to December, every single day, Gatun Lake was lower than it had ever been on that date. | Every day. For five months.
105. In early October twenty twenty-three, the canal published its daily water budget. In a normal year, about fifteen million cubic meters of water flow into the lake every day. That October, only about seven. | Meanwhile, ten million were flowing out.
106. A deficit of three million cubic meters. Every single day. [↺]
107. The canal tried everything. It started cross-filling, pouring water from one lock chamber into its twin instead of into the sea, saving the water of about five crossings a day.
108. It saved water. | It wasn't enough. [↺]
109. The canal kept cutting. Down to twenty-four a day. Then twenty-two. [↺]
110. And when you have a hundred ships and twenty-two places, | the places go to auction.
111. Normally, a slot in the canal's auction goes for something like fifty-five thousand dollars, on top of the toll. [↺]
112. That autumn, a Japanese company paid almost four million. | For one crossing.
113. The canal put it in its own words. The lake had fallen to "unprecedented levels for this time of year."
114. Then, finally, the rain came back. In twenty twenty-four, the lake recovered. By September, thirty-six ships a day were crossing again. [↺]
115. And this year, twenty twenty-six, started perfectly. In February, the lake was so full the canal opened its spillways to let water *out*.
116. In June, the canal published a forecast. | "Current data does not forecast the need for transit restrictions through December thirty-first, twenty twenty-six." [↺]
117. Seventy-two days later, | it cut them.
118. The rainy season had collapsed. From May to August, rainfall was thirty-four percent below average. The water flowing into the lake was forty-four percent below. [↺]
119. Slots went down. Thirty-six, to thirty-four, to thirty-two. Drafts went down. And the queue came back.
120. And that brings us to the second question. | What is a place in line worth when the rain stops? **(pays off L2)** [↺]
121. In August, a ship carrying liquefied gas, the G Spirit, won a slot for one crossing on the first of September. | According to Bloomberg, the winning bid was five point three million dollars.
122. When reporters asked the canal's new administrator if it was true, she said: "Correcto, sí." [↺]
123. Five point three million dollars. To skip a line. | Almost a hundred times the usual price.
124. In September, the rain partly came back, and the canal restored a slot, to thirty-three a day. ⏱
125. But it also warned: the water deficit continues. Ships without a reservation, it said, may face "indefinite delays." [↺]
126. Because the canal isn't worried about this year anymore. | It's worried about the next one.

## 5. THE COUNTDOWN (20:45–24:15): climax
127. On the eighth of October, NOAA, America's weather agency, put out its monthly El Niño update. ⏱
128. It said El Niño is strengthening, with a strong to very strong El Niño likely through the start of twenty twenty-seven. [↺]
129. And it gave an eighty-three percent chance that the last three months of this year will be stronger than every El Niño on record since nineteen fifty.
130. Now remember the canal's rule of thumb. | The worst doesn't come during El Niño. It comes the year after.
131. Look at the record. The lowest the lake has ever been was in twenty sixteen. | The year after an El Niño. [↺]
132. The second lowest, nineteen ninety-eight. | The year after an El Niño.
133. The third, twenty twenty-three. | El Niño again.
134. Which points to one date on the calendar. | The dry season of twenty twenty-seven. January to April. [↺]
135. Here's where the lake stands today. About eighty-five point seven feet. ⏱ That's six feet higher than on this day in twenty twenty-three. A real cushion.
136. The canal projects it will be around eighty-five and a half feet in mid-December, as the rains end. [↺]
137. Then the dry season starts, and the lake starts to fall.
138. In the dry seasons after the big El Niños of ninety-seven and twenty fifteen, the lake dropped by around five feet from mid-December. [↺]
139. If twenty twenty-seven drains like those years, | here's where it ends up.
140. Around eighty feet. | Right back at the levels of the twenty twenty-three crisis.
141. That's not a forecast. The canal hasn't published one. It's what happens if history repeats. [↺]
142. So, is there a fix?
143. There is. | Panama is going to drown another valley.
144. The Río Indio project. A new reservoir west of the canal, joined to Gatun Lake by a nine-kilometer tunnel. About one and a half billion dollars. [↺]
145. A new lake almost the size of Gatun itself, to keep the ships moving and the taps running for the next fifty years.
146. But it comes at a cost. About two thousand people live where the water will go, the canal says. Critics say the wider river basin holds more than thirteen thousand. [↺]
147. And there's the problem with time.
148. The construction contract hasn't been awarded. The main works are now scheduled to *start* in twenty twenty-eight. They'll take years.
149. > So the El Niño arrives now. | The dry season arrives in January. | And the new lake | arrives sometime in the next decade. [↺]
150. One of the canal's former administrators put it bluntly: "Right now, we are late by six years."
151. Until then, the canal has the same tools it had in twenty twenty-three. Reuse every drop it can. Load ships lighter. Cut the number of crossings. | And wait for the sky. [↺]

## RESOLUTION (24:15–25:15)
152. A hundred and twelve years ago, engineers looked at a river that kept destroying everything, and turned it into the engine of world trade.
153. They beat the mountains. | They beat the disease. | They beat the river. [↺]
154. And they built something that, in a strange way, is one of the most natural machines on Earth. No pumps lifting ships over the mountains. Just gravity, a river, and the weather. [↺]
155. That's its genius. | And now, it's its weakness.
156. And every day since, ships have climbed eighty-five feet into the sky on a staircase made of rain.
157. The question for twenty twenty-seven isn't whether the canal can be engineered. | It already has been.
158. It's whether the rain shows up. [↺]
159. > A canal built to beat the mountains | now depends on the sky.
