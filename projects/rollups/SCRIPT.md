## 0:45 CASE 1: CONSTELLATION SOFTWARE. "Buy small. Never sell."
First up: a Canadian company most people have never heard of. Constellation Software.

In 1995 Mark Leonard founded Constellation and started buying software companies. [C1, C2] Not exciting software.
Vertical market software: the boring program one narrow industry runs its whole day on, where customers almost never
switch. [C14]

*B-roll: unglamorous software screens, a small office.*

And here's the thing: he didn't buy big ones. Their published criteria start at about a million dollars of operating
profit. [C9] One analyst estimates they pay under two times revenue and around five times EBITDA. [C8] And their
relationship-driven approach reaches deals that never go to a competitive auction. [C13]

Think about what that means. Their internal database of potential targets went from about 1,200 companies in 2006 to
35,000 by 2017. [C6] They're cold-calling software founders at the scale of a call centre.

Then the part everyone gets wrong. Most buyers raise the price when they want more deals. Leonard did the opposite. He
set a hurdle rate, the minimum return a deal has to earn, reportedly well into the twenties, [C11] and he refused to
lower it. His line was: *"Hurdle rates are magnetic."* [C12] The moment you drop your standard to win one more deal,
every deal after that drifts down to meet it.

And after they buy? Almost nothing changes. No big integration. No central platform. *"It does not buy and sell
businesses, pursue large-scale restructuring programs, or integrate companies into a centralised platform. It owns
them."* [C15] By one count more than a thousand small software businesses, each still run by its own manager, grouped
under autonomous operating groups. [C17] Managers deep in the company can approve acquisitions themselves, up to a limit. [C19]

Two more details. They issue no stock options. [C21] Managers buy shares with their own after-tax bonus money, so they
think like owners. And they barely used debt for most of their history. [C23]

The result: the stock went public in 2006, and one analysis puts its compounding at about 29 percent a year since then.
[C27] Revenue went from around a hundred million to more than eight billion. [C26]

And in September 2025, Leonard stepped down as president for health reasons. ✔ The machine kept running without him,
because it was never about one dealmaker. It was about a rule.

**The lesson:** small deals, bought off-market, at a price you refuse to stretch, then left alone to run.

## 5:30 CASE 2: TRANSDIGM. "Own the bottleneck. Lever it up."
TransDigm started in 1993 as a leveraged buyout of four aerospace units. [T1] One breakdown says the founders put in
about ten million of equity against more than a hundred million of debt. [T12]

Since then they've bought 95 businesses and product lines. [T3 ✔ 10-K] But look at what they buy. Not airlines. Not
whole planes. Small engineered components, focused, in their own words, on proprietary parts with significant
aftermarket content. [T7 ✔ 10-K]

In their own annual report: about 90 percent of sales come from proprietary products, and over half from the
aftermarket. [T17 ✔ 10-K] One analysis says roughly three quarters are sole-source. [T18] Sole-source means when an
airline needs that part again, there's one place to buy it.

*On screen: EBITDA margin ~54% (FY2025) [T21]*

That's the first contradiction with Constellation. Constellation buys businesses where customers don't leave. TransDigm
buys businesses where customers can't leave. And then they use that.

Second contradiction: debt. TransDigm keeps net debt at roughly five to seven times EBITDA. [T13] When cash flow pays
the debt down, they borrow again, to buy the next company or to pay shareholders. In August 2025 they raised five
billion dollars of new debt and the same day declared a 90-dollar-per-share special dividend. ✔ Constellation barely
touches debt. TransDigm treats it like a tool you should always be using.

And this has a cost. In 2021 the Pentagon's inspector general found TransDigm had earned at least 20.8 million dollars
in excess profit on 105 spare parts. [T25 ✔ DoD IG] Congress held a hearing titled *"Price Gouging in Military
Contracts: New Inspector General Report Exposes Excess Profit Obtained by TransDigm Group."* [T28 ✔] Audits haven't changed the model. [T30]

So is that a playbook? For them, it's worked: one feature puts their ten-year return around 760 percent. [T22] But here
is the warning label. Valeant Pharmaceuticals ran the same mix, debt-funded acquisitions plus big price hikes, without
the moat, and its stock went from about 264 dollars in 2015 to about 25 a few months later. [V4]

**The lesson:** pricing power and leverage multiply each other. With a real bottleneck, that's a machine. Without one,
it's a bomb.

## 11:10 CASE 3: BRAD JACOBS. "Build it, integrate it, sell it."
Brad Jacobs has founded and led eight companies that became billion-dollar businesses, across roughly 500
transactions. [B1]

In the early nineties he rolled up rural trash haulers into United Waste, then sold it to Waste Management in 1997 for
billions. [B4, B5] Then he co-founded United Rentals and, according to his own company's SEC filing,
made it the largest equipment-rental company in the world in 13 months. [B7 ✔ SEC] Two-thirds of its 750 locations came
from acquisitions. [B8 ✔ SEC]

In 2011 he took over XPO, a logistics company with 175 million in revenue, [B10 ✔ SEC] bolted on competitors, grew it
into a giant, and then did the opposite of Constellation: he split it into three public companies, GXO in 2021 and RXO
in 2022. [B14 ✔, B18]

Constellation never sells. Jacobs builds to sell, or to split, because a focused company gets valued higher than a
messy one.

Right now he's doing it again: a hostile, five-month fight to buy Beacon Roofing Supply for about 11 billion dollars,
closed in 2025 by his new company, QXO. [B21, B32] The price was about 10.8 times EBITDA. [B22] Constellation pays
around five. Jacobs pays eleven, because his model is to buy a big platform, then add smaller businesses that the market
values at his multiple the moment they're inside. The target: 50 billion dollars of revenue within a decade. [B24]

**The lesson:** if you plan the exit from day one, you can pay up, integrate hard, and let the public market re-price
everything you bolt on.

## 14:00 THE CONTRADICTION
So here's what I wanted to show you. There is no one roll-up playbook.

*On screen: the matrix.*

| | Constellation | TransDigm | Jacobs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sell? | Almost never | Rarely | Builds to sell/spin |
| After the deal | Leave it alone | Price + cost discipline | Integrate onto one platform |
| Debt | Barely | 5–7× EBITDA | Big raises per deal |
| Deal size | Tiny | Mid | Huge platform + bolt-ons |
| Price paid | ~5× EBITDA | n/a | ~11× EBITDA |

But all three share four things. **One:** a narrow definition of what they buy: vertical software, proprietary aircraft
parts, distribution platforms. **Two:** a fixed number they won't break: a hurdle rate, a leverage band, a revenue goal. [C12, T13, B24]
**Three:** a machine that repeats, not a heroic deal. **Four:** they buy earnings cheaper than the market values those same earnings
once they're inside the bigger company. That gap, multiple arbitrage, is the whole game. [G1, G3] *(Small deals average ~5.9× EBITDA; $100–250M
deals ~10× [G4])*

## 14:45 WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU
Now, you're probably not buying a 10-billion-dollar roofing company this year. So what do you actually take?

From Constellation: buy small and off-market, and set your number before you see the deal. If you lower your hurdle
once, you'll lower it forever.

From Jacobs: decide your exit on day one. A group of ten businesses with one finance team, one brand and clean books
gets valued at a different multiple than ten separate shops.

From TransDigm, take the discipline, not the debt. They can run six times leverage because their customers can't leave.
Your HVAC company's customers can. [THIS IS WHY WE USE VENDOR FINANCE AND KEEP DEBT SERVICE COVERAGE ABOVE 1.5×.]

And here's the uncomfortable number: by some estimates, more than two thirds of roll-ups create no value for investors.
[G7] Not because the idea is wrong, but because they copy the wrong playbook for their industry.
