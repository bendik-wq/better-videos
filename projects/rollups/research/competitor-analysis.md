# Research breakdown: roll-up video landscape

## 1) Per-video analysis

### 1. Kelp — “How One Playbook Built 8 Billion-Dollar Companies”
**Views:** 351  
**Angle:** Brad Jacobs as a serial roll-up operator with a repeatable “filter + machine” playbook across waste, rental, logistics, and building products.

**Companies/people covered:** Brad Jacobs; United Waste, United Rentals, XPO, GXO, RXO, QXO; mentions Beacon, GMS, Kodiak, TopBuild.

**Key claims and numbers:**
- “In its first full quarter, QXO earned $13 million in revenue and 56 million in interest on its cash pile.”
- “From 1992 to 1996, revenue climbed from $107 million to nearly 492, almost fivefold, while profit more than quadrupled. In 1997, barely eight years after founding it, Jacobs sold the entire company for roughly two and a half billion dollars.”
- “A small rental business, privately owned, sells for four to six times its yearly earnings. The moment those same earnings sit inside United Rentals, public, liquid, trusted by the market, they're valued at 10 to 12.”
- “He did it roughly 250 times.”
- “Revenue went from roughly $490 million in 1997 to nearly 2.9 billion in 2001, the largest equipment rental company in the world.”
- “$177 million in revenue in 2011. 17.3 billion by 2018.”
- “TopBuild, $17 billion in cash and stock. In a little over a year, $30 billion of acquisitions turned an empty shell into the second largest building products distributor in North America.”
- “Nearly five decades, roughly 500 acquisitions, eight companies at billion-dollar scale.”

**Hook first two sentences:**  
“In 2024, a man took control of a small forgotten software company called Silver Sun and renamed it QXO. Then the money came, more than $5 billion from some of the most sophisticated investors on Earth — not for the software business, real but tiny, not for warehouses, inventory, or customers at scale.”

**What it does well:**  
Strong storytelling; it includes failures and crises, not just wins: SEC inquiry, accounting scandal, short-seller attack, lost buyout, Amazon loss. Clear two-pillar structure makes the playbook easy to follow.

**What it leaves out:**  
Actual integration mechanics, deal-level underwriting, debt structure, cash flow modeling, tax consequences, board/investor terms, management retention, and a realistic sense of what ordinary buyers can copy.

### 2. Acquisition Collective — “How I Bought 58 Businesses & Built a $1B Empire”
**Views:** 17,024  
**Angle:** Long-form practitioner interview with Adam Coffey, a GE-trained CEO/private-equity operator, teaching buy-and-build roll-up mechanics.

**Companies/people covered:** Adam Coffey; GE/Jack Welch; Web Service Company/laundry roll-up; EQT; HVAC roll-up; bookkeeping, payroll, accounting service roll-ups.

**Key claims and numbers:**
- “Bought 58 companies put them together to build my my three Empires… 2.4 billion in exits.”
- Laundry: “Started with a national company that had about 27 million in ebitda… spent $17 million investing in technology… had three exits… the company that I ended with at that time three state company out earned the company I started with… eida was at about 57 million… then sold it to traditional private equity… bought 34 companies… sold it for over a billion dollars to a company called eqt.”
- HVAC: “On average I was paying five times for two million of ebah… 23 transactions that average 10 million a piece… I borrowed 230 million from the banks… sell the combined companies for 14 times multiple… every dollar of IA I bought and paid $5 for I then turned around and sold for 14 I made nine9 of profit.”
- “Round numbers I bought 46 million of ebit do paid five times that's 230 million… sell it for 14 times EIT do 644 million… pay off the 230 million… put 414 million in my pocket.”
- “There’s $6 trillion dollar in capital… 8,000 private Equity firms.”
- “34 million small companies… 99.9% of all companies in the US.”
- “Only 3,000 companies on the planet that have a billion dollars in Revenue.”

**Hook first two sentences:**  
“To seeing some familiar faces out there so hello folks um so for those of you who don't know me um my name is Adam Coffey um…”  
This is not a hard scripted hook; it opens like a podcast/interview.

**What it does well:**  
Very practical operator detail: buy-side advisor, VP of business development, internal M&A team, integration light vs heavy, rollover equity, multiple arbitrage, PE pyramid, cross-selling acquired companies.

**What it leaves out:**  
Failures, bad deals, broken integrations, debt covenant risk, tax drag, integration costs, legal/disputes, market-cycle risk, and the fact that his numbers may reflect strong buy-side timing as much as repeatable method.

### 3. Forbes — “This Billionaire Bought Over A Thousand Small Businesses And Never Lost A Dime”
**Views:** 378,020  
**Angle:** Founder-led private equity with a “Main Street” identity; Justin Ishbia/Shore Capital profile.

**Companies/people covered:** Justin Ishbia; Shore Capital; Matt Ishbia; Southern Vet; Jay Price; BrightView; United Wholesale Mortgage context; Phoenix Suns/Nashville SC.

**Key claims and numbers:**
- Title claims he “Bought Over A Thousand Small Businesses And Never Lost A Dime.”
- “Smaller mom and pop businesses 20 million in Revenue new or less.”
- Southern Vet: “Originally met Jay Price… start out with just three locations and today there's over 400 locations and we've done dozens and dozens of Acquisitions… 1.2 billion in annual revenue.”
- “We had just closed our first funds this is 2014 we raised $112 million.”
- “Our average Partners have 100 employees.”
- “15 years of sunny skies Tailwinds demand Trends.”
- “If you can't measure it you can't manage it.”

**Hook first two sentences:**  
“We at sh Capital love partnering in Industries where there is much more demand than there is Supply and our formula is if there's much more demand and Supply G become the supplier of choice. Justin ishbia is the founder of Shore Capital…”

**What it does well:**  
Humanizes PE; shows founder-led partner credibility, culture, sector selection, and why Main Street operators might prefer a sector-focused partner.

**What it leaves out:**  
Audited fund returns, realized multiples, failure cases, integration details, healthcare PE criticism, and how “never lost a dime” is actually measured. Title claims “thousand businesses” but much of the transcript says “dozens and dozens.”

### 4. Brett Cenkus — “Roll-Up Acquisitions: Consolidating Small Businesses Into Market Dominance”
**Views:** 36,080  
**Angle:** Educational legal/advisor breakdown of roll-ups as financial-buyer strategy, with emphasis on fragmentation, multiple arbitrage, and execution risk.

**Companies/people covered:** Wayne Huizenga; Waste Management; Blockbuster Video; AutoNation; private equity, investment banking context.

**Key claims and numbers:**
- “If you're buying up small companies you might pay two and a half or three times seller discretionary earnings and as you get to be a larger larger company the company… trading for four or five or six times.”
- “We were trading at… 30 40 times our earnings and we're buying companies for three or four times.”
- “Waste management was a roll up. Blockbuster Video… auto nation — those are all Wayne Huizenga roll-ups.”
- “They fail because of execution. And they're very hard to execute.”
- “There can be a lot of integration execution risk.”

**Hook first two sentences:**  
“Hi, this is Brett Cenkus. I am a business attorney with Cenkus Law and an M & A or mergers and acquisitions advisor with Braaten Woods.”  
Again, not a strong YouTube hook; it is an educational intro.

**What it does well:**  
Clear explanation of platform vs roll-up, financial-buyer appeal, multiple arbitrage, integration/culture risk, and why roll-ups are more execution than idea.

**What it leaves out:**  
Modern case studies, actual integration playbooks, debt structures, return math, failure data, and concrete examples beyond Huizenga.

### 5. CNBC — “The AI Rollup Wave Transforming Main Street”
**Views:** 38,362  
**Angle:** AI-native holding companies buying boring Main Street service businesses and rebuilding them around proprietary AI/workflow software.

**Companies/people covered:** General Catalyst; Thrive Capital; Lightspeed; Andreessen Horowitz; Madhu Namburi; Long Lake; Alex Taubman; Amex GBT; Janus Henderson; Vista; Thoma Bravo; Silverlake; OpenAI; Anthropic.

**Key claims and numbers:**
- “General Catalyst has created roughly a dozen of these holding companies in the last three years.”
- “Long Lake… has acquired over 30 businesses spanning HOA management, construction, corporate travel.”
- “Our Nexus platform is actually performing five times better than the off the shelf models.”
- “Services is a $20 trillion market.”
- Software margins “run 70,80, even 90% historically.”
- “Vista bought Citrix, Thoma Bravo bought Anaplan and Coupa. Silverlake bought Qualtrics.”
- “We plan to own and operate these businesses forever, more like a Berkshire Hathaway than a traditional VC or PE fund.”

**Hook first two sentences:**  
“So how is AI going to actually change the economy? It may not be with flashy moonshots like robotics.”

**What it does well:**  
Clear framing of “service as software,” specific actors, and honest tension between venture capital return expectations and unglamorous service businesses.

**What it leaves out:**  
Actual Long Lake P&L, unit economics, margins before/after AI, integration details, churn, regulatory risk, and whether “5x better” is a meaningful commercial metric.
