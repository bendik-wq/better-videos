**TITLE: THE AUCTION THAT SETS YOUR MORTGAGE RATE**

## 1. THE IOU (0:45–5:00)
11. Let's start with the thing being sold.
12. The United States spends more than it collects in taxes. In the last fiscal year, about two trillion dollars more. [↺]
13. So it borrows. And the way it borrows is simple. | It sells IOUs.
14. Hand the government a thousand dollars today. It pays you interest twice a year, | and in ten years, it hands your thousand back.
15. That IOU is a Treasury note. Every Treasury note, bill and bond comes from the same place: an auction.
16. Last fiscal year, the Treasury held four hundred and thirty-six of them. [↺] More than one every single business day.
17. Most are short-term bills. But the ten-year auction is the one the world watches. | Here's how it works.
18. A week before, the Treasury announces what it's selling. On October the first: thirty-nine billion dollars of ten-year notes. Bids close at one p.m. sharp, Eastern time.
19. Then the bids come in. Sealed. | Nobody sees anybody else's.
20. Each bidder writes down one thing: the interest rate they're willing to accept. | Down to the thousandth of a percent. [↺]
21. No single bidder can take more than thirty-five percent of the sale. [↺]
22. And here's something most people don't know. | You can bid too. Through a government website, anyone can put in a non-competitive bid, up to ten million dollars.
23. No haggling. You simply agree to take whatever rate the big bidders set.
24. A bank might say four point three. A pension fund might say five point two five. A hedge fund might hold out for five point four.
25. At one o'clock, the window shuts. | And the Treasury sorts every bid, | from the lowest rate to the highest.
26. Then it starts filling the order. The cheapest money first. Then the next. And the next. | Until it has sold all thirty-nine billion.
27. The last bid it needs to accept | sets the price for everyone. [↺]
28. That's the strange rule at the heart of this auction. The bidder who asked for four point three doesn't get four point three. | Everyone gets the highest accepted rate.
29. On October the seventh, that rate was five point three zero zero percent. | Bids at exactly that rate got a little more than half of what they asked for. Bids above it got nothing.
30. Now, here's the part that trips everyone up. The note itself pays a fixed coupon: four and five-eighths percent. That never changes. [↺]
31. So how do bidders get five point three? | They pay less for it.
32. Instead of a hundred dollars, they paid about ninety-four dollars and eighty-six cents for every hundred dollars the government will hand back in twenty thirty-six.
33. Pay less today, get the same money back later, | and your real return goes up.
34. That's the seesaw at the center of the bond market. When bidders demand more, | prices fall | and yields rise. [↺]
35. And the yield is the number that travels. Out of the auction, | and into everything else.
36. Who are these bidders? **(opens L2)** [↺]
37. First, two dozen big banks called primary dealers. They're required to bid in every auction, at reasonable prices. They're the safety net. | If nobody else shows up, they do.
38. Then everyone else. Bidders who come in directly. And customers who bid through a dealer: mutual funds, pension funds, foreign central banks.
39. And here's what surprised us. | On October the seventh, the safety net was barely needed. The dealers ended up with just two and a half percent of the auction. The smallest share since at least two thousand and eight. [↺]
40. Investors put in a hundred and eight billion dollars of bids, for thirty-nine billion dollars of notes.
41. So this was not an auction nobody wanted. | People wanted it badly.
42. > They just wanted to be paid more.
43. And when the people lending to America want to be paid more, | the cost of borrowing goes up for everyone else. | Including you.

## 2. THE PIPE (5:00–9:30)
44. To see why, follow the money from your front door. [↺]
45. When you take out a mortgage, your bank usually doesn't keep it. | It sells it.
46. Your loan gets bundled with thousands of others into a mortgage-backed security. A bond made of house payments.
47. And the people who buy those bonds are the same kind of people bidding at the Treasury auction: funds, insurers, banks, foreign investors. [↺]
48. So every one of them has a choice. Lend to the US government for ten years. | Or lend to homeowners.
49. The US government is seen as the safest borrower on Earth. Homeowners are not. They might default. | Or, more likely, they might pay you back early.
50. So to buy your mortgage, investors want more than the Treasury pays. Always.
51. Think about it from the investor's side. If rates fall, you refinance, and they get their money back just when they'd rather keep lending at the old, higher rate. [↺]
52. If rates rise, you keep your cheap loan for as long as you can. | Either way, the homeowner wins and the investor loses.
53. The Boston Fed says the gap between mortgages and Treasuries "largely reflects the price" of exactly that option.
54. That extra is called the spread. Since nineteen seventy-one, it has averaged about one and three-quarter points. Today, it's about two point three. [↺]
55. But wait. A mortgage lasts thirty years. Why is it priced off a ten-year note?
56. Because almost nobody keeps a mortgage for thirty years. People move. They refinance. They sell.
57. In the words of the St. Louis Fed: the average time before a mortgage is paid off "tends to be much closer to ten years." [↺]
58. So the ten-year Treasury is the benchmark. Your rate is roughly that, | plus the spread.
59. When the ten-year auction clears at five point three, | mortgages don't start from three. | They start from five point three.
60. Now, back to our question. **(closes L1)**
61. The Federal Reserve doesn't set your mortgage rate. | It sets an overnight rate. The rate banks charge each other to borrow until tomorrow morning. [↺]
62. It can nudge the long rates. Through what people expect it to do next. Through what it buys. But it doesn't sell the ten-year note. | And it doesn't set the price.
63. Even the St. Louis Fed says that after the Fed moves, "you don't usually see immediate movement in mortgage interest rates." [↺]
64. The auction does.
65. So when the Fed started cutting in twenty twenty-four, the bidders weren't pricing tomorrow morning. | They were pricing the next ten years.
66. And they asked for more. The ten-year yield rose by nearly a point | while the Fed was cutting. [↺]
67. In all, the Fed cut by one and three-quarter points. By the time it stopped, in December twenty twenty-five, mortgage rates were *higher* than when it started.
68. Then this September, the Fed reversed course. It raised rates again. | And the bidders kept asking for more.
69. Here's what that does to an actual home. [↺]
70. Take a four-hundred-thousand-dollar mortgage. A year ago, at six point two seven percent, the monthly payment was about two thousand four hundred and sixty-eight dollars.
71. At seven point four, | it's two thousand seven hundred and seventy. **(our math)**
72. Three hundred dollars more. Every month. | For the same house. [↺]
73. Over thirty years, that's more than a hundred and eight thousand dollars in extra interest.
74. And compared with the three percent mortgages of twenty twenty-one, | it's more than a thousand dollars extra, every single month.
75. All of it traced back to one number, | set in an auction you never saw.

## 3. WHEN THE BOND MARKET SAYS NO (9:30–14:00)
76. This isn't new. The bond market has been overruling presidents for a long time. [↺]
77. The first Treasury bill auction took place in December nineteen twenty-nine. Seven weeks after the stock market crash.
78. Bids came in on paper. In special envelopes. They had to arrive by two o'clock on Friday, December the thirteenth.
79. Investors bid for two hundred and twenty-four million dollars. | The Treasury only wanted a hundred million.
80. The Treasury loved it. The Under Secretary said he was "entirely satisfied." | Instead of the government picking a price, | the market would set it. [↺]
81. Sixty-four years later, a political strategist named James Carville learned what that meant.
82. He had just helped get Bill Clinton elected. And in nineteen ninety-three, he told the Wall Street Journal:
83. "I used to think if there was reincarnation, I wanted to come back as the president or the pope or a point four hundred baseball hitter. | But now I want to come back as the bond market. | You can intimidate everybody." [↺]
84. The economist Ed Yardeni had a name for those intimidators. He coined it in nineteen eighty-three. | The Bond Vigilantes.
85. A year after Carville's quote, they rode into town.
86. In February nineteen ninety-four, the Fed began raising rates. And the bond market panicked.
87. From its low the autumn before, the ten-year yield climbed from about five point two percent | to just over eight. [↺]
88. Fortune magazine called it "the worst bond market loss in history." More than six hundred billion dollars wiped off the value of American bonds.
89. In California, Orange County had borrowed heavily to bet that rates would stay low. | They didn't.
90. The county lost one point six four billion dollars. In December, it filed for bankruptcy. | The largest municipal bankruptcy in American history, at the time. [↺]
91. And then there's Britain.
92. September twenty twenty-two. A new government announced a budget with big tax cuts, and no clear way to pay for them.
93. The bond market's answer took three days. | The interest rate on thirty-year British government bonds jumped more than a full point.
94. The Bank of England later called it a move "three times larger than any other historical move" of its kind. [↺]
95. Pension funds that had borrowed against those bonds were forced to sell, which pushed rates higher, which forced more selling.
96. Over about two and a half weeks, the Bank of England bought nineteen point three billion pounds of government bonds. [↺]
97. The Bank of England had to step in, and promised to buy bonds "on whatever scale is necessary."
98. Within weeks, the finance minister was out. | Days later, the Prime Minister announced she would resign. [↺]
99. That is the bond market. It doesn't vote. | It doesn't protest. | It just names its price.
100. And in October twenty twenty-six, the price it named was the highest in more than a quarter of a century.
101. Inside the auctions themselves, the warning signs are usually subtle. [↺]
102. In February twenty twenty-one, a seven-year auction drew the weakest demand in over a decade. Dealers, the safety net, had to take almost forty percent of it.
103. In November twenty twenty-three, a thirty-year auction went the same way. Dealers took a quarter. And the price came in well above what traders expected minutes before.
104. Each time, the safety net caught it.
105. October the seventh was the opposite. The safety net took almost nothing. | Investors queued up. [↺]
106. Which tells you something more uncomfortable. | This wasn't panic. It was a calm, crowded room | deciding that America's money is worth more than it used to be.

## 4. THE PRICE OF FORTY TRILLION (14:00–18:00)
107. So why are bidders asking for more now? [↺] **(pays off L2)**
108. Start with how much America owes. | As of mid-October, a little over forty point three trillion dollars.
109. That's up two point four five trillion in a single year. About six point seven billion dollars a day. **(our math)** [↺]
110. And all of it costs interest. In the fiscal year that just ended, the US government spent a little over a trillion dollars on net interest.
111. That's more than it spent on its entire military. | About one dollar in every five it collected in taxes.
112. And here's the problem nobody can avoid. | That debt doesn't sit still. [↺]
113. About a third of it, more than ten trillion dollars, comes due within the next twelve months. **(our math)**
114. The Treasury doesn't pay it off. It can't. | It sells new IOUs to repay the old ones.
115. Which means it has to go back to the auction. Again, and again, and again.
116. And the old debt was cheap. The average interest rate on everything outstanding is about three and a half percent. [↺]
117. New ten-year money? | Five point three.
118. Every time old debt rolls into new debt, a cheap IOU becomes an expensive one.
119. Last fiscal year, the Treasury sold more than thirty-one trillion dollars of debt at auction. | Most of it short-term bills, rolled over and over. [↺]
120. And the average rate on everything it owes is now the highest since two thousand and eight.
121. You can already see it in the bill. Net interest rose by about eighty-one billion dollars in a single year.
122. Not because of one bad auction. | Because of hundreds of ordinary ones, each a little more expensive than the debt it replaced. [↺]
123. Meanwhile, some of the biggest buyers have stepped back.
124. For years, the Fed itself bought trillions in Treasuries and mortgage bonds. Today it's no longer adding ten-year notes. And its mortgage bonds keep running off, almost two hundred billion dollars in the last year. Someone else has to buy them. [↺]
125. Japan, still the largest foreign holder, has seen its holdings fall by a hundred and seventy-one billion dollars since February.
126. That doesn't mean Japan is dumping America. Part of that fall is just prices: when yields rise, old bonds are worth less. | But the biggest overseas customer isn't adding.
127. Britain's holdings, meanwhile, rose past a trillion dollars. China's fell by over a hundred billion in a year. [↺]
128. And at home, the biggest buyers of short-term bills are money market funds, holding more than three trillion dollars. Part of that may be your savings.
129. So the buyers who are left can choose. | And when you're the one the seller needs, | you set the terms. [↺]
130. That's why October the seventh looked the way it did. Huge demand. | At a high price.
131. And that price didn't stay in the bond market for long.
132. In the week of the auction, the average thirty-year mortgage hit seven point four percent. | The highest since November twenty twenty-three. ⏱ [↺]

## 5. THE COUNTDOWN (18:00–21:00): climax
133. Here's what makes this moment different.
134. Look at the people who already own a home. | About half of all American mortgages are locked in below four percent. ⏱
135. More than three in four are below six. [↺]
136. So for millions of families, moving now means trading a three percent loan | for a seven percent one.
137. So they don't move. Fewer homes come up for sale. And the people who need a home now, | first-time buyers, growing families, people who changed jobs, | pay the auction price.
138. Existing home sales are running at under four million a year. ⏱ [↺]
139. The median home sold in September cost four hundred and seventeen thousand five hundred dollars. ⏱
140. Put twenty percent down, and at today's rate the payment is about two hundred and fifty dollars a month higher than a year ago. **(our math)** [↺]
141. That's the auction, | arriving in your mailbox.
142. And the next price is coming.
143. On Wednesday, November the fourth, the Treasury will announce how much it plans to sell.
144. In August, it said it expected to keep its auction sizes steady for at least the next several quarters. | But the amount is only half the story. [↺]
145. The other half is the price. And the Treasury doesn't get to choose that.
146. On Tuesday, November the tenth, it auctions a brand-new ten-year note. [↺]
147. Two days later, the thirty-year bond.
148. Here is how fast it travels. [↺]
149. Bids lock at one. | The result is posted that afternoon. | Bond prices move. Mortgage-bond investors reprice.
150. Lenders update the rate sheets they hand to loan officers. | And by Thursday, Freddie Mac's weekly survey shows where mortgage rates landed.
151. Nobody knows the price yet. Not the Treasury. Not the Fed. | Not the bidders.
152. If the buyers come back hungrier, | the rate falls. And within days, mortgages can follow it down.
153. If they ask for more, | your next quote goes up. [↺]
154. And either way, it will all be decided the same way it was on October the seventh.
155. Sealed bids. | One deadline.
156. > Twelve fifty-nine. [↺]
157. > One p.m.
158. And one number.

## RESOLUTION (21:00–21:45)
159. We tend to think of a mortgage rate as something a bank decides. Or the Fed. Or the government.
160. But almost every business day, the Treasury asks the world one question: | what will it cost to lend to America?
161. The answer is set by strangers. Pension funds, insurers, banks, foreign treasuries. | Bidding for the right to be paid. [↺]
162. Their answer becomes the government's interest bill. | Your mortgage. | Your rent.
163. So the next time someone tells you the Fed is about to cut rates, and your mortgage is about to get cheaper, | ask one question. [↺]
164. What did the auction say?
165. Because the Fed sets the price of money for one night. | The auction sets it for ten years.
166. > And the house | is sold at whatever price the auction says.
