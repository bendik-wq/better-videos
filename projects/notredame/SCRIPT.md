## COLD OPEN
1. Paris. Six-eighteen on a Monday evening, April 2019.
2. Beside Notre-Dame, a screen flashes one word. | Feu. Fire.
3. The alarm worked.
4. So why was the fire brigade called about half an hour later? Because one message could be read two ways.
5. Later, the spire fell in front of millions. | After it, a second clock started, on a burning bell frame in the north tower.
6. France's deputy interior minister said saving the cathedral came down to fifteen to thirty minutes.
7. Lose that tower, the people in charge feared, and the whole cathedral could follow it down.
8. So what did it take to save that tower? | And why is Notre-Dame still standing?

## 1. TWO ATTICS, ONE MESSAGE
9. It's Holy Week. Inside the cathedral, evening Mass is under way.
10. About forty metres above the congregation sits the roof frame. So many oak beams, it's called the forest.
11. Guarding it is a fire alarm that took dozens of experts six years to put together.
12. Thin tubes run through the attic, drawing in air and testing it for smoke. More than a hundred and sixty detectors and alarms cover the site.
13. At six-eighteen, one of them finds smoke.
14. In the security post, in a small building next door, the screen lights up. Feu. Then a zone: attic, nave, sacristy.
15. Then a code. And last, two technical words: aspirating framework.
16. The code pointed to one detector. The last words pointed to the cathedral's own roof. | But the zone at the top covered two attics.
17. The nave attic is the cathedral's roof. The sacristy is a smaller building attached to its side, with an attic of its own.
18. And the system didn't call the fire brigade by itself. By design, a person had to check first.
19. One person watched the screen. A guard, on the radio, did the checking.
20. The person at the screen had been on since early morning: a double shift, a few days into the job.
21. Now put yourself on that radio. A message written for engineers, the key words last, and a rule that says check before you call.
22. The guard is sent up to the attic of the sacristy. | Nothing to report.
23. What exactly reached the guard is still disputed. The security firm says the location was passed on. The cathedral says its guards heard attic, nave, sacristy, and nothing more.
24. Meanwhile, the Mass is stopped and the cathedral is emptied.
25. The plan behind the alarm, The New York Times reported days later, expected old, dense oak to burn slowly.
26. Even a perfect response, the paper calculated, needed about twenty minutes before anyone with firefighting gear reached the attic.
27. And there were no sprinklers and no fire walls in the roof. Both had been left out to protect the architecture.
28. Six forty-three. A second alert, and a phone call that sends the guard to the other attic.
29. About three hundred narrow steps up into the roof.
30. At the top, the forest is on fire.
31. On our first clock, twenty-five minutes have passed. | And the fire brigade still hasn't been called.
32. The Times puts the call at six forty-eight. The government's record says the alert was received at six fifty-one. | About half an hour after the screen said Feu.
33. The first crews are on their way. | The forest is about to push them back down.

## 2. A WALL OF FLAME
34. Just before seven, the first fire engines pull up in front of the cathedral.
35. The crews run up the stairs with their hoses, into the roof.
36. The Times described what met them there as an unstoppable wall of flame.
37. They're forced to retreat.
38. Out on the Seine, two fireboats take up position to feed the lines. Through the night, nearly five hundred firefighters and more than seventy vehicles join the fight.
39. Above the forest sits a roof made of lead. Two hundred and ten tonnes on the roof. Two hundred and fifty more on the spire.
40. As the oak burns away under it, the lead melts, and runs out over the top of the stone vaults.
41. The spire itself is from 1859. Oak, sheathed in lead, ninety-six metres to the tip of its rooster.
42. Four days earlier, a crane had lifted its sixteen copper statues away, for a restoration already on the calendar. | They were saved by a work schedule.
43. About seven-forty, the president's office postpones his televised address.
44. A little before eight, in front of the crowds and the live cameras, the spire falls.
45. It drops straight into the burning roof.
46. Then word reaches General Jean-Claude Gallet, the brigade's commander. | Between twenty and forty firefighters are trapped inside the cathedral.
47. Their way back out is full of flames.

## 3. NO PLANES
48. The spire, remember, was not what nearly brought Notre-Dame down. | And the obvious fix could have.
49. Millions watching had the obvious answer. So did the American president.
50. He wrote: "Perhaps flying water tankers could be used to put it out. Must act quickly!"
51. You've seen those planes drop water on forest fires.
52. France's civil protection service answered that same night, without naming anyone.
53. All means were being used, they wrote, except water-bombing aircraft. | Those, they warned, could lead to the collapse of the entire structure.
54. The weight of the water, dropped from low altitude, could weaken the building and damage the buildings around it.
55. So the fight stays on the ground, one hose at a time. | And it's about to move.
56. Look at the front of Notre-Dame. Two towers, sixty-nine metres tall.
57. In the north tower, eight bells hang in a wooden frame. They range from about three-quarters of a tonne to more than four tonnes.
58. In the south tower hangs the great bell, Emmanuel, cast in 1686. Thirteen tonnes.
59. By about half past eight, the Times reports, the north tower is already on fire.
60. The bells were never the danger. | The burning oak holding them up was.
61. Here's the chain they feared. The frame burns through. The bells fall. The north tower goes. | And that could take the rest of the cathedral with it.
62. Gallet said it that night: if the bells fall, this tower falls.
63. A culture ministry fire expert went further. Lose the war of the bell towers, he said, and you lose the cathedral.
64. From the ground, nobody could tell how much of that frame was left. | The only way to find out was to climb into it.

## 4. THE SECOND CLOCK
65. At police headquarters, Gallet briefs the officials. The roof can't be saved. He'll fight for the towers.
66. The district mayor, Ariel Weil, was in the room. Gallet told them he'd soon know if they had lost it.
67. "We knew what he meant," Weil said later. | "He meant Notre-Dame could collapse."
68. Gallet gives the president two options. Do nothing, and by five in the morning, he says, we won't see the two towers any more.
69. Or try a manoeuvre he isn't sure will work, because it has to be done within the half hour.
70. At half past nine, by the culture ministry's account, his plan is approved. | A second clock starts.
71. Now you're the one sending them. One tower, burning under eight bells. | Do you send people in? | Make your call.
72. Weil remembers it was clear some firefighters would go in without knowing if they'd come back out.
73. About twenty of them went.
74. And the water is failing them. The Times reports one of the cathedral's own pipes leaking, and the pressure dropping.
75. Embers the size of your thumb are burning holes in some of the hoses.
76. So they run their own line. Up the south tower they go, dragging two hoses fed straight from a fire truck.
77. At the top, they cross into the north tower, at the height of the bells.
78. Nine thirty-six. The government's record: two thousand eight hundred square metres of roof on fire, and the fire has reached the bell frame.
79. Around them hang eight bells, the biggest over four tonnes. | Under them, the oak that holds them up is burning.
80. Four minutes later, Laurent Nuñez, the deputy interior minister, tells the cameras the fire is not contained. | Saving the cathedral is not certain.
81. Ten minutes into Gallet's half hour. | Stand where they stood. The frame is the only clock you can see.
82. Up at the bells, the Times says, they find part of a wall and the floor burning. | And they put it out.
83. Then another general, Jean-Marie Gontier, goes up to look. He comes back down with three words. | "She is saved." | Elle est sauvée.
84. It's about ten at night. | Gallet's half hour is about up.
85. The next day, Nuñez gave his estimate of how close it had been. | It came down to fifteen to thirty minutes.
86. That's an estimate, not a measurement. | And it was the second half hour of the night.
87. The first had been lost before anyone called. General Gontier compared it to starting a four-hundred-metre race several dozen metres behind.
88. At half past eleven, President Macron stands in front of the cathedral. "We will rebuild it. All together."
89. Nobody died. Of nearly five hundred firefighters, one was slightly hurt, along with two police officers.
90. And the twenty to forty Gallet was told were trapped? | Every one of them got out.
91. But saved isn't the same as safe. | Above the nave, the stone itself was still taking hits, and the night wasn't over.

## 5. WHAT HELD
92. Under the roof, Notre-Dame has a second ceiling, made of stone.
93. Jacques Heyman, an engineer, wrote that one of this vault's jobs is to be a fire-resistant barrier between roof and church.
94. When the burning timbers came down, he wrote, they were largely caught by the vaults.
95. It gave way in only three places. At the crossing, in one bay of the nave, and at the end of the north transept.
96. And here's the detail. The official survey found that every hole was punched by falling timber, not burned through by fire.
97. Then, in the night, says the culture ministry, the spire's burnt wooden base finally gave way. | And it took the vault over the crossing down with it.
98. And the frame in the north tower? Afterwards, surveyors found some beams charred, not enough to threaten it. | Two of the eight bells were damaged by the heat.
99. That's the frame the tower team left behind, after putting out the fire beside it.
100. So was Notre-Dame thirty minutes from collapse? | That was the judgement of the people in charge that night. Nobody can rerun the night to check.
101. All of the stained glass survived, including both thirteenth-century rose windows. So did the great organ, coated in lead dust.
102. The walls had their own scare. Witnesses saw the gable at the end of the north transept tip toward the street, then settle back.
103. It was left with cracks running across it and down it.
104. Without the roof's weight on top, the flying buttresses were pushing the walls in. | So under all twenty-eight of them, carpenters fitted giant wooden arches, each over eight tonnes.
105. In the rubble, the next day, someone found the spire's copper rooster. Dented, but probably restorable. The diocese later confirmed the relics inside were intact.
106. The Crown of Thorns had gone out the door the night before, carried by firefighters, police, officials and the brigade's chaplain.
107. A human chain passed out paintings and treasures, and the treasures spent the night at city hall.
108. The building held. | But the eight bells that hung over all of it still had to come down.

## 6. THE BELLS COME HOME
109. First, how it started. | More than seven years later, the cause is still undetermined. No one has been charged.
110. The prosecutor said early on that nothing pointed to a deliberate act.
111. The leading hypotheses are both accidents: a cigarette not put out, or an electrical fault. | Neither has ever been proved.
112. What the record does show is a system. One message, one rule, one plan. Each made sense on its own. | Together, they made the first half hour.
113. The new roof frames are oak again, squared by hand with axes, from well over a thousand trees.
114. Philippe Jost, head of the rebuilding agency, said in 2024 that every wooden frame would have a misting system.
115. And he chose his words with care.
116. "I believe we can say that the fire of 2019 would no longer be possible."
117. In July 2023, the eight bells were lowered from the north tower.
118. They were blessed in September 2024, and hung back in their frame that October.
119. On the eighth of November, 2024, just before half past ten in the morning, they rang. | For the first time since the fire.
120. A month later, Notre-Dame reopened.
121. In 2019, one message could be read two ways, and the first half hour went to the wrong attic.
122. That November morning, nobody had to ask where the sound was coming from.
123. The north tower. | The one they climbed into.
