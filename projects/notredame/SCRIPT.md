## COLD OPEN
1. Six-eighteen, April 2019.
2. Beside Notre-Dame, a screen flashes: | Feu. Fire.
3. The alarm worked. | But its message pointed to two attics.
4. The fire brigade was called late. | Clock one: the lost time.
5. Then fire reached the north tower's bell frame. | Clock two: the fire chief's half hour to save the tower.
6. Lose that tower, officials feared, and the cathedral could collapse.
7. A minister said saving it had all come down to fifteen to thirty minutes.
8. So who climbed into that burning tower, and what was waiting at the top?

## 1. TWO ATTICS, ONE MESSAGE
9. It's Holy Week. Inside the cathedral, evening Mass is under way.
10. About forty metres above the congregation sits the roof frame. It has so many oak beams, it's called the forest.
11. Guarding it is a smoke alarm six years in the making: thin tubes that sniff the attic air.
13. At six-eighteen, one detector finds smoke.
14. Next door, in the security post, the screen lights up. Feu. Then a zone: attic, nave, sacristy.
15. Last come two technical words, aspirating framework: the cathedral's own roof frame. | But the zone named before them covered two attics.
17. One was the cathedral's roof. The other belonged to the sacristy, a smaller building attached to its side.
18. And by design, the system didn't call the fire brigade itself. | The plan put one person at the screen, and that person's job was to radio a guard to check first.
20. Now put yourself on that radio. | Four strings of jargon, the key words last. | The New York Times called it nearly indecipherable.
21. The guard is sent up to the attic of the sacristy. | Nothing to report.
22. In the other attic, nobody is looking yet. | And the smoke is becoming a fire.
24. Down in the nave, the Mass is stopped and the cathedral is emptied.
27. Six forty-three. A second alert, and a phone call that sends the guard to the other attic.
28. When that alert came, clock one already read twenty-five minutes. | And the fire brigade still hadn't been called.
29. About three hundred narrow steps up into the roof. | About five minutes of climbing.
30. At the top, the forest is on fire.
31. The fire brigade gets the call at about ten to seven. | Half an hour after the screen first said fire. | The lost half hour.
32. The first crews are minutes away. | They're about to climb into the forest.

## 2. A WALL OF FLAME
33. Just before seven, the first fire engines pull up. The crews drag their hoses into the burning roof.
34. Up there: an unstoppable wall of flame.
35. They're forced back.
37. The forest was covered in lead: | about four hundred and sixty tonnes, roof and spire together.
38. As the oak burns away under it, the lead melts, and runs out over the top of the stone vaults.
40. Four days earlier, a crane had lifted sixteen copper statues off the spire for restoration. | Saved by a work schedule.
41. Other treasures were saved by hand. | That night, firefighters, police and officials got the Crown of Thorns out. | The brigade's chaplain took the Blessed Sacrament himself, and a human chain handed the rest to safety.
39. The spire itself is from 1859: oak, sheathed in lead, ninety-six metres to the copper rooster on top. | And now it's on fire.
42. About seven-forty, President Macron's office postpones the televised address he has already recorded.
43. A little before eight, in front of the crowds and the live cameras, the spire falls.
44. It drops straight into the burning roof.
45. Then word reaches General Jean-Claude Gallet, commander of the Paris fire brigade. | As he later recalled it, between twenty and forty firefighters are trapped inside.
46. Their way back out is full of flames.

## 3. THE WRONG FIX
47. The cameras were all on the spire.
48. But the spire was not the danger the people in charge feared most.
49. And the fix so many people were calling for, officials warned, could have brought the whole thing down.
50. Online, plenty of people had an answer. So did the American president.
51. He wrote: "Perhaps flying water tankers could be used to put it out. Must act quickly!"
52. You've seen those planes drop water on wildfires.
53. France's civil protection service answered that same night, without naming anyone.
54. All means were being used, they wrote, except water-bombing aircraft. | Those, they warned, could lead to the collapse of the entire structure.
55. The weight of the water, dropped from low altitude, could weaken the building and damage the buildings around it.
56. So the fight stays on the ground, one hose at a time.
57. And the firefighters trapped inside? | They would all come out alive.
58. But the fire is heading for the part the people in charge feared most.
59. Look at the front of Notre-Dame. Two towers, sixty-nine metres tall.
60. In the north tower, eight bells hang in a wooden frame.
61. They range from about three-quarters of a tonne to more than four tonnes.
62. By then, The New York Times reports, the north tower is already on fire.
63. The bells were never the danger. | The burning oak holding them up was.
64. Here's the chain they feared. The frame burns through. The bells fall. The north tower goes. | And that could take the rest of the cathedral with it.
66. A culture ministry fire expert put it this way. Lose the war of the bell towers, he said, and you lose the cathedral.
67. Gallet admitted that night he wasn't sure they could stop the fire spreading into the bell frame. | The only way to find out was to climb into it.

## 4. THE SECOND CLOCK
68. The roof was gone. | Now Gallet had to decide whether to send a team up into a burning tower, under eight bells.
69. Two men in that briefing room later told the story: the district mayor, Ariel Weil, and Gallet himself. | Weil remembers Gallet telling the room he would soon know whether the cathedral was lost.
70. "We knew what he meant. | He meant Notre-Dame could collapse."
71. By his own account, Gallet put two options to President Macron. | "Do nothing, and by five in the morning, we won't see the two towers any more."
72. Or try something he wasn't sure would work: send a team into the north tower, and do it within the half hour.
73. Now you're the one deciding. | Send them in, and you might lose them. | Hold back, and you might lose both towers by morning. | Make your call.
74. The president's answer, as Gallet remembers it: | "OK. Go ahead, General." | Clock two starts: Gallet's half hour.
76. About twenty go in. | It was clear, Weil said later, that some might not come back out.
78. Nine-forty. The deputy interior minister, Laurent Nuñez, tells the cameras the fire is not contained, | and saving the cathedral is not certain.
79. First problem: water. | One of the cathedral's own pipes was leaking, and the pressure was dropping.
80. Embers the size of your thumb were burning holes in some of the hoses. | The New York Times later traced what came next.
81. So the team brings its own water. | Two hoses, fed straight from a fire engine, go up the stairs of the south tower. | Gallet's half hour is running.
82. High up, they cross into the north tower. | They break through a gate.
83. Part of a wall is burning. | So is the floor.
84. They climb on, to the height of the bells.
85. Eight bells hang above them, the biggest more than four tonnes. | The fire has reached the frame that holds them, the government's timeline says.
86. They turn the hoses on the wall. | Then the floor. | One by one, those flames go out.
86a. Then more water, at the very top of the towers, | aimed at the fire in the frame itself.
87. Then another brigade general, Jean-Marie Gontier, goes up to look. | He comes back down with three words. | "She is saved." | Elle est sauvée.
88. It's about ten at night. | By the culture ministry's account, about half an hour since the go-ahead.
93. Nobody died. | Not in the tower, not anywhere in the cathedral.
94. Of nearly five hundred firefighters, one was slightly hurt, along with two police officers.
88a. But how close had Notre-Dame come? | The answer was still up in that tower, and surveyors took months to read it.
95. Saved isn't the same as safe. | Below where the roof had been was a second ceiling: a stone vault. | Burning pieces of the roof had been falling onto it.

## 5. WHAT HELD
97. That vault had a second job, the engineer Jacques Heyman wrote: | a fire barrier between the roof and the church.
98. When the burning timbers came down, they were largely caught by the vaults.
99. The vault gave way in three places. | Where the arms of the cross meet, in one bay of the nave, and at the end of the north arm.
100. And here's the detail. The official survey found that every hole was punched by falling timber, not burned through by fire.
102. And the bell frame? | The survey found the flames had attacked one corner of it, and burned the stair to the upper bells.
103. Some beams were charred, | but not enough, the survey found, to threaten the frame. | Two of the eight bells were scarred by the heat. | The fire had reached the frame, | and the frame had held.
104. So was Notre-Dame thirty minutes from collapse? | The people in charge believed it was that close, and said so.
105. Gallet says he had warned that, left alone, both towers would be gone by morning. | The next day, Laurent Nuñez said it had all come down to fifteen to thirty minutes. | Three months later, The New York Times concluded it came far closer to collapsing than people knew.
105a. Nobody can measure a margin that was never used up. | Because nobody waited to find out.
107. The walls had their own scare. Witnesses saw the gable at the end of the north arm tip toward the street, then settle back.
109. Without the roof's weight on top, the flying buttresses were pushing the walls in. | So carpenters fitted giant wooden arches under all twenty-eight buttresses. | Each weighs more than eight tonnes.
110. In the rubble, the next day, someone found the spire's copper rooster. Dented, but probably restorable. | The diocese later confirmed the relics inside were intact.
111. The building held.
112. The tower they saved was still standing. | But its bells would stay silent for more than five years.

## 6. THE BELLS COME HOME
113. In July 2023, the eight bells came down from the north tower. | The frame that had held them through the fire had to be restored, and the bells cleaned of lead dust.
114. The investigation is still open. | More than seven years on, no cause has been proved, and no one has been charged. | The leading theories are accidents: a cigarette, or an electrical fault.
117. What the record does show is a system. | Two attics in one message. A rule to check before calling. | And an old oak roof with no sprinklers, which, The New York Times reported, the plan expected to burn slowly.
117b. The first two help explain the lost half hour. | The third is why the plan thought there was time to spare. | The search had begun in the wrong attic.
118. Every wooden frame now has a misting system, said Philippe Jost, who led the rebuilding. | "I believe we can say," he added, "that the fire of 2019 would no longer be possible."
119. The bells were blessed in September 2024 and hung back in their frame that October, weeks before the cathedral reopened.
120. One November morning in 2024, all eight rang together. | For the first time since the fire.
125. From the north tower. | The one they climbed into.
