## COLD OPEN
1. Six-eighteen, a Monday evening, April 2019.
2. Beside Notre-Dame, a screen flashes: | Feu. Fire.
3. The alarm worked. | But its message pointed to two attics, and the rule said check first.
4. Firefighters were called about half an hour later. | That's clock one.
5. Then fire reached the frame holding the north tower's bells. | Clock two.
6. Lose that tower, officials feared, and the cathedral could collapse.
7. A minister called it a matter of fifteen to thirty critical minutes.
8. So who climbed into that tower, and what did they find?

## 1. TWO ATTICS, ONE MESSAGE
9. It's Holy Week. Inside the cathedral, evening Mass is under way.
10. About forty metres above the congregation sits the roof frame. It has so many oak beams, it's called the forest.
11. Guarding it is a smoke alarm six years in the making: thin tubes that sniff the attic air.
13. At six-eighteen, one detector finds smoke.
14. In the security post, in a small building next door, the screen lights up. Feu. Then a zone: attic, nave, sacristy.
15. Last come two technical words: aspirating framework.
16. Those last words meant the roof frame of the cathedral itself. | But the zone named before them covered two attics.
17. One was the cathedral's roof. The other belonged to the sacristy, a smaller building attached to its side.
18. And the system didn't call the fire brigade by itself. By design, a person had to check first.
19. The screen was staffed by one person, a few days into the job. A second person, a guard, did the checking by radio.
20. Now put yourself on that radio. | A message The New York Times called nearly indecipherable, key words last. And a rule: check before you call.
21. The guard is sent up to the attic of the sacristy. | Nothing to report.
22. In the other attic, nobody is looking yet.
23. What exactly the guard was told is still disputed. The security firm and the cathedral tell it differently.
24. Down in the nave, the Mass is stopped and the cathedral is emptied.
27. Six forty-three. A second alert, and a phone call that sends the guard to the other attic.
28. When that alert came, clock one already read twenty-five minutes. | And the fire brigade still hadn't been called.
29. About three hundred narrow steps up into the roof. | About five minutes of climbing.
30. At the top, the forest is on fire.
31. The New York Times puts the call at six forty-eight. The French government's record says the alert came in at six fifty-one. | About half an hour after the screen said Feu. | The lost half hour.
32. The first crews are minutes away. | They're about to climb into the forest.

## 2. A WALL OF FLAME
33. Just before seven, the first fire engines pull up. The crews drag their hoses into the burning roof.
34. At the top, an unstoppable wall of flame.
35. They're forced back.
37. The forest was covered in lead. Two hundred and ten tonnes on the roof, and two hundred and fifty more on the spire.
38. As the oak burns away under it, the lead melts, and runs out over the top of the stone vaults.
39. The spire itself is from 1859: oak, sheathed in lead, ninety-six metres to the tip of its rooster. | And now it's on fire.
40. Four days earlier, a crane had lifted the spire's sixteen copper statues away for restoration. | Saved by a work schedule.
41. Other treasures were saved by hand. | That night, firefighters, police, officials and the brigade's chaplain got the Crown of Thorns out. A human chain carried out the rest.
42. About seven-forty, the president's office postpones his televised address.
43. A little before eight, in front of the crowds and the live cameras, the spire falls.
44. It drops straight into the burning roof.
45. Then word reaches General Jean-Claude Gallet, the brigade's commander. | Between twenty and forty firefighters are trapped inside the cathedral.
46. Their way back out is full of flames.

## 3. NO PLANES
47. Here's what most people watching didn't know.
48. The spire was not the danger the people in charge feared most.
49. And the obvious fix, officials warned, could have brought the whole thing down.
50. Plenty of people watching had an answer. So did the American president.
51. He wrote: "Perhaps flying water tankers could be used to put it out. Must act quickly!"
52. You've seen those planes drop water on wildfires.
53. France's civil protection service answered that same night, without naming anyone.
54. All means were being used, they wrote, except water-bombing aircraft. | Those, they warned, could lead to the collapse of the entire structure.
55. The weight of the water, dropped from low altitude, could weaken the structure and damage the buildings next door.
56. So the fight stays on the ground, one hose at a time.
57. And the firefighters trapped inside? | They would all come out alive.
58. But the fire is heading for the part the people in charge feared most.
59. Look at the front of Notre-Dame. Two towers, sixty-nine metres tall.
60. In the north tower, eight bells hang in a wooden frame.
61. They range from about three-quarters of a tonne to more than four tonnes.
62. By then, The New York Times reports, the north tower is already on fire.
63. The bells were never the danger. | The burning oak holding them up was.
64. Here's the chain they feared. The frame burns through. The bells fall. The north tower goes. | And that could take the rest of the cathedral with it.
66. A culture ministry fire expert put it this way. Lose the war of the bell towers, he said, and you lose the cathedral.
67. Gallet admitted that night he wasn't sure they could stop the fire spreading into the bell frame. | The only way to find out was to climb into it.

## 4. THE SECOND CLOCK
68. The roof was lost. | At police headquarters, Gallet now faced a choice: send firefighters into a burning bell tower, or hold back.
69. The district mayor, Ariel Weil, was in the room. | He remembers Gallet's words: soon, he would know if they had lost it.
70. "We knew what he meant," Weil said later. | "He meant Notre-Dame could collapse."
71. Gallet later recalled the two options he gave the president. | Do nothing, he said, and by five in the morning, we won't see the two towers any more.
72. Or try something he wasn't sure would work: send a team into the north tower, and do it within the half hour.
73. Now you're the one deciding. | Send them in, and you might lose them. | Hold back, and you might lose both towers by morning. | Make your call.
74. The culture ministry puts the go-ahead at nine-thirty. | Clock two starts: Gallet's half hour.
76. About twenty went in. | Weil remembers it was clear some of them might not come back out.
77. Nine thirty-six. The French government's log: the roof is ablaze, and the flames have reached the bell frame.
78. Four minutes later, the deputy interior minister, Laurent Nuñez, tells the cameras the fire is not contained. | Saving the cathedral, he says, is not certain.
79. What happened next comes from a reconstruction by The New York Times. | Water is a problem. One of the cathedral's own pipes is leaking, and the pressure is dropping.
80. Embers the size of your thumb are burning holes in some of the hoses.
81. So the team brings its own water, and Gallet's half hour is running. | Two hoses, fed straight from a fire engine, dragged up the stairs of the south tower.
82. At the top, they cross into the north tower. | They break through a gate.
83. Part of a wall is burning. | So is the floor.
84. They climb on, to the height of the bells.
85. Eight bells hang above them, the biggest more than four tonnes. | And the fire is already in the frame that holds them.
86. They turn the hoses on the wall. | Then the floor. | One by one, the flames go out.
87. Then General Jean-Marie Gontier goes up to look. | He comes back down with three words. | "She is saved." | Elle est sauvée.
88. It's around ten at night.
88a. But was it really fifteen to thirty minutes from collapse? | The evidence was still up there, and surveyors took months to read it.
93. Nobody died. | Not in the tower, not anywhere in the cathedral.
94. Of nearly five hundred firefighters, one was slightly hurt, along with two police officers.
95. But saved isn't the same as safe. | Under that roof was a second ceiling, made of stone, and it had been taking hits all along.

## 5. WHAT HELD
97. The engineer Jacques Heyman wrote that one job of this vault is to act as a fire barrier between roof and church.
98. When the burning timbers came down, he wrote, they were largely caught by the vaults.
99. The vault gave way in only three places. At the crossing, in one bay of the nave, and at the end of the north transept.
100. And here's the detail. The official survey found that every hole was punched by falling timber, not burned through by fire.
101. When the crossing vault fell is disputed: with the spire, Gallet remembered; later that night, says the culture ministry.
102. And the bell frame in the north tower? | Afterwards, the official survey found the flames had reached one corner of it, and burned the stair to the upper bells.
103. Beams were charred, but the frame stayed stable. | Two of the eight bells were damaged by the heat.
104. So was Notre-Dame thirty minutes from collapse? | By his own account, the fire commander had warned that, left alone, both towers would be gone by morning.
105. The minister put the margin at fifteen to thirty minutes. | It was an estimate made that night, not a measurement. | The people at that table had to decide without one.
106. Every stained-glass window survived. So did the great organ, under a coat of lead dust.
107. The walls had their own scare. Witnesses saw the gable at the end of the north transept tip toward the street, then settle back.
108. It was left cracked, across and down.
109. Without the roof's weight on top, the flying buttresses were pushing the walls in. | So under all twenty-eight of them, carpenters fitted giant wooden arches, each over eight tonnes.
110. In the rubble, the next day, someone found the spire's copper rooster. Dented, but probably restorable. | The diocese later confirmed the relics inside were intact.
111. The building held.
112. But the bells in the tower they saved would stay silent for more than five years.

## 6. THE BELLS COME HOME
113. In July 2023, the eight bells were lowered from the north tower.
114. While they were away, the investigation stayed open. | More than seven years on, the cause is still undetermined, and no one has been charged.
116. The leading hypotheses are accidents: a cigarette not put out, or an electrical fault. | Neither has been proved.
116a. What the record does show is a system.
117. One message that pointed to two attics. One rule: check before you call.
117a. And one plan, The New York Times reported, that expected old oak to burn slowly, under a roof with no sprinklers.
117b. Together, they help explain that first half hour. | The New York Times called that lost time critical. | And it began in the wrong attic.
118. Philippe Jost, who led the rebuild, said every wooden frame would get a misting system. | "I believe we can say," he said, "that the fire of 2019 would no longer be possible."
119. The bells were blessed in September 2024 and hung back in their frame that October, weeks before the cathedral reopened.
120. On the morning of the eighth of November, 2024, they rang. | For the first time since the fire.
124. This time, nobody had to ask where to look.
125. The north tower. | The one they climbed into.
