## COLD OPEN
1. Paris, April 2019. Six-eighteen on a Monday evening.
2. Beside Notre-Dame, a screen flashes one word. | Feu. Fire.
3. The alarm worked. | But its zone held two attics, and the rule said check first.
4. The fire brigade was called about half an hour later.
5. After the spire fell, a second clock started, on the north tower's burning bell frame.
6. Lose that tower, officials feared, and the cathedral could follow.
7. A French minister put the margin at fifteen to thirty minutes.
8. So what did it take to save that tower?

## 1. TWO ATTICS, ONE MESSAGE
9. It's Holy Week. Inside the cathedral, evening Mass is under way.
10. About forty metres above the congregation sits the roof frame. It has so many oak beams, it's called the forest.
11. Guarding it is a fire alarm that took dozens of experts six years to put together.
12. Thin tubes run through the attic, drawing in air and testing it for smoke. More than a hundred and sixty detectors and alarms cover the site.
13. At six-eighteen, one of them finds smoke.
14. In the security post, in a small building next door, the screen lights up. Feu. Then a zone: attic, nave, sacristy.
15. Last come two technical words: aspirating framework.
16. Those last words pointed to the cathedral's own roof. | But the zone, the first place named on the screen, covered two attics.
17. The nave attic is the cathedral's roof. The sacristy is a smaller building attached to its side, with an attic of its own.
18. And the system didn't call the fire brigade by itself. By design, a person had to check first.
19. One person watched the screen, a few days into the job. A guard, on the radio, did the checking.
20. Now put yourself on that radio. | A message written for engineers, key words last. And a rule: check before you call.
21. The guard is sent up to the attic of the sacristy. | Nothing to report.
22. Above the other attic, nobody is looking yet.
23. What exactly reached the guard is still disputed. The security firm says the location was passed on. The cathedral says its guards heard attic, nave, sacristy, and nothing more.
24. Meanwhile, the Mass is stopped and the cathedral is emptied.
25. Days later, The New York Times reported the plan behind the alarm. It expected old, dense oak to burn slowly.
26. Even at best, by the paper's sums, firefighting gear needed about twenty minutes to reach the attic. | And the roof had no sprinklers and no fire walls.
27. Six forty-three. A second alert, and a phone call that sends the guard to the other attic.
28. When that alert came, our first clock already read twenty-five minutes. | And the fire brigade still hadn't been called.
29. About three hundred narrow steps up into the roof.
30. At the top, the forest is on fire.
31. The New York Times puts the call at six forty-eight. The French government's record says the alert came in at six fifty-one. | About half an hour after the screen said Feu. | The lost half hour.
32. The first crews are minutes away. | They'll take the stairs straight up, into the roof.

## 2. A WALL OF FLAME
33. Just before seven, the first fire engines pull up. The crews run their hoses up, into the burning roof.
34. The New York Times described what met them there as an unstoppable wall of flame.
35. They're forced to retreat.
36. Two fireboats on the Seine feed the lines. Through the night, nearly five hundred firefighters and more than seventy vehicles join the fight.
37. The forest was covered in lead. Two hundred and ten tonnes on the roof, and two hundred and fifty more on the spire.
38. As the oak burns away under it, the lead melts, and runs out over the top of the stone vaults.
39. The spire itself is from 1859. Oak, sheathed in lead, ninety-six metres to the tip of its rooster.
40. Four days earlier, a crane had lifted its sixteen copper statues away, for a restoration already on the calendar. | They were saved by a work schedule.
41. Others were saved by hand. That night, firefighters, police, officials and the brigade's chaplain got the Crown of Thorns out. A human chain carried out other treasures.
42. About seven-forty, the president's office postpones his televised address.
43. A little before eight, in front of the crowds and the live cameras, the spire falls.
44. It drops straight into the burning roof.
45. Then word reaches General Jean-Claude Gallet, the brigade's commander. | Between twenty and forty firefighters are trapped inside the cathedral.
46. Their way back out is full of flames.

## 3. NO PLANES
47. Here's what most people watching didn't know.
48. The spire was not the danger the people in charge feared most.
49. And the obvious fix, officials warned, could have brought the whole thing down.
50. Millions watching had an answer. So did the American president.
51. He wrote: "Perhaps flying water tankers could be used to put it out. Must act quickly!"
52. You've seen those planes drop water on forest fires.
53. France's civil protection service answered that same night, without naming anyone.
54. All means were being used, they wrote, except water-bombing aircraft. | Those, they warned, could lead to the collapse of the entire structure.
55. The weight of the water, dropped from low altitude, could weaken the structure and damage the buildings next door.
56. So the fight stays on the ground, one hose at a time.
57. And the firefighters trapped inside? | Not one of them would die that night.
58. But the fire is heading for the part the people in charge feared most.
59. Look at the front of Notre-Dame. Two towers, sixty-nine metres tall.
60. In the north tower, eight bells hang in a wooden frame.
61. They range from about three-quarters of a tonne to more than four tonnes.
62. By about half past eight, The New York Times reports, the north tower is already on fire.
63. The bells were never the danger. | The burning oak holding them up was.
64. Here's the chain the people in charge feared. The frame burns through. The bells fall. The north tower goes. | And that could take the rest of the cathedral with it.
65. Gallet put it plainly: if the bells fall, this tower falls.
66. A culture ministry fire expert went further. Lose the war of the bell towers, he said, and you lose the cathedral.
67. Gallet admitted that night he wasn't sure they could stop the fire spreading into the bell frame. | The only way to find out was to climb into it.

## 4. THE SECOND CLOCK
68. The roof was lost. | At police headquarters, Gallet told the officials he would fight for the towers.
69. The district mayor, Ariel Weil, was in the room. As he remembers it, Gallet said he'd soon know if they had lost it.
70. "We knew what he meant," Weil said later. | "He meant Notre-Dame could collapse."
71. Gallet later recalled the two options he gave the president. | Do nothing, and by five in the morning, we won't see the two towers any more.
72. Or try a manoeuvre he isn't sure will work: send a team into the north tower, within the half hour.
73. Now you're the one deciding. | Send them in, and you might lose them. | Hold back, and you might lose both towers by morning. | Make your call.
74. At half past nine, by the culture ministry's account, the plan is approved. | A second clock starts: Gallet's half hour.
75. Weil remembers it was clear some firefighters would go in without knowing if they'd come back out.
76. About twenty went in.
77. Nine thirty-six. The French government's log: the roof is ablaze, and the flames have reached the bell frame.
78. Four minutes later, the deputy interior minister, Laurent Nuñez, tells the cameras the fire is not contained. | Saving the cathedral, he says, is not certain.
79. What happened next comes from The New York Times' reconstruction. | The water is failing. One of the cathedral's own pipes is leaking, and the pressure is dropping.
80. Embers the size of your thumb are burning holes in some of the hoses.
81. So the team brings its own. Two hoses, fed straight from a fire truck, dragged up the stairs of the south tower.
82. At the top, they cross into the north tower, at the height of the bells. | They break through a gate.
83. Part of a wall is burning. | So is the floor under their feet.
84. The fire has got into one corner of the oak bell frame. The stair to the upper bells has burned.
85. Eight bells hang over the team, the biggest over four tonnes. | Beneath the bells, the oak that holds them is on fire.
86. They turn the hoses on the wall. | Then the floor. | One by one, the flames go out.
87. Then another general, Jean-Marie Gontier, goes up to look. He comes back down with three words. | "She is saved." | Elle est sauvée.
88. It's around ten at night. | Gallet's half hour is nearly up.
89. The next day, Nuñez gave his estimate of the margin. | Between fifteen and thirty minutes.
90. That's an estimate, not a measurement. | And he didn't say which minutes he meant.
91. About the lost half hour at the start, The New York Times was blunt. | All that time lost, it wrote, became critical.
92. General Gontier compared it to starting a four-hundred-metre race several dozen metres behind.
93. Nobody died.
94. Of nearly five hundred firefighters, one was slightly hurt, along with two police officers.
95. But saved isn't the same as safe. | Above the nave, the stone itself was still taking hits, and the night wasn't over.

## 5. WHAT HELD
96. Under the roof, Notre-Dame has a second ceiling, made of stone.
97. The engineer Jacques Heyman wrote that one job of this vault is to act as a fire barrier between roof and church.
98. When the burning timbers came down, he wrote, they were largely caught by the vaults.
99. The vault gave way in only three places. At the crossing, in one bay of the nave, and at the end of the north transept.
100. And here's the detail. The official survey found that every hole was punched by falling timber, not burned through by fire.
101. And the vault over the crossing? When it fell is disputed. | Gallet remembered it coming down with the spire. The culture ministry says the spire's burnt wooden base gave way later, in the night, and took it down.
102. And the frame in the north tower? | The tower team had put out the fire beside it.
103. Afterwards, surveyors found charred beams, but not enough to threaten it. | Two of the eight bells were damaged by the heat.
104. So was Notre-Dame thirty minutes from collapse? | The fire commander had warned that, left alone, both towers would be gone by morning.
105. The minister put the margin at fifteen to thirty minutes. | Nobody measured it. | They were the ones who had to decide.
106. All of the stained glass survived, including both thirteenth-century transept rose windows. The great organ was spared by fire and water, and coated in lead dust.
107. The walls had their own scare. Witnesses saw the gable at the end of the north transept tip toward the street, then settle back.
108. It was left cracked, across and down.
109. Without the roof's weight on top, the flying buttresses were pushing the walls in. | So under all twenty-eight of them, carpenters fitted giant wooden arches, each over eight tonnes.
110. In the rubble, the next day, someone found the spire's copper rooster. Dented, but probably restorable. | The diocese later confirmed the relics inside were intact.
111. The building held.
112. But the bells in the tower they saved would stay silent for more than five years.

## 6. THE BELLS COME HOME
113. In July 2023, the eight bells were lowered from the north tower.
114. While they were away, the investigation stayed open. | More than seven years on, the cause is still undetermined. No one has been charged.
115. The prosecutor said early on that nothing pointed to a deliberate act.
116. The leading hypotheses are both accidents: a cigarette not put out, or an electrical fault. | Neither has ever been proved.
117. What the record does show is a system. One message, one rule, one plan. Each made sense on its own. | Together, they cost that first half hour.
118. Philippe Jost, head of the rebuilding agency, said in 2024 that every wooden frame would have a misting system. | And he chose his words with care: "I believe we can say that the fire of 2019 would no longer be possible."
119. The bells were blessed in September 2024, and hung back in their frame that October.
120. On the eighth of November, 2024, just before half past ten in the morning, they rang. | For the first time since the fire.
121. A month later, Notre-Dame reopened.
122. In 2019, one message could be read two ways.
123. And the first half hour began in the wrong attic.
124. That November morning, nobody had to ask where the sound was coming from.
125. The north tower. | The one they climbed into.
