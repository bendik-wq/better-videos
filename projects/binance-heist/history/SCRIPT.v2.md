# The Job Interview That Cost $6.3 Billion: narration v2

**Structure:** the countdown (`script-patterns.md` §1.4). One laptop followed through five months, with an on-screen clock (T–5 MONTHS … T–0 … T+5 DAYS) on every chapter card. Runner-up: the investigation; it lost because the "who" is already named by the FBI, so the open question is "how", which a clock tells best.

**Look:** night mode (style bible). Work light on a desk for everything human; fluorescent green-black for the inside of Binance's systems; red only for annotation.

**Length:** see the critic run in LOOP.md (150 wpm, VO only). The sponsor read adds 45–75 s on top.

**Re-hooks:** `[↺]` marks one; target one every 30–40 s.

**Sponsor:** one slot after the Ch.2 reveal, about 3:00, marked `[SPONSOR SLOT — offer TBD]`. No copy yet. The first line after it is written as the strongest re-hook in the film (it is where skippers land).

**Fact rules:**
- Every fact is in SOURCES.md (IDs in the fact map at the bottom).
- ⏱ = everything. This is a six-day-old story; re-check every number on publish day.
- Binance's account is a company claim: "Binance says", "according to Binance's report".
- North Korea is an F.B.I. attribution, denied by North Korea. No one is charged. Never "North Korea stole".
- The engineers are private people: only Binance's own role labels, no faces, no blame.
- Engineer B's team: the report's label is "Software Engineer B", while A and C are labelled "Wallet …". The VO only ever says what the label says ("no 'wallet' in the title"), never that B was on or off a team.
- The "permission slip" reading of the take-home test is our interpretation and is spoken as one ("Here's how we read it").

### TITLE CARD · THE JOB INTERVIEW THAT COST SIX POINT THREE BILLION DOLLARS

## 1. CAUGHT ONCE (T–5 MONTHS · JUNE)

<!-- beat: the ad | emotion: unease 2 | kind: setup | link: therefore -->
12. June. According to Binance, an engineer on the team that runs its wallets clicks an online ad.
13. It leads to a tampered copy of a popular developer tool. The engineer downloads it. [↺]
14. It's an old trick. Poison something developers already trust, then advertise it to them.

<!-- beat: caught | emotion: tension 3 | kind: complication | link: but -->
15. But this time, someone notices. | Binance catches it.
16. The company says it detected the tool, replaced the computer and changed the credentials. [↺]
17. So there are only two possibilities. Either Binance missed something. | Or catching it didn't matter.

<!-- beat: the machine | emotion: curiosity 2 | plant: P3 | kind: setup | link: therefore -->
18. To see which, you need to know what that engineer's team looks after.
19. An exchange keeps most of its customers' coins offline. Slow to move. Hard to steal.
20. But some coins have to stay online, so withdrawals can happen in seconds. That's the hot wallet. [↺]
21. A hot wallet only moves when a computer signs off on it. A signing service. | Automatic. Fast. Trusted.
22. Keep that machine in mind. Everything in this story is trying to reach it.

<!-- beat: the map | emotion: surprise 4 | close: L2 | open: L3 | plant: P2 | kind: reversal | link: but -->
23. So what did the attackers get in June? Not a password. Binance had already changed those. [↺]
24. By Binance's own account, they had learned something more useful. Who was on the wallet team. | And what each person did.
25. > You can change a password. | You can't change who works for you.
26. So it was the second possibility. Catching the laptop didn't matter.
27. The next step needed one person at Binance to run the attackers' code. On purpose. [↺]
28. Who opens a stranger's file, on purpose? Someone careless? | Or someone who has been told to?

## 2. THE INTERVIEW (T–7 WEEKS · SEPTEMBER)

<!-- beat: the recruiter | emotion: intrigue 3 | open: L4 | kind: complication | link: therefore -->
29. September. Binance says a recruiter reaches out to one of its engineers, on a real job-hunting platform.
30. Here is how the report puts it.
31. > "Software Engineer B was contacted through a legitimate job-hunting platform by an individual posing as a Project Director." [↺]
32. Notice the label. Not Wallet Engineer. | Software Engineer B.
33. The attackers had just learned who was on the wallet team. So why go after someone with no "wallet" in the title?

<!-- beat: the company | emotion: surprise 4 | kind: revelation | link: but -->
34. The hiring process looks normal. A first-round interview on a live video call, with someone presenting as H.R.
35. They even share a social media account. The second round is with the company's chief executive, on a chat app. [↺]
36. The job site was real. The video call was real.
37. But according to Binance, the company didn't exist. | And the chief executive was a character.

<!-- beat: the folder | emotion: intrigue 3 | close: L3 | plant: P4 | kind: revelation | link: but -->
38. So far, a fake company has touched nothing at Binance. It's just a story. | Until the last step of every developer interview. [↺]
39. The engineer was asked to download a coding assignment from an online code repository.
40. If you're not a programmer, that might sound odd. If you are, it sounds like any other Tuesday.
41. Take-home tests are a normal part of hiring developers. You download the project. You run it. That's the point.
42. > It's the one moment a careful engineer is *told* to run a stranger's code. [↺]

<!-- beat: not detected | emotion: dread 4 | kind: escalation | link: therefore -->
43. The folder ran. And according to Binance, something else ran with it.
44. Custom malware, never seen before. The report says plainly:
45. > "The zero-day malware was not detected by the endpoint security protection tools at the time." [↺]
46. It hid inside a tool programmers use every day. It deleted a real program called pie-env, and slipped its own copy into its place.
47. Nothing crashed. Nothing looked wrong. | The laptop now answered to someone else.

<!-- beat: your chair | emotion: empathy 3 | kind: release | link: but -->
48. It's easy to blame the engineer. Look at it from that chair instead.
49. Every step was the step you'd expect. If you've ever applied for a job online, you've done most of it yourself. [↺]
50. You do everything the process asks. And your laptop becomes the way in to the largest crypto theft on record.
51. That's what it can cost.
52. Binance's report gives the engineers letters, not names. Asked by Fortune, Binance declined to say whether they would keep their jobs.

<!-- beat: one laptop | emotion: curiosity 2 | kind: complication | link: but -->
53. But one engineer's laptop still isn't the wallet. Binance's money doesn't move because one developer's machine says so. [↺]
54. And this is the engineer with no "wallet" in the title.
55. On paper, the attackers just spent a fake company on the wrong engineer. | Or did they?

## 3. THE DOOR (T–9 DAYS · 20 OCTOBER)

<!-- beat: the helper | emotion: tension 3 | open: L5 | kind: setup | link: therefore -->
56. From one laptop, there are two ways to the money. Steal the keys. | Or steal the people who hold them.
57. Like a lot of companies now, Binance had its own internal A.I. tool. According to its report, the attackers used it. [↺]
58. Binance says the tool played only a supporting part. But it played one.
59. By the twentieth of October, they had used the engineer's laptop and that A.I. tool to take over two more computers.

<!-- beat: wallet team | emotion: surprise 4 | close: L4 | pay: P2 | kind: reversal | link: but -->
60. Not random people. | Binance calls them Wallet DevOps Engineer A. | And Wallet Software Engineer C. [↺]
61. Remember June. Not a password. A list of who does what. | This is what it was for.
62. On Binance's account, the engineer they courted with a fake job was the way in to two people who did have "wallet" in their titles.
63. > B wasn't the target. | B was the door.

<!-- beat: who | emotion: intrigue 3 | open: L6 | kind: escalation | link: therefore -->
64. Step back for a second. A fake company. A fake chief executive. Malware nobody had seen. Five months of patience. [↺]
65. Who runs an operation like that? And have they done it before?
66. In twenty twenty-two, the crypto game Axie Infinity lost more than half a billion dollars. The Block later reported, citing two anonymous sources, that it began with a fake job offer.
67. In February twenty twenty-five, the exchange Bybit lost about one and a half billion.

<!-- beat: the pattern | emotion: unease 4 | close: L6 | kind: escalation | link: therefore -->
68. Half a billion. | One and a half billion. | Now six point three. More than four times Bybit. [↺]
69. U.S. officials tied the first to North Korea. The F.B.I. blamed the second on a North Korean operation it calls TraderTraitor.
70. On the thirty-first of October, the F.B.I. named that same operation for this one. North Korea rejects that. No one has been charged.
71. Blockchain analysts at T.R.M. Labs reached the same conclusion, "with high confidence."
72. Binance says it stopped fifty-seven North Korea-related attempts this year alone.
73. > Fifty-seven attempts stopped. | This one wasn't.

<!-- beat: the token | emotion: tension 3 | kind: complication | link: but -->
74. Here's how this one got through. Engineer A's computer held a container deployment token. [↺]
75. In plain words, a key that lets you put new code onto the company's own servers. The attackers could now reach the wallet's doorstep.
76. But Binance says it found no evidence that any wallet's private key was ever stolen. And without the key, you can't move the coins.
77. So either they found the keys somewhere. | Or they found a way not to need them.

## 4. TWENTY MINUTES (T–0 · 29 OCTOBER)

<!-- beat: the script | emotion: tension 2 | pay: P3 | kind: escalation | link: therefore -->
78. Thursday, the twenty-ninth of October. Keys or no keys, tonight we find out. | A little after eight in the evening, U.T.C., according to analysts' timelines.
79. Binance says the attackers used the wallet engineers' stolen credentials to deploy a malicious script.
80. The script reached the machine you were asked to keep in mind. | The wallet signing service. [↺]

<!-- beat: the permission slip | emotion: intrigue 3 | plant: P6 | kind: complication | link: therefore -->
81. Here's the trick, as blockchain security firms reconstruct it on Ethereum.
82. Since an upgrade last year, an ordinary Ethereum wallet can sign a kind of permission slip. It says: let this piece of software act for me.
83. While that slip is in force, the software can move the wallet's money. No new signature needed. [↺]
84. The security firms Fireblocks and Blockaid say Binance's own hot wallet signed fifty of those slips. All pointing to the attackers' code.
85. Why fifty? They say only one had to work. The rest were insurance.

<!-- beat: the vault signs | emotion: shock 5 | close: L5 | open: L7 | pay: P5 | kind: revelation | link: but -->
86. So did they find the keys? | No. The private keys never left Binance. | They didn't have to. [↺]
87. > Nobody broke into the vault. | The vault signed for them.
88. Which leaves a question for anyone with money on an exchange. If Binance's own machine signed the money away, | who pays?

<!-- beat: twenty minutes | emotion: dread 4 | kind: stakes | link: therefore -->
89. So the money moved. Ten blockchains. Analysts at T.R.M. Labs say most of it went in about twenty minutes.
90. Here's our math. Even if "most" means just over half, that's more than two and a half million dollars every second. [↺]
91. Binance halted withdrawals. For about two days, customers of the world's largest exchange couldn't take out their own money.
92. And according to Binance, even after withdrawals stopped, the permission slips kept working. | The draining went on.

<!-- beat: aftermath | emotion: amusement 2 | kind: release | link: but -->
93. That same night, Binance's founder, Changpeng Zhao, posted: "User funds are safe!" [↺]
94. Eventually, Binance revoked the slips. By then, the money had left.
95. Five days later, Binance published how it happened, with an apology. | "This should not have happened."

## 5. WHO PAYS (T+5 DAYS · 3 NOVEMBER)

<!-- beat: binance pays | emotion: surprise 3 | close: L7 | open: L8 | kind: complication | link: therefore -->
96. So who pays? Either customers eat this loss. | Or Binance does.
97. Quick guess. The money vanished on a Thursday night. How much would you expect a company to replace by Saturday? [↺]
98. A tenth? | A quarter?
99. Binance says it had replaced ninety-eight percent of the affected tokens by then, from its emergency fund and its own money.
100. Its message to customers: "Users will not bear the financial impact of this incident."
101. But replacing the money isn't the same as getting it back. | So where is it? [↺]

<!-- beat: still held | emotion: indignation 4 | close: L8 | open: L9 | kind: stakes | link: but -->
102. Stolen crypto is hard to hide. Every coin sits on a public ledger, and every move can be watched.
103. Stablecoin issuers froze what they could. Binance puts the frozen total at close to three billion dollars.
104. On the second of November, the analytics firm Elliptic said about three point two four billion dollars still sat in addresses linked to the theft.
105. Its chief scientist, Tom Robinson, said the funds that haven't moved are "very likely still held by North Korea". North Korea rejects that.
106. > About half the money. | Still sitting there, in plain view. [↺]
107. And the method that took it is still out there too.

<!-- beat: the answer | emotion: awe 5 | close: L1 | pay: P4, P6 | kind: payoff | link: therefore -->
108. So. How does one coding test turn into the largest theft in crypto history?
109. Go back to the permission slip. A message that says: let this software act for me. [↺]
110. Now look at the take-home test again. Download this. Run it. Let this code act for you.
111. Here's how we read it. A take-home test *is* a permission slip.
112. > The engineer signed one in September. | The vault signed fifty in October.
113. Neither was forced. Both did exactly what they were asked.
114. Of every door in this story, that's the one a security team can't simply lock. | Because opening it is the job. [↺]
115. If it worked on the biggest exchange in the world, who else is it working on?

<!-- beat: button | emotion: unease 4 | close: L9 | pay: P1 | kind: button | link: but -->
116. Security researchers have documented the same lure aimed at ordinary developers. Not necessarily the same attackers. The same lure.
117. In twenty twenty-five, the security firm Socket reported thirty-five malicious coding packages tied to one fake-recruiter campaign. [↺]
118. Fake recruiters. Fake companies. Poisoned coding tests.
119. The next one won't look like a heist either.
120. > It will look like one folder.
