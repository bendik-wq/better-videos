# The Job Interview That Cost $6.3 Billion: narration v4

**Structure:** the countdown (`script-patterns.md` §1.4). One laptop followed through five months, with an on-screen clock (T–5 MONTHS … T–0 … T+5 DAYS) on every chapter card. Runner-up: the investigation; it lost because the "who" is already named by the FBI, so the open question is "how", which a clock tells best.

**Ending choice (v4):** the button lands within 60 s of the vault reveal. The vault ("Nobody stole the key. The vault signed for them.") is the strongest *fact* in the film, and three blind reviews rated anything after it lower, because an interpretation can't outrank a fact. So the money questions (who pays, where is it) now come *before* the mechanism, inside the open "how did it leave?" loop, and the L1 answer (the permission-slip rhyme) is the second half of the vault beat itself. The film's biggest peak is now also its last peak, and the button follows it directly.

**Look:** night mode (style bible). Work light on a desk for everything human; fluorescent green-black for the inside of Binance's systems; red only for annotation.

**Length:** see the critic run in LOOP.md (150 wpm, VO only). The sponsor read adds 45–75 s on top.

**Re-hooks:** `[↺]` marks one; target one every 30–40 s.

**Sponsor:** one slot right after the first big mid-film peak (*the map*, about 2:30 VO), marked `[SPONSOR SLOT — offer TBD]`. No copy yet. The first line after it answers a question the viewer is holding ("how do you get past the tool that just caught you?") with a partial, sharper one, because it is where skippers land.

**Fact rules:**
- Every fact is in SOURCES.md (IDs in the fact map at the bottom).
- ⏱ = everything. This is a breaking story; re-check every number on publish day. Every status that can change carries an "as of" date in the VO.
- Binance's account is a company claim: "Binance says", "according to Binance's report", "on Binance's account", "in Binance's telling". Every sentence that states a step of the attack carries it, or follows one in the same beat.
- North Korea is an F.B.I. attribution plus analysts' assessments, denied by North Korea. No one is charged. Never "North Korea stole". The attribution and the denial are spoken *before* the earlier thefts are lined up, and the ladder is introduced as conditional ("If the F.B.I. is right").
- The engineers are private people: only Binance's own role labels, no faces, no blame. The June engineer is never said to have made a mistake. Roles, never "who": the report says roles were exposed, not names.
- Engineer B's team: the report's label is "Software Engineer B", while A and C are labelled "Wallet …". The VO only ever says what the label says ("no 'wallet' in the title"), never that B was on or off a team.
- The "permission slip" reading of the take-home test is our interpretation and is spoken as one ("Here's how we read it").
- `[ON SCREEN: …]` lines are picture notes for shot-design, not narration.

**Markup:** `*word*` stressed · `> ` slower line · `|` beat · `[↺]` re-hook. Beat annotations follow `docs/research/emotional-engine.md` §7. Setups use `P1…` (plant IDs) so they don't clash with source IDs `S01…`.

**Loops (open → close):** every beat that closes a loop opens the next one, until the payoff.
- **L1:** how does one coding test turn into the largest theft in crypto history? (the number → the vault signs; restated in *the interview* and *the vault signs*)
- **L2:** Binance caught part of this attack once already. So either Binance missed something, or catching it didn't matter. (three strange facts → the map)
- **L3:** Binance's security just caught them. How do you get past the exact tool that caught you? (the map → not detected, across the sponsor slot)
- **L4:** why go after an engineer with no "wallet" in the title? Either they misread their own map, or B was useful in a way the title doesn't show. (the recruiter → wallet team)
- **L5:** one laptop inside Binance. Dead end, or a way to reach someone who can touch the money? (not detected → wallet team)
- **L6:** who plans a heist like that? (wallet team → the pattern)
- **L7:** whoever it was, how did this one get through the defences that stop the rest? (the pattern → the token)
- **L8:** either Binance is wrong about the keys, or there's a way to move the money without them. (the token → the vault signs)
- **L9:** how does money leave an exchange that has shut its doors? (the drain → the vault signs; restated at the end of *still held*)
- **L10:** who pays for it? (the drain → binance pays)
- **L11:** replacing the money isn't getting it back, so where is it? (binance pays → still held)

**Setups (plant → pay), six:**
- **P1:** one folder (the interview → button)
- **P2:** June: the attackers learn which roles run the vault, the role chart (the map → wallet team)
- **P3:** the signing service, "keep that machine in mind" (the machine → the script)
- **P4:** the take-home test is the one moment a careful engineer is *told* to run a stranger's code (the folder → the vault signs, where it becomes a permission slip)
- **P5:** the two impossibilities in the cold open: "the private keys were never stolen" and "after Binance shut withdrawals, the money kept leaving anyway" (three strange facts → the drain, where the second gets its number; → the vault signs, where both are explained)
- **P6:** June: Binance's security caught the first tool, "round one to Binance" (caught once → not detected, where the same security misses)
- Callback, not a setup: the permission slip (explained in *the permission slip*, rhymed 40 s later in *the vault signs*).

**Hooks:** three variants scored in the hook table at the bottom; the misconception-first variant won.
