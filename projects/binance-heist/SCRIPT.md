**TITLE: THE JOB INTERVIEW THAT COST SIX POINT THREE BILLION DOLLARS**

## 1. THE AD (T–5 MONTHS · JUNE)

<!-- beat: the vault | emotion: curiosity 2 | plant: P3 | kind: setup | link: therefore -->
15. First, what was actually robbed.
16. An exchange holds its customers' coins. Most exchanges keep the bulk offline, in cold storage. Slow to move. Hard to steal.
17. But some coins have to stay online, so withdrawals can happen in seconds. That's the hot wallet.
18. A hot wallet only moves when a computer signs off on it. A signing service. | Automatic. Fast. Trusted. [↺]
19. Keep that machine in mind. Everything in this story is trying to reach it.

<!-- beat: the ad | emotion: unease 3 | kind: complication | link: but -->
20. In June, according to Binance, an engineer on the team that runs those wallets clicked an online ad.
21. It led to a tampered copy of a popular developer tool. The engineer downloaded it.
22. It's an old trick. Poison something developers already trust, then advertise it to them. [↺]

<!-- beat: caught | emotion: relief 2 | kind: release | link: but -->
23. And here's the thing. | Binance caught it.
24. The company says it detected the tool, replaced the computer and changed the credentials.
25. That's a security team doing its job. So what went wrong? [↺]

<!-- beat: the map | emotion: surprise 4 | close: L2 | open: L3 | plant: P2 | kind: reversal | link: but -->
26. By Binance's own account, the attackers had already learned something more useful than a password.
27. They had learned who was on the wallet team. | And what each person did.
28. > You can change a password. You can't change who works for you. [↺]
29. So the attackers didn't need that laptop anymore. They needed a new way in, through a different person.
30. Who on that team would open a file from a stranger, on purpose?

## 2. THE INTERVIEW (T–7 WEEKS · SEPTEMBER)

<!-- beat: the recruiter | emotion: intrigue 3 | kind: escalation | link: therefore -->
31. Someone looking for a new job.
32. In September, Binance says, a recruiter reached out to one of its software engineers. Its report calls this person Software Engineer B.
33. Here is how the report puts it. [↺]
34. > "Software Engineer B was contacted through a legitimate job-hunting platform by an individual posing as a Project Director."
35. A real platform. A fake person.

<!-- beat: the CEO | emotion: tension 2 | plant: P4 | kind: complication | link: therefore -->
36. Then a first-round interview: a live video call with someone presenting as H.R. They even shared a social media account.
37. The second round was with the company's chief executive, on a chat app.
38. At the end, the engineer was asked to download a coding assignment from an online code repository. [↺]
39. If you're not a programmer, that might sound odd. If you are, it sounds like any other Tuesday.
40. Take-home tests are a normal part of hiring developers. You download the project. You run it. That's the point.
41. > It's the one moment a careful engineer is *told* to run a stranger's code. [↺]

<!-- beat: not detected | emotion: dread 4 | close: L3 | open: L4 | kind: revelation | link: therefore -->
42. The folder ran. And according to Binance, something else ran with it.
43. Custom malware, never seen before. The report says plainly:
44. > "The zero-day malware was not detected by the endpoint security protection tools at the time." [↺]
45. It hid inside a tool programmers use every day. It deleted a real program called pie-env, and slipped its own copy into its place.
46. Nothing crashed. Nothing looked wrong. | The laptop now answered to someone else.

<!-- beat: your chair | emotion: empathy 3 | kind: release | link: but -->
47. It's easy to blame the engineer. Look at it from that chair instead.
48. A real job site. A recruiter with a title. A video call with H.R. A chief executive who makes time for you. [↺]
49. Then homework, like at every other developer job. Every step is the step you'd expect.
50. If you've ever applied for a job online, you've done most of it yourself.

<!-- beat: one laptop | emotion: curiosity 2 | kind: complication | link: but -->
51. That's who opens the file. Someone told to.
52. But one software engineer's laptop still isn't the wallet. Binance's money doesn't move because one developer's machine says so. [↺]
53. So how do you get from a laptop at home to the machine that signs for billions?

## 3. THE INSIDE (T–9 DAYS · 20 OCTOBER)

<!-- beat: the helper | emotion: tension 3 | kind: setup | link: therefore -->
54. From one laptop, there are two ways to the money. Steal the keys. | Or steal the people who hold them.
55. Like a lot of companies now, Binance had its own internal A.I. tool.
56. Binance hasn't said exactly what it does. But it says the attackers used it. [↺]
57. By the twentieth of October, according to the report, they had used the engineer's laptop and that A.I. tool to take over the computers of two more people.

<!-- beat: wallet team | emotion: surprise 4 | pay: P2 | kind: reversal | link: but -->
58. Not random people. | Both of them worked on the wallet team. [↺]
59. Remember what leaked in June. Not a password. A list of who does what. | This is what it was for.
60. Binance calls them Wallet DevOps Engineer A and Wallet Software Engineer C.
61. Engineer A's computer held a container deployment token. In plain words, a key that lets you put new code onto the company's own servers.
62. > The company's own A.I. helper had become part of the break-in.

<!-- beat: secondary | emotion: curiosity 3 | close: L4 | open: L5 | kind: complication | link: but -->
63. Binance stresses that A.I. played a secondary role. The main weapon was still old-fashioned deception. [↺]
64. And now the attackers had a way onto the servers. That's how you get from one laptop to the wallet's doorstep.
65. But Binance says it found no evidence that any wallet's private key was ever stolen.
66. And without the private key, you can't move the coins. | Right?

<!-- beat: who | emotion: tension 3 | kind: escalation | link: therefore -->
67. Before the answer, the question investigators asked first. Who runs a five-month operation like this? [↺]
68. On the thirty-first of October, the F.B.I. issued a cyber alert. It said North Korea was responsible, and named the operation TraderTraitor.
69. Analysts at T.R.M. Labs had reached the same conclusion, "with high confidence." Chainalysis called it "likely."
70. North Korea has rejected the accusation, Reuters reports. No one has been charged.

<!-- beat: the pattern | emotion: awe 4 | kind: escalation | link: therefore -->
71. If the trick sounds familiar, it should. [↺]
72. In twenty twenty-two, the crypto game Axie Infinity lost more than half a billion dollars. U.S. officials tied it to North Korea.
73. The Block later reported, citing two anonymous sources, that it began with a fake job offer.
74. In February twenty twenty-five, the exchange Bybit lost about one and a half billion. The F.B.I. blamed TraderTraitor.
75. Half a billion. | One and a half billion. | Now six point three. [↺]
76. Binance says it stopped fifty-seven North Korea-related attempts this year alone.
77. > Fifty-seven attempts stopped. | This one wasn't.

## 4. TWENTY MINUTES (T–0 · 29 OCTOBER)

<!-- beat: the script | emotion: tension 3 | pay: P3 | kind: escalation | link: therefore -->
78. Thursday, the twenty-ninth of October. A little after eight in the evening, U.T.C., according to analysts' timelines.
79. Binance says the attackers used the wallet engineers' stolen credentials to deploy a malicious script.
80. The script reached the machine you were asked to keep in mind. | The wallet signing service. [↺]

<!-- beat: the permission slip | emotion: intrigue 2 | kind: complication | link: therefore -->
81. Here's the trick, as blockchain security firms reconstruct it on Ethereum.
82. Since an upgrade last year, an ordinary Ethereum wallet can sign a kind of permission slip. It says: let this piece of software act for me.
83. While that slip is in force, the software can move the wallet's money. No new signature needed. [↺]
84. The security firms Fireblocks and Blockaid say Binance's own hot wallet signed fifty of those slips. All pointing to the attackers' code.
85. Why fifty? They say only one had to work. The rest were insurance.

<!-- beat: the vault signs | emotion: shock 5 | close: L5 | open: L9 | pay: P5 | kind: revelation | link: but -->
86. The private keys never left Binance. | They didn't have to. [↺]
87. > Nobody broke into the vault. | The vault signed for them.
88. Then the money moved. Ten blockchains. T.R.M. says most of it went in about twenty minutes.
89. Here's our math. Even if "most" means just over half, that's more than two and a half million dollars every second.
90. Binance halted withdrawals. For about two days, customers of the world's largest exchange couldn't take out their own money.
91. And according to Binance, even after withdrawals stopped, the permission slips kept working. | The draining went on.
92. So how do you stop a machine that has permission to empty you?

<!-- beat: aftermath | emotion: curiosity 3 | close: L9 | open: L6 | kind: release | link: therefore -->
93. You take the permission back. Eventually, Binance revoked the slips.
94. That same night, its founder, Changpeng Zhao, had posted: "User funds are safe!" [↺]
95. Which leaves the question anyone with money on an exchange actually cares about.
96. > Who pays for six point three billion dollars?

## 5. WHO PAYS (T+5 DAYS · 3 NOVEMBER)

<!-- beat: binance pays | emotion: relief 2 | kind: release | link: therefore -->
97. Binance says its customers won't. "Users will not bear the financial impact of this incident."
98. Quick guess. The money vanished on a Thursday night. How much of it would you expect a company to replace by Saturday? [↺]
99. A tenth? A quarter?
100. Binance says it had replaced ninety-eight percent of the affected tokens by then, from its emergency fund and its own money.
101. Stablecoin issuers like Tether and Circle froze what they could. Binance puts the frozen total at about two point eight five billion dollars.
102. And on the third of November, it published how it happened, with an apology. | "This should not have happened."

<!-- beat: the laundering | emotion: tension 3 | open: L8 | kind: escalation | link: but -->
103. But stealing crypto is the easy half. Spending it is harder. [↺]
104. Every coin sits on a public ledger. Every move can be watched.
105. The F.B.I. says the attackers were converting the stolen assets mainly into Ether. It warned they could launder more through decentralised finance platforms and instant-swap exchanges.
106. With the Secret Service and the I.R.S., it asked the whole industry to block the addresses involved.
107. After Bybit, the F.B.I. said that money had already been spread across thousands of addresses.
108. So can anyone actually cash out six billion stolen dollars?

<!-- beat: the rest | emotion: indignation 4 | close: L6, L8 | open: L7 | kind: stakes | link: but -->
109. Replacing the money isn't the same as getting it back. [↺]
110. On the second of November, Elliptic said about three point two four billion dollars still sat in addresses linked to the theft.
111. Its chief scientist, Tom Robinson, said the funds that haven't moved are "very likely still held by North Korea".
112. He added that some may already be with Chinese money-laundering networks.
113. U.S. intelligence has assessed that Pyongyang nets at least a billion dollars a year from crypto heists and other financial crime.
114. Experts say that money helps fund its economy and its nuclear programme, despite U.N. sanctions. [↺]
115. Asked about it, President Trump said: "We don't know if it's North Korea, but if it is, we'll talk to North Korea about it."
116. And a method that worked once can be used again.

<!-- beat: the answer | emotion: awe 5 | close: L1 | pay: P4 | kind: payoff | link: therefore -->
117. So. How does one coding test turn into the largest theft in crypto history?
118. Not with one genius exploit. With a chain of ordinary things. An ad. A list of names. A recruiter. A video call. A helpful internal tool. [↺]
119. And a signing machine doing exactly what it was built to do.
120. Every link was something a company is supposed to trust.
121. > And the strongest link was the first one a stranger could touch. A take-home test. The one moment when running a stranger's code isn't a mistake. | It's the job.
122. Binance has declined to say what happens to the engineers.
123. If this worked on the biggest exchange in the world, who else is it working on?

<!-- beat: button | emotion: unease 4 | close: L7 | pay: P1 | kind: button | link: but -->
124. Binance says it's spending more on A.I. threat detection, and starting a security research fund.
125. But security researchers have documented the same lure aimed at ordinary developers. Fake recruiters. Fake companies. Poisoned coding tests. [↺]
126. The next one won't look like a heist either.
127. > It will look like one folder.
