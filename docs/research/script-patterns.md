# Script patterns: structures, beat devices, TTS rhythm, fact safety

How to build a 12–18 min faceless documentary script that holds viewers. Read with:
`hooks.md` (cold opens, re-hooks, buttons), `retention-playbook.md` (evidence),
`channel-breakdowns.md` (who does what) and `editing-library.md` (how the picture carries it).
`retention-script` follows this file step by step.

Trust labels as in the playbook. **[test]** marks something we measured on our own engine.

---

## 1. Story structures for 12–18 min documentaries

Pick **one spine** per video. Every structure below is drawn at **16:00**; scale the timestamps
proportionally. All share the house frame: cold open (0:00–~1:00), title card, a first payoff
around 20–25% (where the affiliate read goes), big re-engagements near 3:00 and 6:00, the twist
around 65–75%, L1 closed in the last 15%, and an abrupt button
([memo](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)).

### Chooser

| Your material has… | Use | Seen at |
|---|---|---|
| A wrongdoing, a trail of evidence, a reveal | **The investigation** | Fern, LEMMiNO, hoog |
| A founder or company that rose and crashed | **Rise and fall** | ColdFusion, MagnatesMedia, Business Casual |
| An invisible machine behind everyday life | **The hidden system** | Wendover, PolyMatter, Neo, Vox |
| A deadline, a collapse measured in hours or days | **The countdown** | Fern ("The Hunt for…"), ColdFusion disasters |
| Two sides with one winner | **The duel** | Business Casual (Lamborghini), MrBeast "$1 vs" |
| One strange object or fact that unlocks everything | **The mystery box** | Veritasium (black balls), Neo (empty islands), Fern |
| A topic people think they understand | **The explainer-with-twist** | Kurzgesagt, RealLifeLore, Johnny Harris |

If two fit, pick the one whose **central question is shortest** to say aloud.

### 1.1 The investigation

**Spine:** "Who did it, and how did they get away with it (or not)?" The narrator is a detective
showing the evidence in the order it was discovered, not in chronological order.

| Time | Beat | Job | Loops |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Cold open: the crime scene or the artefact (H001, H025, H088) | Show the evidence object; the anomaly | open L1 (who/how) |
| 0:45 | Stakes: who was hurt, how much | Make it matter | open L2 (why did nobody stop it) |
| 1:00 | Title card | | |
| 1:05 | **Ch.1 The first clue** | The anomaly someone noticed | close nothing; open L3 |
| ~3:00 | Re-engagement: the primary document on screen | Wow factor | |
| 3:40 | First payoff, then affiliate read (option A) | | close L3 |
| 4:40 | **Ch.2 Following the money / the trail** | Each step stair-steps the size of the scheme | open L4 |
| ~6:00 | Re-engagement: the reveal that it's bigger than one person | | |
| 7:30 | **Ch.3 How it worked** (mechanism) | The lull; keep it visual (diagram, map, reconstruction) | close L2 |
| 10:30 | **Ch.4 The mistake** | The one thread that unravelled it (twist) | close L4, open L5 |
| 13:30 | **Ch.5 The reckoning** | Arrest, ruling, admission, or the case left open | close L1, L5 |
| 15:40 | Button (B16, B03) | Where things stand | |

**Rules:** every accusation must be traceable to a filing, ruling, admission or attributed
reporting (§4). Show at least one primary document per chapter. Never "solve" what the record
doesn't solve; say "the case is still open" (LEMMiNO's epistemic honesty).

### 1.2 Rise and fall

**Spine:** "How did the thing everyone believed in fall so fast?" Start at the fall, rewind
(H032), then ride the rise with growing dread.

| Time | Beat | Job | Loops |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Cold open on the fall (in medias res, H028/H032/H045) | The end state, vivid | open L1 (how did it come to this) |
| 0:40 | "Rewind [N] years" + the promise | Make the peak feel real | open L2 (what was the flaw) |
| 1:00 | Title | | |
| 1:05 | **Ch.1 The idea** | Underdog origin; why it was genuinely clever | open L3 (who believed) |
| ~3:00 | Re-engagement: the first impossible-seeming win | | |
| 3:40 | Affiliate read (option A) | | |
| 4:40 | **Ch.2 The rocket** | Stair-step the valuation ×3; the celebrity backers | close L3 |
| ~6:00 | Re-engagement: the first crack, hidden in plain sight | | open L4 |
| 7:30 | **Ch.3 The machine behind the curtain** | How it really worked (the lull, made visual) | close L2 |
| 10:30 | **Ch.4 The unravelling** | Countdown pacing; shorter beats, faster cuts | close L4 |
| 13:30 | **Ch.5 The aftermath** | Who paid; what changed (or didn't) | close L1 |
| 15:40 | Button (B11–B15 irony) | | |

**Rules:** the protagonist must be understandable, not a cartoon, or the fall has no weight.
The flaw must be **planted in Ch.1** and paid off in Ch.4 (callback).

### 1.3 The hidden system

**Spine:** "How does [everyday thing] actually work, and who profits?" (Wendover, PolyMatter,

| Time | Beat | Job | Loops |

| 1:00 | Title | | |

**Rules:** one **traced unit** must recur in every chapter (the same coffee payment, the same
packet). That unit is your callback engine.

**Rules:** cross-cut between sides with a **visual rule** (left versus right of frame, warm
versus cold key light) so the viewer always knows whose beat it is. Rounds must alternate winners
at least once.

### 1.6 The mystery box

**Spine:** "Why is [strange object] like this?" (Veritasium's 96 million balls, Neo's empty

**Rules:** the hero object appears in **every chapter**, each time lit or framed differently, and
the final shot repeats the first shot so the viewer sees it with new eyes.

### 1.7 The explainer-with-twist

**Spine:** "You think you understand [X]. Here's what you're missing." Kurzgesagt and
RealLifeLore's biggest videos. Teach the standard explanation well, then flip it.

| Time | Beat | Job | Loops |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | The common belief stated as true (H054–H061) | | open L1 (what's wrong with it) |
| 0:30 | A quick prediction test (H061) | Viewer commits to an answer | open L2 |
| 1:00 | Title | | |
| 1:05 | **Ch.1 The standard story** | Explain it properly; the viewer feels smart | close L2 |
| ~3:00 | Re-engagement: the first fact that doesn't fit | | open L3 |
| 3:40 | Affiliate | | |
| 4:40 | **Ch.2 The zoom** | Analogy ladder / zoom-out (§2.7, §2.8) | |
| ~6:00 | Re-engagement: the scale reveal | | |
| 7:30 | **Ch.3 Why everyone got it wrong** | Mechanism of the misconception | close L3 |
| 10:30 | **Ch.4 The twist** | The new model, with one surprising implication | open L4 |
| 13:30 | **Ch.5 What it means for you** | Personal stakes; close L1 | close L1, L4 |
| 15:40 | Button (B26) | | |

**Rules:** the misconception must be one the target viewer actually holds (check comments on
competing videos). Never mock the viewer for holding it; Johnny Harris's "Why People Think…"
explains beliefs from the inside.

## 2. Beat-level devices

### 2.1 Open loops

A question the viewer is waiting to have answered. It keeps them watching across dull stretches.

- **Explicit, in the viewer's words.** "So where did the money go?", not "the financial
  irregularities would prove significant".
- **2–4 loops open at all times; never more than 4.** Each opened explicitly and closed
  explicitly. Keep the ledger in the outline (`retention-script` §5).
- **Nest them.** Big loop (L1, the whole video) → chapter loops (closed within 2–4 min) → micro
  loops (closed within 30–60 s: "Keep that number in mind", R50).
- **Close one, open one.** Never close a loop without opening the next, until the payoff chapter.
- **Promise the payoff, not the answer.** "By the end, you'll know who's on the other side of the
  glass" (Palantir) is good. "The answer is X, but first…" is a spoiler.
- **An unclosed loop is a broken promise** ([playbook §4](retention-playbook.md)). Run the ledger
  check before shipping.

### 2.2 Stair-stepping

Stakes climb in visible steps toward a payoff ([memo](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/):
$1 → $10 → $50 → $375 → $1,000 → $10,000 → world record).

- **Three axes to climb:** money ($1M → $1B → a country's GDP), reach (one person → one company →
  an industry → a government), consequence (embarrassing → illegal → lethal).
- **At least 3 steps per video, and one step per chapter.** Each step is a re-hook (R09–R14).
- **Say the previous step aloud** when you announce the next: "One company was a scandal. Forty
  companies was a system."
- **Show the step physically**: a counter that ticks up, a map that widens, a stack that grows
  (`editing-library.md` §5).

### 2.3 But / therefore

Join beats by causation, never by sequence (Parker and Stone, [Matt Rickard](https://mattrickard.com/but-therefore)).

- Every beat-to-beat link reads as **"but"** (complication) or **"therefore / so"**
  (consequence). If it reads as "and then", rewrite or cut it.
- Alternate them: but → therefore → but. Two "therefores" in a row feel like a lecture; two
  "buts" in a row feel like a tease.
- The words themselves don't have to appear. Test by inserting them between beats in the outline.
- Score: −1 per "and then" link (`retention-script` check 7).

### 2.4 Callbacks

A callback brings back an earlier image, line or number with a new meaning.

- **Plant early, pay late.** Plant in the cold open or Ch.1; pay off in Ch.4–5.
- **Three kinds**: image (the cold-open object re-lit), line (the cold-open sentence repeated
  with a new meaning), number (the first number compared with today's).
- **One major callback per chapter** after Ch.2, plus the button (B06–B10).
- On screen: repeat the same framing and lens so the eye recognises it (graphic match,
  `shot-design` §7).

### 2.5 The reveal by numbers

Make a number land by building to it rather than stating it.

1. **Set the scale.** Give the viewer a reference they can feel ("a good salary is about sixty
   thousand dollars").
2. **Ladder up** in 2–3 steps ("a hundred times that… a thousand times that…").
3. **Pause** (padOut 0.7–1.0 s, §3.4).
4. **The number**, alone, on its own line and its own shot, with a source line on screen.
5. **Make it physical**: weight, distance, time to spend it (H014, H084).

Rules: one reveal number per minute at most; round in the VO ("about forty-two billion"),
exact on screen with the source ("$42.0B · FDIC, 2023"). Never stack two big numbers in one
sentence.

### 2.6 Personification

Give a system, company or object a will so the viewer can follow it like a character.

- "The algorithm wanted one thing: more time on the app." "The bank's bonds were quietly losing value."
- **Only for systems, never for real people's inner thoughts.** Don't write what a real person
  felt or intended unless they said it on the record (§4).
- Pair with a recurring visual (the stone, the server, the coin) so the "character" has a face.

### 2.7 Analogy ladders

Explain one hard idea through 2–3 analogies of rising precision.

1. **Everyday analogy** (feel): "A bank is like a coat check that lends out your coat."
2. **Mechanical analogy** (how): "When you deposit, the bank writes a number. When it lends, it
   writes another number. Both are promises."
3. **The real thing** (precise): "According to the Bank of England, loans create deposits."
4. Say where the analogy breaks ("the analogy breaks here, and that's the interesting part"),
   which is itself a re-hook.

Rules: every rung gets its own visual; never more than 3 rungs; the last rung is the literal truth
with a source.

### 2.8 Zoom in, zoom out

Alternate between one concrete case and the whole system (Kurzgesagt, RealLifeLore, Neo).

- **Zoom in**: one person, one transaction, one server, one day. Gives emotion and memory.
- **Zoom out**: the map, the chart, the total. Gives scale and stakes.
- **Rhythm**: switch roughly every 60–90 s. Long stretches at one scale feel either trivial (all
  in) or abstract (all out).
- **Say it**: "That's one account. There were two million." (R42)
- **Picture it**: a literal camera zoom (the oner from street to orbit; `LIBRARY-BRIEF.md`) is
  the house signature for this device.

### 2.9 More devices

- **Misconception first** (Muller): state the wrong belief as if true, then break it. Proven to
  improve understanding ([THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/articles/2015/05/05/watching-videos-does-not-necessarily-lead-to-learning.aspx)).
- **Predict, then reveal** (Veritasium): ask the viewer to guess, pause, then answer. A micro
  loop that lasts 3–10 s.
- **Rule of three, broken**: list two expected items and a third that surprises ("Stolen cards.
  Fake accounts. A Russian fraudster named Igor.").
- **Chapter cliffhanger**: end each chapter on an open question or a reversal, never on a
  summary. The chapter card is a mid-roll slot, so the hook must survive an ad.
- **Signposting** ("structural context"): tell viewers where they are ("That was the first
  mistake"). It lowers the urge to leave because they can see the end.
- **Progressive novel information** (Joos): every 20–30 s the viewer should learn something they
  didn't know. If a passage only restates, cut it.
- **Picture contradicts the VO** (Sam O'Nella): once or twice per video, for dry humour.

## 3. Sentence-level rhythm for TTS narration

Our narrator is a TTS voice: Kokoro `af_heart` at speed 0.96 by default (`engine/voice.py`,
`projects/palantir/project.mjs`), with ElevenLabs and Piper as alternatives. Palantir measured
about **2,450 words for 16 min, ~153 words per minute** including pauses (`SCRIPT.md`).

### 3.1 Sentence length and mix

- **Average 10–14 words; never over 25.** Long sentences flatten TTS intonation and bury the stress.
- **Mix**: after one or two long sentences (18–25 words), land a short one (2–6 words). The short
  one carries the emphasis: "It saved the company."
- **One idea per sentence.** If it has "and" joining two facts, split it.
- **Front-load the subject and verb.** "Palantir sued the Army." Not "In what would become one of
  the most unusual moves in defence contracting history, Palantir…"
- **No parentheses, brackets or nested clauses.** TTS reads them flat or reads the brackets.
- **End on the important word.** English stresses the end of a sentence: "The money came from the
  C.I.A." beats "The C.I.A. was where the money came from."
- **Fragments are allowed** for rhythm: "Stolen cards. Fake accounts. Organised crime."
- **Contractions** ("it's", "didn't") sound natural; use them except for emphasis ("It did not work.").
- **Avoid tongue-twisters and same-sound runs** ("sixty-six sixth-sense…") and homographs that the
  model may misread ("lead", "read", "live", "record") where context is weak.

### 3.2 Numbers for TTS [test]

We tested Kokoro (`af_heart`, 0.96) on 2026-10-11 and transcribed the output with Whisper:

| Written | Result | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| `$2M` | heard as "$2.2M" | **Never** abbreviate M/B/K |
| `$4.3B` | garbled | Write "four point three billion dollars" |
| `$4.3 billion` | fine | Acceptable, but words are safer |
| `1/3` | "one slash three" | Write "a third" |
| `ASML` | read as a word ("Asmo") | Dot letter-by-letter acronyms: "A.S.M.L." |
| `SEC`, `TSMC`, `FBI`, `NATO` | fine | Still dot any you haven't heard (S.E.C.) |
| `[pause]` | read aloud as "pause" | **Never** write stage directions in VO text |
| `80x` | fine in test | Prefer "eighty times" |
| `10:30 p.m.` | fine in test | Prefer "ten thirty at night" |

**House rules for numbers in VO:**

1. **Spell every number as it should be spoken** in the VO text. Digits go only in on-screen
   captions. This makes the output deterministic across engines (Kokoro, ElevenLabs and Piper
   normalise digits differently) and is what Palantir did ("two thousand and three").
2. **Round in speech, exact on screen.** "About forty-two billion dollars" (VO) /
   "$42.0B · FDIC" (caption).
3. **Years**: "two thousand and three", "nineteen ninety-nine", "twenty twenty-four". Pick one
   style for 2000–2009 per episode and keep it.
4. **Money**: "four point three billion dollars", "ten cents", "a hundred and seventy-eight thousand dollars".
5. **Percentages**: "seventy-eight percent". **Fractions**: "a third", "three quarters".
6. **Ranges**: "between three and four billion", never "3–4B".
7. **Dates**: "March the tenth, twenty twenty-three" or "on March tenth"; pick one style.
8. **Model and product names**: write them how they're said: "G.P.T. four", "H one hundred" when
   the digit form is misread. Test new names with a one-line render first.
9. **Brand names** the voice gets wrong: respell phonetically in the VO only ("En-vidia") and keep
   the real spelling in captions. `script_align` maps VO words to timings, so captions still sync.
10. **Never more than two numbers per sentence**, and never two big numbers back to back.

### 3.3 Words per minute and budget

- Budget **~150 words per minute** of runtime (Palantir measured ~153 at Kokoro 0.96, including
  pad gaps). 12 min ≈ 1,800 words; 16 min ≈ 2,400; 18 min ≈ 2,700.
- Reveal and emotional beats run slower (fewer words, longer pads); mechanism chapters run faster.
- Re-measure after any voice or speed change and update `retention-script` Inputs.

### 3.4 Pauses

What we measured [test]: inside one synthesised line, a full stop produces about **0.3–0.45 s**
of silence after a normal sentence, but only **~0.1 s** between two very short sentences ("It
worked. That was the problem."). Commas, semicolons, em dashes, ellipses and double spaces made
no measurable extra pause. So:

- **Real pauses come from line breaks, not punctuation.** Each VO line is one shot, and the
  renderer adds `padIn` 0.25 s + `padOut` 0.35 s by default (`engine/render.mjs`). That's a
  ~0.6 s gap between lines.
- **Dramatic pause**: put the punch line in its own shot and set `padOut` 0.7–1.2 s on the line
  *before* it (Palantir: `padOut: 0.9` before "Then came September the eleventh.").
- **Pause sizes** (padOut on the preceding line):
  - beat: 0.35 s (default);
  - breath after a reveal number: 0.7–0.9 s;
  - chapter end before a card: 0.9–1.2 s;
  - the button's final beat before black: 1.0–1.5 s.
- **Don't** rely on "…", "—" or ";" to pause. They read as commas.
- **Don't** put more than ~3 sentences in one VO line; long lines also limit cut points.

### 3.5 Writing for the ear

- **Say the subject's name again** instead of "it/they" after any break; listeners can't
  re-read.
- **Signal lists**: "Three things." then each on its own line.
- **Quote framing**: say who before the quote ("Its CEO said it on an earnings call:"), then
  put the quote on its own line with a quote card. Never "quote… end quote".
- **No visual-only references** in VO ("as you can see on the left"). The picture can add,
  the words must stand alone (and many viewers listen).
- **Read it aloud, or render it**, before locking. If the TTS stumbles twice, rewrite the line.

## 4. Fact-safety patterns

This is not legal advice. Defamation law varies by country (US, UK and EU differ a lot).
For any video that accuses a named living person or a private company of wrongdoing, get the
script read by someone who knows media law before publishing.

### 4.1 Principles

- **Attribution doesn't cure a false claim.** Under the US "republication" doctrine, repeating
  someone else's defamatory falsehood can make you liable too, even with "according to"
  ([RCFP](https://www.rcfp.org/journals/news-media-and-law-winter-2012/libelous-truth/)).
- **Truth and fair, accurate reporting of official records are the strongest protection.**
  Reporting from public documents is safest when you cite the source and fairly portray what the
  document says ([Colorado FOIC](https://coloradofoic.org/news/denver-post-9news-did-not-engage-in-libel-reporting-on-mans-attempted-murder-conviction-court-says/)).
- **Know what kind of document it is.** A court *decision* is a finding. A *complaint* or
  *indictment* is one side's allegation. Some courts extend fair-report privilege only to
  decisions, not to parties' pleadings
  ([RCFP](https://www.rcfp.org/fair-report-privilege-temporarily-returns/)).
- **Misleading framing can be actionable** even when each sentence is technically sourced
  ([Hollywood Reporter on Gizmodo](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/thr-esq/gizmodo-cant-dodge-jason-miller-defamation-claim-1204399)).

### 4.2 The status ladder: say exactly what stage the claim is at

| Status | Safe phrasing | Never |
|---|---|---|
| Rumour, unconfirmed | "A rumour spread that… [Company] has never confirmed it." | Present it as fact, or as the hook's payoff |
| Reported by press | "According to [outlet], …" / "[Outlet] reported that…" | Drop the attribution after the first mention if the claim recurs |
| Alleged in a lawsuit | "In a lawsuit, [plaintiff] alleges that… [Defendant] denies it." | "[Defendant] did X" |
| Charged / indicted | "Prosecutors charged [person] with… [He/She] pleaded not guilty." | "the fraudster", "the criminal" |
| Regulator's complaint | "The S.E.C. alleged that… [Company] settled without admitting wrongdoing." (if that's what happened) | Present a settlement as an admission |
| Admitted / pleaded guilty | "[Company] pleaded guilty to…" / "admitted in a settlement that…" | Expand beyond what was admitted |
| Convicted | "A jury found [person] guilty of [exact counts]." | Add crimes they weren't convicted of |
| Overturned / appealed | "[Person] is appealing." / "The conviction was overturned in [year]." | Omit a known appeal or reversal |

### 4.3 Attribution phrases (pick by evidence type)

- **Document**: "Its own filings say…", "In its annual report, the company said…", "Court
  records show…", "An internal memo, later published by [outlet], said…"
- **Person on record**: "[Name] said in [venue, year]…", "He later told [outlet]…", "In his own
  words:"
- **Press**: "[Outlet] reported…", "according to reporting by [outlet]…"
- **Estimate**: "One widely cited estimate puts it at…", "[Source] estimates…", "By some counts…"
- **Disputed**: "[A] says X. [B] disputes that." "The company says the figure is wrong."
- **Unknown**: "Nobody knows for sure.", "There's no public record of…", "That has never been
  confirmed."
- **Opinion (ours)**: label it: "Here's how we read it." "In our view…" Opinion based on
  disclosed facts is safer than an implied fact.

### 4.4 Safe constructions for hard claims

- **Describe the act, not the label.** "The company moved customer money into its own trading
  firm, according to prosecutors" instead of "the company stole".
- **Give the response.** If a person or company is accused, say what they said back, or that they
  didn't respond ("Palantir didn't respond to our request" only if we actually asked).
- **Don't imply intent** without evidence of intent. "The update crashed eight and a half million
  computers" (fact) versus "they pushed a dangerous update" (implies negligence).
- **No mind-reading.** Never write what a real person thought, felt or wanted unless they said it
  on the record.
- **Hedge words are not shields** on their own. "Allegedly" helps only if the allegation itself
  is accurately sourced and the surrounding framing doesn't present it as proven.
- **Thumbnails and titles count.** A title that states an allegation as fact is as risky as the
  VO. "The Most Dangerous Company" (opinion, supported in the video) is fine; "The Company That
  Commits [Crime]" is not, unless convicted.
- **Images of real people**: never put a real private individual in a "villain" frame; public
  figures only, and only in connection with documented acts.
- **Dates on everything that can change**: "As of October 2026, the case is ongoing."

### 4.5 The fact-safety pass (before `make-video`)

1. Every sentence with a number, a name, or a verb of wrongdoing has a source line in `RESEARCH.md`.
2. Each accusation's status is labelled on the ladder (§4.2) and phrased to match.
3. Each accused party's response is included or noted as "didn't respond / not asked".
4. No mind-reading lines; no invented scenes, dialogue or times. In-medias-res openers use
   documented scenes only.
5. Title, thumbnail text and chapter names pass the same test as the VO.
6. "⚑" items from the research pack are resolved or cut.
