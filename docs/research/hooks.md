# Hook library: cold opens, re-hooks and buttons

Cold-open templates grouped by **mechanism** (the psychological lever), plus re-hook and
transition lines and ending "buttons". `retention-script` uses this file: choose a structure
from `script-patterns.md`, pick 3 hook templates from **different** mechanisms here, write 3
variants, and score them with §0.

Evidence behind the mechanisms is in `retention-playbook.md` §3 and `channel-breakdowns.md`.

> **Every worked example is a drafting illustration.** Most use well-documented public facts, but
> numbers move (market caps, prices, user counts). **Re-source every fact in the research pack
> before it goes into a script.** Examples marked ⚑ are especially time-sensitive or contested.

## 0. Anatomy and scoring

A cold open does four jobs in about 30 s ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en): the
first 30 s must match the title and thumbnail):

1. **Image (0–5 s).** A concrete thing on screen: the thumbnail's hero object. Never a logo or greeting.
2. **Lean (5–15 s).** Context, so the right viewer self-selects.
3. **Turn (15–22 s).** "But…", "Except…", "And yet…": the contrarian snapback (Kallaway's
   context → interjection → snapback, [summary](https://opentools.ai/youtube-summary/how-to-create-irresistible-hooks-and-blow-up-your-content)).
4. **Loop (22–30 s).** The central question, stated in the viewer's words (opens **L1**).

**Hook score (out of 25).** Ship a hook only at **≥20**, and only if the gate passes.

| # | Check | Pts |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First 5 s is a concrete image or claim that the thumbnail already promised | 5 |
| 2 | The curiosity gap is specific: the viewer can state the question in one sentence | 5 |
| 3 | Words before the turn: ≤25 = 4, 26–40 = 2, >40 = 0 (fewer words = faster turn) | 4 |
| 4 | Stakes: it touches money, power, safety or the viewer's own life by 0:30 | 4 |
| 5 | Renderable: we can show it in our engine (a set, an object, a document, a map) | 3 |
| 6 | Novel: not the same mechanism as our last 2 episodes' cold opens | 2 |
| 7 | Reads aloud cleanly in TTS on the first take (no tongue-twisters, numbers written for speech) | 2 |
| G | **Gate:** every fact is sourced and fact-safe (`script-patterns.md` §4) | pass/fail |

Write the three variants from **three different mechanisms**. If two variants score within 2
points, keep the one with fewer words before the turn (Paddy Galloway's "write 3–4 intros,
listen back", [Creator Science](https://podcast.creatorscience.com/paddy-galloway/)).

## 1. Mystery object (H001–H008)

The object *is* the story (Fern, Neo, Veritasium's "96,000,000 Black Balls"). Our strongest
frame, and the easiest to render.

| ID | Template | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| H001 | This is a [object]. It [mundane fact]. And [one company/place] controls [huge share]. | This is a chip smaller than a postage stamp. Almost every AI model you've used was trained on thousands of them. And nearly all of them are made by one company, on one island. |
| H002 | [Depth/place] below [familiar place] sits [object]. Almost none of it belongs to [expected owner]. | Eighty feet below a street in Manhattan sits one of the largest piles of gold on Earth. Almost none of it belongs to America. |
| H003 | Look at [everyday object]. See [small detail]? That [detail] [does something powerful]. | Look at your bank card. See the first six digits? They tell a bank who issued it, and whether to let you pay, before you've finished tapping. |
| H004 | In [year], [person] paid [odd price] for [object]. [Later], that price was worth [huge]. | In May 2010, a programmer in Florida paid ten thousand bitcoin for two pizzas. In December 2024, those pizzas were worth more than a billion dollars. ⚑ |
| H005 | In [year], an internal [document] leaked. Its title was [N] words long: "[title]." | In May 2023, an internal Google document leaked. Its title: "We have no moat. And neither does OpenAI." |
| H006 | Almost every [object you own] has a [hidden component]. Most owners don't know it's there. | Almost every new car sold in America has a black box. Most owners don't know it's there, or who can read it. |
| H007 | One [object]. [Price]. And [buyer] [unexpected use]. | One web address: voice.com. In 2019, a crypto company paid thirty million dollars for it. |
| H008 | This is what [abstract thing] physically looks like. | This is what "the cloud" actually looks like. A windowless building the size of several football fields, humming next to a cornfield in Iowa. |

## 2. Shocking number (H009–H016)

One number, then make it physical. Never two numbers in the first sentence.

| ID | Template | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| H009 | [Number]. That's [what it measures]. [Physical comparison]. | Seven trillion dollars. That's how much Sam Altman reportedly wanted to raise for AI chips, according to the Wall Street Journal. It's more than the economy of every country on Earth except America and China. ⚑ |
| H010 | Every [second], [system] can [do X] [N] times. | Visa says its network can handle more than sixty-five thousand transactions every second. |
| H011 | [Small number] people. [Huge valuation]. | In early 2022, FTX had a few hundred employees. Investors valued it at thirty-two billion dollars. |
| H012 | [Entity] told [audience] it would make [big number]. According to [authority], it made [tiny number]. | Theranos told investors it would make more than a hundred million dollars in 2014. According to the SEC, it made about a hundred thousand. |
| H013 | It took [old thing] [long time] to reach [milestone]. It took [new thing] [short time]. | It took Netflix three and a half years to reach a million users. It took ChatGPT five days. |
| H014 | If you [spent X every day] since [distant date], you still wouldn't have [reached number]. | If you'd spent a million dollars every day since the year zero, you still wouldn't have spent a trillion. |
| H015 | About [percent] of [critical thing] comes from [one place]. | About ninety percent of the world's most advanced chips come from one company. ⚑ |
| H016 | [Company] makes [amount] every [minute]. | Apple made about a hundred and seventy-eight thousand dollars of profit every minute of 2024. ⚑ |

## 3. Contradiction and paradox (H017–H024)

Two true facts that can't both be true. The gap is the question.

| ID | Template | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| H017 | [Company] invented [thing]. [Thing] helped kill [company]. | Kodak built the first digital camera, in 1975. Digital cameras helped bankrupt Kodak. |
| H018 | The most valuable [X] company in the world doesn't make [X]. | The most valuable AI company in the world doesn't make AI. It sells the shovels. ⚑ |
| H019 | [Company] lost money for [years]. It was worth [huge]. | Uber lost billions of dollars a year for most of its first decade. It went public valued at about seventy-five billion. |
| H020 | [Loser] had [every advantage] before [winner] existed. It lost anyway. | Microsoft had a smartphone with apps before the iPhone existed. It had the money, the partners and the market. It lost anyway. |
| H021 | [Entity] held [the safest thing]. That's what sank it. | Silicon Valley Bank parked its money in U.S. government bonds, the safest assets on Earth. Those bonds are what sank it. |
| H022 | Almost nobody [does X]. Everyone [is bound by X]. | Almost nobody reads the terms of service. Every one of us has signed them. |
| H023 | [Product] is free. It made [company] [huge sum] last year. | Google Search is free. In 2023 it brought Google about a hundred and seventy-five billion dollars. |
| H024 | In [year], [thinker] noticed [paradox]. It's happening again with [new tech]. | In 1865, an economist noticed something strange. When steam engines got more efficient, Britain burned more coal, not less. The same thing is happening with AI. |

## 4. In medias res (H025–H032)

Start in the middle of the most dramatic real moment, then rewind. Only with a **documented**
scene: never invent dialogue, times or thoughts.

| ID | Template | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| H025 | On [date], [authority] [did drastic thing]. The day before, [cause]. | On the morning of March 10, 2023, regulators shut down Silicon Valley Bank. The day before, customers had tried to pull out forty-two billion dollars. |
| H026 | That [sound] is [machine] [doing X]. [Cost of running it]. | That hum is a cluster of GPUs training an AI model. Training GPT-4 cost more than a hundred million dollars, according to Sam Altman. |
| H027 | On [day], [organisation] published [N] lines. [Consequence]. | On Friday, November 17, 2023, OpenAI's board published a short blog post. Sam Altman, it said, had not been "consistently candid". He was out. |
| H028 | In about [minutes] on [date], [huge value] vanished. Then [twist]. | In about half an hour on the afternoon of May 6, 2010, nearly a trillion dollars of U.S. stock value vanished. Then most of it came back. |
| H029 | [N] minutes into [ordinary process], [entity] had lost [amount]. | Forty-five minutes into the trading day, Knight Capital had lost four hundred and forty million dollars. |
| H030 | By the time anyone at [company] noticed, [intruder] had been inside for [N] days. | By the time anyone at Equifax noticed, hackers had been inside its systems for seventy-six days. |
| H031 | Right now, [system] is deciding [something about the viewer]. | Right now, a model you'll never see is deciding whether your next card payment looks like fraud. |
| H032 | This is [person] in [end scene]. Rewind [N] years. | This is Sam Bankman-Fried in 2023, convicted on all seven counts. Rewind four years, and he was the most celebrated founder in crypto. |

## 5. Stakes (H033–H039)

Say what is at risk, for whom, early.

| ID | Template | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| H033 | If [single point of failure] stopped tomorrow, [what stops for the viewer]. | If one company's factories in Taiwan stopped tomorrow, the newest phones, cars and AI models would all stall. Analysts have a name for that risk. |
| H034 | [Entities] are betting [amount] that [claim]. If they're wrong, [who pays]. | Four tech companies are spending hundreds of billions of dollars a year betting that AI will pay off. If they're wrong, it won't only be their shareholders who pay. ⚑ |
| H035 | [Tiny group] had the power to [huge act]. They used it. | In 2023, a handful of people on a nonprofit board had the power to fire the CEO of the most important AI company on Earth. They used it. |
| H036 | The last time [similar mood], [catastrophe]. | The last time investors were this excited about a new technology, the Nasdaq went on to fall seventy-eight percent. |
| H037 | Only [N] [entity] on Earth can [critical thing]. | Only one company on Earth can build the machines that print the most advanced chips. It's in a small town in the Netherlands. |
| H038 | Whoever controls [A] controls [B]. Right now, that's [who]. | Whoever controls the chips controls AI. Right now, that's a short list, and it's getting shorter. |
| H039 | If you have [common asset], you already own part of this bet. | If you have a pension or an index fund, you already own a piece of this bet. Whether you wanted to or not. |

## 6. Villain (H040–H046)

A face (or a system) to root against. Fact-safety applies to every line here (§4 in
`script-patterns.md`): stick to convictions, admissions, filings and attributed reporting.

| ID | Template | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| H040 | Meet [person]. At [age], [peak]. At [age], [fall]. | Meet Elizabeth Holmes. At thirty, Forbes called her the youngest self-made female billionaire in America. At thirty-eight, she was sentenced to more than eleven years in prison. |
| H041 | [Company]'s own researchers knew. [Quoted internal line]. | Facebook's own researchers knew. One internal slide, reported by the Wall Street Journal, said: "We make body image issues worse for one in three teen girls." |
| H042 | Every [maker] in [market] pays the same toll: [percent], to [gatekeeper]. | Every app maker on the iPhone pays the same toll. Up to thirty percent, to Apple. |
| H043 | In [year], [company] [admitted/pleaded guilty to] [wrong] and paid [amount]. | In 2023, the world's largest crypto exchange pleaded guilty to breaking U.S. anti-money-laundering law. It agreed to pay more than four billion dollars. |
| H044 | The villain of [event] isn't a person. It's a [system/formula]. | The villain of the 2008 crash wasn't one banker. It was a formula, and Wall Street loved it. |
| H045 | On [date], [person] said "[quote]". [N] days later, [contradiction]. | On November 7, 2022, Sam Bankman-Fried posted: "FTX is fine. Assets are fine." Four days later, FTX filed for bankruptcy. |
| H046 | They named it after [ominous reference]. On purpose. | In 2003, a group of Silicon Valley founders named their surveillance company after the seeing stones in The Lord of the Rings. The cursed ones. |

## 7. Countdown and clock (H047–H053)

A ticking structure gives the viewer a reason to stay to the end.

| ID | Template | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| H047 | [Entity] had [time] to [save itself]. This is how [it] spent it. | Silicon Valley Bank had about forty-eight hours between announcing a loss and being shut down. This is how it spent them. |
| H048 | One [small cause]. One [small cause]. And [short time]. | One old piece of code. One server that didn't get the update. And forty-five minutes. |
| H049 | On [future date], [system] will [fail in a strange way]. | On January 19, 2038, millions of older computers will think it's 1901. |
| H050 | Every [interval] that [condition], [thing] quietly [loses/gains]. | Every year your savings earn less than inflation, they quietly buy a little less. You never see a bill for it. |
| H051 | The clock started on [date], when [origin]. | The clock started on January 3, 2009, when someone calling themselves Satoshi Nakamoto mined the first block. |
| H052 | [N] things had to go wrong for [disaster]. This was the first. | Three things had to go wrong for eight and a half million computers to crash on the same morning. This was the first. |
| H053 | [Duration]. That's how long it took [stable thing] to [collapse]. | Under a week. That's how long it took a "stable" coin worth eighteen billion dollars to become almost worthless. ⚑ |

## 8. "Everyone believes X" (misconception) (H054–H061)

State the common belief **as if true**, then break it. Muller's PhD found that videos which
voice the misconception first nearly doubled learning versus a clear explanation alone
([THE Journal](https://thejournal.com/articles/2015/05/05/watching-videos-does-not-necessarily-lead-to-learning.aspx)).

| ID | Template | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| H054 | You've probably heard [belief]. It's not true. | You've probably heard that the internet runs on satellites. Almost all of it runs through cables on the sea floor. |
| H055 | Ask anyone [question] and they'll say [A]. The answer is [B]. | Ask anyone who's making the most money from AI and they'll say OpenAI. So far, the answer is the company selling the chips. ⚑ |
| H056 | For [N] years, [experts] assumed [X]. Then [person] [disproved it]. | For decades, economists assumed people make rational decisions about money. Then two psychologists started running experiments. |
| H057 | The myth: [X]. The reality, according to [authority]: [Y]. | The myth: banks lend out your deposits. The reality, according to the Bank of England: when a bank makes a loan, it creates the deposit. |
| H058 | Everyone says [X]. It's one of the most [opposite] [things] ever built. | Everyone says Bitcoin is anonymous. It's one of the most traceable payment systems ever built. |
| H059 | We were told [X] is [light/clean/free]. It runs on [heavy reality]. | We were told the cloud is weightless. It runs on power plants. |
| H060 | If you think [X], you're in good company. You're also wrong. | If you think the stock market is mostly people buying and selling, you're in good company. Most trades are now placed by machines. |
| H061 | Quick test: [A] or [B]? Most people get it wrong. | Quick test. Which is worth more: Nvidia, or every listed company in Germany combined? Most people say Germany. ⚑ |

| H064 | One [hidden company] can hold [N] facts about you. You've never heard of it. | One data broker has claimed it holds thousands of data points on a single person. You've probably never heard of it. |
| H065 | By the time you finish this video, [thing] will happen [N] times. | By the time you finish this video, millions of card payments will have been approved or refused by software. |

Jump in time to make the present strange.

| ID | Template | Worked example |

| H069 | By [future year], [authority] projects [startling outcome]. | By 2030, the International Energy Agency projects the world's data centres could use about as much electricity as all of Japan does today. ⚑ |

| H071 | On [date], [unknown person] posted [small artefact] to [obscure place]. [Muted reaction]. | On October 31, 2008, someone calling themselves Satoshi Nakamoto posted a nine-page paper to a cryptography mailing list. Most of the replies were sceptical. |
| H072 | If you'd put [amount] into [X] in [year], you'd have [result] today. | If you'd put a thousand dollars into [company] in [year], you'd have [verify] today. (Fill only from a dated price source.) |
| H073 | Imagine explaining [present fact] to [person from past]. | Imagine telling a banker in 1975 that one day, money would be a number on a glass rectangle in your pocket. |

## 11. Absence: the missing thing (H076–H081)

Something that should exist doesn't. The viewer's brain wants to fill the hole.

| ID | Template | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| H076 | [Place] should have [expected thing]. Instead, [surprise]. | Kenya should have needed bank branches on every corner. Instead, millions of people skipped banks entirely and went straight to their phones. |
| H077 | [Amount] of [owner]'s money was missing. | When FTX collapsed, about eight billion dollars of customer money was missing. |
| H078 | [Asset] hasn't moved in [N] years. Nobody knows if [owner] is alive. | An estimated one million bitcoins, believed to have been mined by its creator, haven't moved in more than fifteen years. Nobody knows whether that person is alive. |
| H079 | Why is there no [expected thing]? | Europe has some of the best engineers in the world. So why is there no European Google? |
| H080 | The person who [created world-changing thing] has never been identified. | The person who invented a trillion-dollar currency has never been identified. |
| H081 | Here are [list from past]. Only [N] are still [there]. | Here are the ten biggest companies in the world in 2000. Only one is still in the top ten today. ⚑ |

## 16. Re-hook and transition lines (R01–R50)

`retention-script` needs one re-hook every **30–40 s**. Rotate devices; never the same device
twice in a row. Bracketed slots are filled from the story. Each line should either open a new
loop, raise the stakes, or reverse the last beat (but/therefore, never "and then").

**New question (opens a loop)**
- R01 So where did the other [amount] go?
- R02 Which raises a question nobody at [company] wanted asked.
- R03 But that only works if [condition]. And it didn't.
- R04 So who was on the other end of [the call / the trade / the stone]?
- R05 Why would anyone pay [price] for that?
- R06 The obvious question is [X]. The better question is [Y].
- R07 To answer that, we need to go back to [place/year].
- R08 And that's where the story should have ended.

**Stakes jump (stair-step)**
- R09 That was [small number]. The next one was [bigger].
- R10 One company was a scandal. [N] companies was a system.
- R11 And it wasn't just [first victim]. It was [larger group].
- R12 At this point, the problem was no longer [company]'s. It was [country]'s.
- R13 That's bad. What came next was worse.
- R14 Multiply that by [N], and you get [result].

**Reversal**
- R15 But that's not what happened.
- R16 Except it wasn't a [mistake]. It was the plan.
- R17 It worked. That was the problem.
- R18 On paper, they'd won. On paper.
- R19 Everyone assumed [X]. They were wrong about one thing.
- R20 Then the numbers came in.
- R21 There was just one flaw. Nobody had checked [X].
- R22 And then, for the first time, someone said no.

**Reveal**
- R23 This is the [document/chart/photo]. Look at [line/corner].
- R24 Here's the number they didn't put in the press release.
- R25 The answer was in [place] the whole time.
- R26 This is what [it] actually looked like.
- R27 In their own words: "[quote]."
- R28 It's in the filing. Page [N].

**Countdown and ladder**
- R29 Three things had to go wrong. That was the first.
- R30 That was mistake number two.
- R31 They now had [time] left.
- R32 There are [N] reasons this matters. The first is the one nobody talks about.
- R33 Step one was easy. Step two is where it broke.

**Callback (to the cold-open image)**
- R34 Remember the [cold-open object]? This is where it comes back.
- R35 That's the second time we've seen [object]. It won't be the last.
- R36 Which brings us back to [cold-open place], [time] later.
- R37 Now look at [opening image] again.
- R38 This is why the [object] mattered.

**Zoom in / zoom out**
- R39 Zoom out, and the pattern gets clearer.
- R40 Now zoom all the way in. One [person/server/account].
- R41 From [small scale], this looks like [ordinary]. From [large scale], it's [extraordinary].
- R42 That's one [unit]. There are [N] of them.

**Gear change and time jump**
- R43 [Year]. [Place]. (hard cut, drone drops)
- R44 Meanwhile, [N] miles away, [parallel actor] was [doing X].
- R45 [N] years later, nobody remembered [X]. Except [person].
- R46 Fast forward to [date].

**Direct address (use sparingly: ≤3 per video)**
- R47 If you've ever [common experience], this is why.
- R48 You've almost certainly used [product]. Here's what it was doing.
- R49 Stay with me here, because this is the part that matters.
- R50 Keep that number in mind. We'll need it in a minute.

## 17. Ending buttons (B01–B30)

The **button** is the last 5–20 s after L1 closes. One line of implication, then stop (memo:
"endings must always be abrupt"). No recap, no "so in conclusion", no "thanks for watching"
before the button. One end-screen line may follow the button, after a beat of silence.

**Implication**
- B01 [Company] never answered the question. It just stopped being asked.
- B02 The [system] still works exactly the way it did. That was always the point.
- B03 Nobody went to prison. Most of them are still in the business.
- B04 The next [crisis] won't look like this one. It'll look like something we trust.
- B05 It was never really about [surface topic]. It was about who gets to decide.

**Callback to the cold open**
- B06 [Cold-open object] is still there. [One detail of where].
- B07 Remember the [first number]? Today it's [new number].
- B08 Which is the stone. And which is the one looking back?
- B09 The [object] cost [small price]. Everything after it cost [huge].
- B10 [Cold-open line, repeated], and now you know what that means.

**Irony and reversal**
- B11 The company built to [stop X] became the best at [doing X].
- B12 In the end, the only people who made money were [unexpected group].
- B13 They were right about everything except [the one thing].
- B14 The safest thing they owned is what sank them.
- B15 [Person] got exactly what they asked for.

**Status line (where things stand)**
- B16 As of [month, year], [status]. The case is still open.
- B17 [Person] is now [current situation]. [Company] is worth [number].
- B18 The [asset] hasn't moved since. Somebody is still waiting.
- B19 Today, [N] people still use it every day. Most have no idea.
- B20 [Regulator] has promised to look into it. That was [N] years ago.

**Quote button**
- B21 "[Earlier quote]." [Person] said that in [year]. [Today's contradiction].
- B22 As [person] put it: "[short line]." (end)
- B23 Their own slogan, still on the website: "[slogan]."

**Open question (only one, and only as an implication; L1 must already be closed)**
- B24 The question isn't whether it happens again. It's who notices first.
- B25 Somewhere, someone is building the next one. Probably in a garage. Probably with good intentions.
- B26 And the next time someone tells you [belief], you'll know what to ask.

**Number button**
- B27 [Final number]. That's how much it cost. [Pause.] So far.
- B28 It took [N] years to build and [N] days to lose.
- B29 Every [interval], the number goes up. It went up while you watched this.
- B30 [One digit or word on screen, silence, hard cut to black.]

## 18. Usage rules

- Pull the 3 cold-open candidates from **three different mechanism groups**.
- The re-hook device must differ from the previous re-hook (rotate the groups in §16).
- Re-hooks every 30–40 s; the big re-engagements at ~3:00 and ~6:00 must be a reveal (R23–R28)
  or a stakes jump (R09–R14) backed by a visual we built.
- Never re-use a cold-open template from the last 2 episodes (log it in the outline).
- A button never introduces a new fact that needs a source the viewer hasn't seen.
