drawing plus a clear question can go**.

## 2. Title shapes

- **A why-question about something you've wondered and never looked up:** rain, rivers, mules,
  boys vs girls. Short (about 30 characters), concrete nouns, no adjectives.
- **"Why it sucks to be [animal]":** a funny sympathy angle on biology.
- **A famous paradox settled:** immovable object vs unstoppable force, the grandfather paradox,
  portals from a video game.
- **A myth correction in capitals:** "Screens are NOT the reason…".

## 3. Thumbnail grammar [observed: 9 + 9 looked at]

- **White or paper background with hand-drawn line art.** Black stick figures or simple coloured
  animals with **expressive faces** (a sad hyena with a scar, a worried hamster mother, planets
  with faces).
- **A diagram-question:** a straight river → "?" → a winding river; two stick figures, one walking
  and one running, under a rain cloud with "?".
- **Sometimes one hand-lettered phrase** (WHY CAN'T MULES HAVE BABIES?, EATING BABIES, DODECAGON?!).
- **A pop-culture intruder** for spice: a lightsaber-wielding Darth Vader straightening a river.

**What the transcripts show** (replacing the first pass's recall):
- **The ident comes after the hook** on MinuteEarth (~19–30 s), never before it.
- **The question or paradox lands by ~15–25 s**, and **the obvious answers are knocked down
  aloud** right after (the fish "didn't walk there").
- **MinutePhysics states a count as the contract** ("three good reasons", "mistake number one")
  within 7–24 s.
- **Speech density is very high: 190–240 words per minute of runtime**, against Kurzgesagt's
  159–167 and TED-Ed's 150–167. These are the fastest narrators in the study, which fits their
  "clipped tight" sound.
- The first pass's "two stick figures arguing" device did not appear in these five openings
  [unverified elsewhere].

## 5. Script structure

- **Question → naive answer → mechanism → surprising consequence → kicker.** At the measured 190–240
  words per minute of runtime, a 2.5-minute video is about 475–600 words [data: transcript word
  counts ÷ runtime].
- **Process** [source: *How We Make MinuteEarth Videos*, rosetta.to]: a writer pitches a topic
  they're excited about; research in papers and with researchers; many drafts and editor rounds;
  multiple narration takes cut together; **music added at the audio stage**; then the
  illustrator's rough storyboard (several versions) and only then final drawings. **Up to eight
  videos in production at once**, a remote team across up to eight time zones.

## 6. Animation grammar [observed + recall]

- **Drawn on screen:** the hand or marker draws the diagram as it's explained (classic
  MinutePhysics). The viewer watches the idea being built: *the act of drawing is the animation*.
- **Simple diagrams over illustration:** arrows, curves, timelines, Venn diagrams (*Bell's
  Theorem: The Quantum Venn Diagram Paradox*), branch-trees of timelines (*Time Travel in Fiction
  Rundown*).
- **Characters with faces on anything:** Earth, planets, animals. Emotional cues are drawn
  minimally.
- **White space:** the frame is mostly empty, with one idea on screen at a time.

## 7. Sound

- A fast, slightly breathless narration (Henry Reich on MinutePhysics), clipped tight with
  almost no pauses: **225–240 words per minute of runtime** on the two MinutePhysics videos above,
  the densest in this study [data].
- Light ukulele, piano or pizzicato beds; few sound effects.

## 8. Why it gets shared

- **"Finally, an answer to the thing I wondered as a kid."** Short enough to send without
  apology [inferred].
- **Ending an argument:** walk or run in the rain? [inferred].
- **The audience asks for the next one** [observed]: *ALL THE CATS* opens by saying one comment
  "kept coming up over and over" on the dog video. That's direct evidence that the dog video was
  shared and discussed enough to commission its sequel.

## 9. What we take, and what we must not copy

**Take:**
1. **Draw the diagram on screen as you say it.** In our engine: a stroke reveal on a 2D canvas
   layer over the 3D set.
2. Correct the naive answer.
3. A short concrete title of about 30 characters.
4. The **"ALL THE X, EXPLAINED"** comprehensive sweep as a long-form format.
5. Faces or a minimal expression on non-human objects, used sparingly.

**Don't copy:** the whiteboard stick-figure look, the hand-drawn marker style, or the "Minute"
naming.
