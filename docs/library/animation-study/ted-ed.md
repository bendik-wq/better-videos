# TED-Ed: animation study

TED-Ed makes short animated lessons. Each is written by an educator and animated by a different
independent studio or director, so there is **no house art style**, only a house *format*.
Transcripts here come from ted.com's timed cue data (every cue has a timestamp), so the openings
below are measured.

Labels: **[data]** our yt-dlp pull on 2026-11-05 · **[observed]** from ted.com cues, thumbnails or
credits · **[source]** a named web source · **[recall]** · **[inferred]**.

## 2. Title shapes

1. `Can you solve the [X] riddle?`: a challenge in the second person. Two of the top six are
   riddles, and the riddle series is the channel's franchise.
2. `What would happen if you [stopped doing an everyday thing]?`: the deprivation experiment
   (water, brushing teeth).
3. `What are those [thing you've noticed] …?`: naming something everyone has seen (eye floaters).
4. `How do/does [everyday thing] work?` and `What is [condition]?`: plain, evergreen, searchable.
5. Puns: *A brie(f) history of cheese*.
6. **The educator's name sits after a dash.** It signals a real expert at no cost to the hook.

## 3. Thumbnail grammar [observed: 9 looked at]

- **The question, or most of it, set in big hand-lettered or display type** that matches the
  lesson's art style: CAN YOU SOLVE THE BRIDGE RIDDLE?, WHAT IF YOU STOPPED BRUSHING?, CAN'T FOCUS?
  WATCH THIS, THE BEST WAY TO USE A CREDIT CARD.
- **One character acting out the problem:** the hat prisoners in a size-ordered row under a UFO;
  a clay face with a foaming mouth; a woman under an umbrella in rain (depression).
- **The red TED-Ed logo top right**, every time.
- **The style changes per lesson:** paper cut-out, clay, ink, flat vector, painted hotel facade.
  The brand is the format, not the look.

### b. *Can you solve the bridge riddle?* (24M; 585 words, ~167 wpm)
- 0:09 "Taking that internship in a remote mountain lab might not have been the best idea."
- 0:14 "Pulling that lever with the skull symbol just to see what it did probably wasn't so smart either"
- 0:20 "but now is not the time for regrets because you need to get away from these mutant zombies fast."
- Then the cast (janitor, lab assistant, old professor), the constraint (one rope bridge),
  numbers for each person (1, 2, 5, 10 minutes) and the deadline (17 minutes).

**Device: the viewer is the hero of a B-movie, through their own bad choices.** It is funny,
specific, and hands them a timed puzzle [observed].

### c. *The Infinite Hotel Paradox* (25M; 931 words, ~163 wpm)
- 0:06 "In the 1920's, the German mathematician David Hilbert devised a famous thought experiment"
- 0:12 "to show us just how hard it is to wrap our minds around the concept of infinity."
- 0:18 "Imagine a hotel with an infinite number of rooms and a very hardworking night manager."
- 0:31 "A man walks into the hotel and asks for a room." **0:37 "How?"** 0:39 "Easy…"
- Then the escalation ladder: 1 guest, then any finite number, then an infinitely full bus, then
  infinite buses, then rooms like 1/2, √2 and π. The closing joke at 5:37: "we might need you to
  change rooms at 2 a.m."

**Device: one character (the night manager) carries a formal idea through steps that each get
bigger** [observed].

### d. *What is depression?* (26M; 628 words, ~150 wpm)
- 0:07 "Depression is the leading cause of disability in the world."
- 0:11 About 10% of US adults.
- 0:15 "But because it's a mental illness, it can be a lot harder to understand than, say, high cholesterol."
- 0:21 The confusion named: having depression vs feeling depressed. A list of everyday sadness
  (bad grade, losing a job, rainy day), then **0:46 "Clinical depression is different."**
- It ends on what you can do: don't compare it to feeling down; asking about suicidal thoughts
  reduces risk.

**Device: a stat, a misconception, the correction, then practical help.**

### e. *What would happen if you didn't drink water?* (23M; 637 words)
- It opens wide ("Water is virtually everywhere"), then the body is 55–60% water, babies 75%,
  "swimmingly similar to fish" (a pun). **0:56–1:00: the brain and heart are about ¾ water,
  "roughly equivalent to the amount of moisture in a banana."** A human-unit comparison at the
  one-minute mark.

### f. *The language of lying* (22M)
- It opens with **five spoken lies in quotation marks** ("Sorry, my phone died." "I love you."),
  then "We hear anywhere from 10 to 200 lies a day." A cold open made of the viewer's own life.

### g. Newer openings
- *4 ways to fix your attention span* (4.5M): the narrator **keeps getting interrupted** ("oh, a
  new photo", "did I get that email?") before the study is finished. The form acts out the topic.
- *What if you didn't brush your teeth for a year?* (1.1M): "10 out of 10 dentists agree… But what
  if you just… didn't?", then a day-by-day timeline of decay. It ends on a rhyming rule: "brushing
  for two minutes twice a day will keep the pathobionts at bay."

## 5. Script structure

- **Length:** 585–931 words, 3:50–6:00. That's about 134–167 wpm over the cue span; median about
  150 [observed].
- **Templates:**
  1. **The riddle:** scenario, rules, a "Pause now" countdown, the solution, then a callback joke.
  2. **The thought experiment:** a historical originator, then a character carrying the idea, then
     an escalation ladder, then a meta coda.
  3. **The deprivation timeline:** "what if you stopped X", then hour, day, week and year
     consequences, then the fix.
  4. **The myth-clearing lesson:** a stat, a misconception, the mechanism, then advice.
- **Endings:** every lesson ends on a useful takeaway or a callback joke, never a cliffhanger.
- **Process** [source: ofcom.org.uk TED-Ed submission; Cartoon Brew on Buzzco; a former
  animation director quoted on rosetta.to]: an educator writes; the TED-Ed team workshops the
  script; a studio is chosen and "gets to decide what style and what way to execute the project
  each time"; then storyboard, animatic, final. One studio did 5½ minutes in about 2½ weeks.

## 6. Animation grammar [observed in thumbnails · recall for motion]

- **One art direction per lesson, chosen to fit the content:** puppets or clay for body horror,
  pen-and-ink for history, a geometric hotel facade for infinity. The team says it matches
  technique to topic (e.g. puppet animation for zombies) [source].
- **The single-character conduit:** a night manager, a prisoner, "you" as an intern. The abstract
  idea always happens *to someone*.
- **Visual metaphor as the backbone:** big data drawn as a growing tree [source: *Making a TED-Ed
  Lesson* listing]; a pun on screen for wordplay.
- **Diagrammatic clarity at the key moment:** in the riddle, the line of hats from the back, with
  parity shown as counted hats.
- **Text on screen:** the countdown "Answer in: 3, 2, 1"; labels for terms (commensals,
  pathobionts).

## 6b. Pacing, measured [data: ted.com video files]

**Method.** ted.com hosts TED-Ed lessons as downloadable MP4s (the 1200k "fallback" file linked from
each talk page), so unlike every other channel here we could measure real video. For 6 lessons we
ran ffmpeg scene detection (`select='gt(scene,0.2)'`, scores kept), counted a **hard cut** at a
score ≥ 0.3, merged bursts within 1.0 s (strobes and flashes), and skipped the 6.5 s logo sting.
We also ran `silencedetect` (−40 dB, ≥ 0.6 s) and `ebur128` on the full mix. Script:
`data/scripts/measure.sh`; results: `data/derived/pacing-ted-ed-measured.txt`. Video files were
kept in a scratch folder and not committed.

| Lesson | Runtime | Hard cuts/min | Median shot | Longest held shot | Integrated loudness | LRA | Full-mix near-silences ≥ 0.6 s, per min | Longest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Can you solve the bridge riddle? | 3:34 | 6.9 | 6.0 s | 32 s | −18.5 LUFS | 5.2 LU | 0.6 | **6.8 s** |
| Can you solve the prisoner hat riddle? | 4:19 | 5.0 | 4.7 s | **106 s** | −22.0 | 3.7 | 2.8 | 3.0 s |
| What is depression? | 4:14 | **9.0** | 4.1 s | 35 s | −19.9 | 2.7 | 4.5 | 1.6 s |
| The Infinite Hotel Paradox | 5:46 | 4.8 | 8.6 s | 64 s | −22.3 | 3.1 | 4.8 | 2.2 s |
| What would happen if you didn't drink water? | 4:37 | **2.2** | 9.4 s | **140 s** | −22.1 | 3.1 | 3.7 | 1.6 s |
| The language of lying | 5:26 | 4.9 | 4.4 s | 69 s | −20.9 | 3.6 | 4.6 | 1.8 s |

**Hard cuts undercount what the eye sees.** We checked the first 60 s of two lessons against 1 fps
contact sheets:
- **Bridge riddle:** about **12 distinct compositions in the 54 s after the logo (≈13/min)**,
  against a whole-video detector rate of 6.9/min. The detector also fired 22 times in 6 s on a
  flashing red alarm (16–22 s); the burst merge removes that.
- **Hat riddle:** about **9 compositions in 54 s (≈10/min)** but only 3 hard cuts: most changes
  are a **push-in, a pull-out or a morph inside one set** (the pod → the crowd of prisoners → the
  aliens on the same stage; a white flash into the silhouette line-up).

So TED-Ed's real grammar is **a new composition every 4–6 s, but only half of those are cuts**; the
rest are camera moves and morphs inside a held set. Long held "shots" of 60–140 s are single sets
the camera travels through.

## 7. Sound

- One narrator for most lessons (Addison Anderson on the riddles), calm and dry with comic timing.
- A per-lesson music credit (e.g. WORKPLAYWORK and Cem Misirlioglu on the hat riddle) [observed].
- The first ~6–10 s is music and title before the first line [observed from cue start times:
  6.5–10.7 s].
- **Loudness** [data, table above]: integrated **−18.5 to −22.3 LUFS** (YouTube normalises to about
  −14, so these play back slightly quiet), with a **narrow loudness range of 2.7–5.2 LU**: voice and
  bed are compressed into a tight band. The riddle with the action set (bridge) is the loudest and
  widest.
- **Music in and out** [data]: full-mix near-silences (both voice and music below −40 dB) run
  **0.6–4.8 per minute, almost all 0.6–2 s**: the bed usually drops briefly *with* the voice
  between paragraphs rather than playing under everything. **The one long silence, 6.8 s from 1:58.5
  to 2:05.3 of the bridge riddle, is the pause-and-guess beat**: the caption cues for the pause line
  and the countdown (1:58.8–2:04.4) all fall inside it, so they play as **on-screen text over a
  completely silent mix**, and the voice returns at 2:05.6 with the answer. That's a measured
  instance of "let it breathe".
- SFX density couldn't be separated from music in a stereo mix with ffmpeg alone; not measured.

## 8. Why it gets shared

- **Classroom distribution:** teachers assign it, and lessons have quiz pages. That's evergreen
  traffic outside YouTube's feed [inferred].
- **The challenge share:** "Can you solve…?" is sent to friends as a dare [inferred].
- **The reassurance share:** "What is depression?" gets sent to a person who needs it.

## 9. What we take, and what we must not copy

**Take:**
- The **participatory pause** as a mechanism: stop before the answer, ask the viewer to commit to
  a guess, and **drop the whole mix to silence for 4–7 s** (measured: 6.8 s in the bridge riddle).
  Our version must use our own wording and a set object (for example, a counter wheel freezing),
  **not** their "Pause the video now… Answer in: 3, 2, 1" line or an on-screen numeric countdown.
- Second-person peril in the first sentence.
- One character carrying the abstraction.
- An escalation ladder of cases.
- A callback joke at the end.
- A rhyming or quotable rule as the last line.
- The form acting out the topic (the interrupted narrator).

**Don't copy:** the red logo bug, "Can you solve the… riddle?" as a series name, the educator
by-line format, or the "Pause the video… Answer in: 3, 2, 1" phrasing and countdown card.
